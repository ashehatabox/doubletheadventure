Teaching empathy to preschoolers can sound like a big, abstract job, but it often starts with something very physical: crouching down to a friend's height and looking at what they look at. In this article you'll find what empathy really means for a child of 3 to 7, what researchers have learned about when children start to see the world through someone else's eyes, how a tall young giraffe in our picture book *The View From Down Low* learns to do exactly that, and a set of small, everyday ways to practise it at home.

## What empathy means to a three- to seven-year-old

For grown-ups, empathy is a big word with lots of layers. For a young child, it boils down to one simple idea: to understand a friend, go to where they are and see what they see.

That idea has a few parts, and children grow into each of them at their own pace:

- **Noticing.** Seeing that something is wrong for someone else: a friend has gone quiet, a little brother can't reach the tap, a classmate is always last in the race.
- **Imagining.** Guessing what it's like to be in that person's place. "If I were small, the slide steps would be really high."
- **Caring.** Feeling that their problem matters, even though it isn't yours.
- **Acting, after listening.** Helping in a way that fits what the other person actually needs, not what you assume they need.

Harvard's Making Caring Common project, which studies how children become caring people, describes empathy this way too: it starts with taking another person's perspective, but it also includes valuing other people and feeling compassion for them. In other words, empathy isn't just a thinking trick. It's seeing, and then caring about what you see.

## When do children start seeing through someone else's eyes?

This is one of the most studied questions in child development, and the answer is reassuring: earlier than we used to think, and it keeps growing for years.

### The three mountains

In the 1950s, the Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget designed a famous experiment. Children sat in front of a model of three mountains, each with a different feature on top. A doll was placed somewhere else around the table, and children were asked to pick the photo that showed what the doll could see. Four-year-olds usually picked their own view. By seven or eight, most children could pick the doll's view correctly. Piaget concluded that young children are "egocentric": not selfish, but stuck in their own point of view.

### Grover changes the picture

About twenty years later, the psychologist Helen Borke tried the same idea with a scene that made more sense to small children: a farm with a lake, animals and people, and the Sesame Street character Grover driving around it in a little car. When Grover stopped to look at the scenery, children were asked what he could see. Even three-year-olds did well. The lesson for parents is a good one: when the situation is familiar and the question is clear, young children can often take another view. They just need the right setting, and some practice.

### Feelings come early too

Zero to Three, a non-profit that works on early childhood development, explains that between 18 and 24 months toddlers start to realize that other people can think and feel differently from them. Around the same time, they start to recognize themselves in a mirror. That doesn't mean a three-year-old will be fully empathetic. As Zero to Three puts it, empathy keeps developing throughout life, and small children are naturally focused on themselves. That's not a flaw. It's simply where everyone starts.

So when your four-year-old doesn't notice that their game is leaving the baby out, they're not being unkind. They may genuinely not see it yet. That's exactly where our story begins.

## The story: a giraffe who couldn't see it from up there

*The View From Down Low* is book 14 in our *Double the Adventure* series. It takes place on a golden Southern African plain at the end of the dry season, and its hero is Langa, the tallest young giraffe around.

From up high, everything looks fine to Langa. He gallops to the waterhole each morning with a happy "Clomp, clomp, clomp!" and never notices the cloud of dust he leaves in the face of his best friend, Themba the meerkat. He eats the only seed pods the smaller animals could have reached. When he leads a game, nobody can keep up. He isn't mean. He's kind, just unaware, and that matters: this is not a story about a bully.

When his friends start to droop, Themba gently tells him the problem: he simply can't see it from up there. That night, Langa makes a choice. He does the wobbly giraffe bow, front legs spread wide and long neck lowered all the way down, and asks his friend to show him the small world.

Down at meerkat height, everything changes. The grass is a forest. The dust stings his own eyes. The waterhole bank, which he strides down without thinking, is far too steep for Kulu the tortoise. Langa listens to each small animal before he does anything, and then he helps in ways that fit what they told him. And by the end, the empathy runs both ways: Langa notices something Themba never says out loud, and finds a way to share his own view in return.

The book's theme line says it simply: "When we truly see each other, we all grow taller." You'll find the book here: [The View From Down Low](/books/the-view-from-down-low).

A small note from us: real giraffes do lower their heads this way to drink, and it's an awkward and vulnerable position for them. That's part of why Langa's bow means so much. Getting down to someone's level isn't always comfortable, for giraffes or for people.

## Three things the story shows about empathy

### Empathy often starts with not noticing

Langa's first problem isn't a lack of caring. It's a lack of seeing. Many children (and plenty of adults) are in the same place. The story gives you a kind, blame-free way to talk about it: "Langa didn't know. Once he knew, he wanted to understand." That framing helps children hear about the effects of their actions without feeling attacked.

### Listen before you fix

When Langa gets down low, he doesn't rush around solving things the way a tall giraffe would. He asks, and listens, and only then helps. This is a valuable lesson for children who love to "help" by taking over. Real help starts with finding out what the other person needs.

### It runs both ways

We were careful to avoid a story where the big one rescues the little ones. Themba has something to teach Langa, and Langa has something to share with Themba. Empathy isn't about the strong being kind to the weak. It's about friends seeing each other, whatever their size.

## How to practise empathy at home

Here are habits that work with real three- to seven-year-olds, many of them drawn from Zero to Three and Making Caring Common.

### Get down low, literally

This is the most direct way to bring the book into your week. With your child, crawl around a room at toddler or pet height. What looks different? What's hard to reach? What's scary from down here? Then let your child stand on a safe, steady step stool (with you holding their hand) and look at the room from "giraffe height." Talk about how the same room looks different depending on where you stand.

### Name feelings, theirs and others'

Children need words before they can recognize feelings in someone else. When your child is upset, name it and stay close: "You look frustrated. That tower kept falling." Later, point out how other people feel: "Look at Maya's face. I think she's sad her turn ended."

### Point out the effect, then suggest an action

When your child's actions affect someone, describe the effect calmly and offer a way to help. "When you ran past, the sand went in Leo's eyes. Can you get him a cloth?" This mirrors Langa's discovery: the effect first, then a caring action.

### Ask before helping

Teach the habit Langa learns: before fixing a friend's problem, ask. "Do you want help, or do you want to try?" It's a small question, and it carries a big idea: other people know what they need.

### Use books and pretend play

Zero to Three suggests picture books about feelings and pretend play with toys to explore how characters feel. After reading, act out a scene with stuffed animals: one tall, one small. Let your child play both parts.

### Look after the feelings that block empathy

Making Caring Common points out that strong emotions such as anger or shame can get in the way of empathy. A child who is overwhelmed can't easily think about someone else. Practise calming down together first: stop, take a slow breath, count to five. Then come back to the friend.

### Model it, and stretch the circle

Children watch how we treat others, including the people we don't know well. Let them see you crouch to talk to a small child, slow down for an older neighbour, or listen patiently to someone who is struggling. Making Caring Common calls this widening the "circle of concern": helping children care about people beyond family and close friends.

## Share it with your child

A few things you can say or do right after reading:

- **Ask the big question.** "Why didn't Langa notice that his friends were unhappy?" Help your child see that he wasn't unkind, he just couldn't see it from up there.
- **Feel the bow.** "How do you think it felt for Langa to bend all the way down?" Try the giraffe bow together, legs wide, slowly lowering your head. It's wobbly!
- **Spot the clues.** "How did Langa know Themba wanted something, even though Themba didn't say it?" Talk about faces, bodies and quiet moments as clues to feelings.
- **Make it personal.** "When could you bend down to see how a friend feels?" Maybe it's a younger cousin, a new child at school or a grandparent who walks slowly.
- **Join the refrain.** Say "Clomp, clomp, clomp!" loudly at the start of the book and softly at the end. Ask your child why it changes.

## Where the value comes from

Every book in our series starts from one value, and this one draws on Ubuntu, from Southern Africa, often summed up in the isiZulu saying *Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*, "a person is a person through other people." The everyday isiZulu greeting *Sawubona* means "we see you." The heroes' names come from the same languages: Langa means "sun" and Themba means "hope." The story itself is a simple, secular tale of animals learning a life lesson, and uses no Zulu words.

## Questions parents ask

### At what age do children develop empathy?

Empathy grows over many years. According to Zero to Three, toddlers between 18 and 24 months start to understand that other people can think and feel differently from them. In Helen Borke's version of Piaget's three mountains task, even three-year-olds could describe what a character saw from another spot. Empathy keeps developing well into school age and beyond.

### My child doesn't seem to notice when others are upset. Is that normal?

Yes, very often. Young children are naturally focused on themselves, and noticing others is a skill they learn with practice. Naming feelings, pointing out other people's faces and reading stories like this one all help. If you have real worries about your child's social development, talk to their doctor or educator.

### What is the difference between empathy and kindness?

Empathy is understanding and caring about what someone else feels and sees. Kindness is what you do about it. In the book, Langa needs empathy first (getting down low to understand) before his kindness can actually help.

### Should I make my child say sorry?

Zero to Three suggests that with toddlers, focusing on the other person (how they feel and what might help) is often more useful than insisting on "I'm sorry," which young children may not fully understand yet. As children get older, a sincere apology becomes more meaningful.

### Is The View From Down Low right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The idea of tall and small is easy to grasp, the refrain invites joining in, and the problems Langa notices are concrete: dust, steep banks, high branches. Older children (5 to 7) enjoy thinking about the quieter moments, such as how Langa notices what Themba doesn't say.
