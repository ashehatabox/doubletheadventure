Teaching kids to share their feelings is rarely about getting them to talk more. It is about becoming the kind of listener a small person feels safe telling things to. Most of us have asked "How was your day?" and heard "Fine." Most of us have also watched a quiet child carry something around for days before it came out sideways, in a meltdown at bedtime or a sudden "I don't want to go." In this article we look at why young children keep feelings inside, what a safe listener actually does, how our picture book *The Pebbles Inside* shows the whole idea through a little turtle named Tavi, and practical things to try at home or in a classroom, with a few sentences you can share with your child.

## The value at the heart of *The Pebbles Inside*

*The Pebbles Inside* is the twelfth book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and its value is sharing our feelings with the friends who listen. The theme line says it in two short sentences: "Feelings kept inside grow heavy. Feelings we share become a path." You can find the book here: [The Pebbles Inside](/books/the-pebbles-inside).

For a child of three to seven, we wanted that to be something they could picture. A feeling you don't tell anyone about is like a pebble you tuck into your shell. One pebble is nothing. But if you keep tucking, the shell gets heavy, and you can't climb the hill or play your favourite game. The good news in the story is that a pebble gets lighter the moment someone you trust holds it with you.

Two things matter in that picture, and we want to keep both in view. The first is the child who shares. The second, which is easy to forget, is the friend who listens. The book spends as much time on Fern, the hare who knows how to sit beside someone, as it does on Tavi.

## Why do young children say "I'm fine"?

Tavi is a quiet turtle with a big heart. When the frogs tease her for being slow, she says she's fine and tucks the hurt away. The same thing happens with a worry about thunder, with a "no, thank you" she wanted to say, with a game idea, and even with the words "you're my best friend."

The last few are what make the book useful for parents. It would be easy to think that children only hide the bad stuff. Tavi hides the happy and hopeful things too, because they all feel risky. When we read the story with children, the same reasons come up again and again:

- **Fear of being laughed at.** The frogs' teasing teaches Tavi that saying how she feels can get her laughed at. The little frog later admits he laughed because he was scared of being laughed at himself.
- **Fear of being wrong.** What if my worry is silly? What if my idea is a bad one?
- **Fear of hearing "no."** Sharing a want, or saying "no, thank you," feels like a risk.
- **Not having the words.** Young children often feel something big and can't yet say what it is.
- **Wanting to keep the peace.** "I'm fine" is quick, and it stops the conversation.

None of this means a child is difficult. It usually means a child is careful. Young children are still learning what is safe to say and who is safe to say it to. Our job is to make the answer to both questions as clear as we can.

## What research says about being heard

You don't need a study to know that feeling listened to matters, but a few trustworthy organisations have put it into words.

The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University describes [serve and return](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/key-concept/serve-and-return/): a young child reaches out with a babble, a gesture, a cry or a question, and a caring adult responds, like a game of tennis. The Center explains that these back-and-forth exchanges help build the brain's connections for communication and social skills, and support early emotional health. It also notes that naming what a child sees, does or feels helps build language connections even before the child can talk.

ZERO TO THREE, a national non-profit for early childhood, makes a similar point in its [tips for promoting social-emotional development](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/tips-for-promoting-social-emotional-development/). It suggests that putting feelings into words in the middle of a conflict helps: its example is a teacher telling a child, "I know you like the dinosaur book and you are sad," while still explaining that grabbing isn't okay. It also says that helping children name their feelings, and letting them see and practise ways to control impulses, is how they learn to handle conflicts on their own over time. And it adds that when children feel responded to and understood, they develop confidence and good self-esteem.

The Gottman Institute, which teaches "emotion coaching," lists five steps in a [short article for parents](https://www.gottman.com/blog/strengthen-childs-emotional-intelligence/): notice emotions, see them as a chance to connect, listen and validate, help the child label the feeling, and problem-solve within limits ("all feelings are okay; not all behaviours are"). The same article makes a comforting point: parents in the research followed all five steps only about a fifth to a quarter of the time. In other words, nobody does this perfectly, and you don't have to.

## What Fern does: five things a safe listener does

The most useful moment in the book is the one where Fern doesn't say much. Tavi has been getting slower and heavier, and Fern, who is usually the one who blurts out whatever she feels, sits down beside her and asks gently. Tavi says she's fine. Fern doesn't push, and she doesn't leave. She simply stays.

Here is what we think Fern is doing, in the order she does it.

1. **She notices.** She sees that her friend has stopped climbing and stopped giggling.
2. **She asks, once, gently.** There's no interrogation and no list of questions.
3. **She accepts "I'm fine" without a fight.** She doesn't argue or say "No you're not."
4. **She stays.** Her presence says what words can't: you're not in trouble, and I'm not going anywhere.
5. **She thanks Tavi when the first hurt comes out.** She listens without laughing, and tells her she understands.

Notice what's missing: advice, fixing, and a quick "don't be silly." The book also avoids forcing Tavi to talk. That matters, because pressing a child to open up often makes the shell close tighter. A calm, unhurried grown-up who is simply available is often what makes it possible for a child to start.

## Teaching kids to share their feelings at home

Here are practical things to try. Pick one or two. They work best when they become small habits rather than special occasions.

### Ask better questions

The Child Mind Institute points out that [vague questions invite "I'm fine"](https://childmind.org/article/how-to-ask-what-kids-are-feeling-during-stressful-times/). It suggests specific, open questions like "What was the most fun thing you did today?" or "What was the toughest part of your day?" It also suggests choosing a calm, neutral moment, such as a meal or a walk, rather than bedtime or right after an argument.

### Go first

The same article suggests modelling the conversation by sharing something of your own. "I felt a bit nervous when I had to speak at the meeting, and then it went fine" shows a child what sharing sounds like, and that grown-ups have pebbles too. Keep it small and light; a child shouldn't have to carry yours.

### Try a daily "rose, thorn and bud"

Also from the Child Mind Institute: each person at the table shares a petal (something they liked), a thorn (something they didn't like) and a bud (something they're looking forward to). Go first so your child can see how it's done. Because it's a ritual, nobody is put on the spot, and the happy things get shared as well as the hard ones, just like in Tavi's story.

### Give feelings names

You can offer words without putting them into a child's mouth: "That looked like it made you feel left out. Is that close?" Let your child correct you. Naming a feeling is a first step to managing it, and a wide vocabulary ("disappointed," "nervous," "proud," "embarrassed") gives children more ways to say what's inside.

### Respond to the feeling first, the behaviour second

When a child shares something, thank them before you do anything else. "Thank you for telling me" is a powerful sentence. If a feeling comes with a behaviour that needs a limit, name the feeling and hold the limit, as in the ZERO TO THREE example above.

### Don't laugh, don't fix, don't rush

If a child's pebble seems small to you, it isn't small to them. A response like "Oh, that's nothing!" teaches the shell to close. A response like "That sounds like it really bothered you" teaches it to open.

### Let them choose how to share

Some children talk more easily side by side, in the car, or while drawing. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that [pretend play](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Emotional-Development-in-Preschoolers.aspx) lets preschoolers explore feelings from love to anger to fear. Joining in, with your child in charge, can be a way for a pebble to come out sideways before it comes out straight.

## Share it with your child

These are short, plain sentences you can read aloud or say in your own words. Use the ones that fit.

- "A feeling can feel like a pebble. If we keep it inside, it can get heavy."
- "You don't have to tell me right now. I'll be right here when you want to."
- "Thank you for telling me. I'm glad you did."
- "Happy feelings and big ideas count too, not just hard ones."
- "Everybody has pebbles, even grown-ups. We can share them."
- "Who is a safe person you could tell? Let's name three."

It helps to make that last one concrete. Together, think of two or three trusted people: a grown-up at home, a teacher, a grandparent, a good friend. Children who know who their listeners are find it easier to reach for them.

## Using the book with a child

After reading *The Pebbles Inside*, try these questions. There are no right answers; follow your child's lead.

1. Why did Tavi say "I'm fine" when she wasn't?
2. What happened to Tavi's shell as she kept more feelings inside?
3. What did Fern do that helped Tavi feel safe to talk?
4. Why do you think the frog laughed at Tavi? What did he do later?
5. Who could you tell when you have a "pebble" inside? What kind of pebble would you like to share today?

For a three-year-old, you might only ask the third question and point to Fern in the pictures. For a six- or seven-year-old, the question about the frog opens a good conversation about why people sometimes laugh when they're scared.

One more thing: the pebbles are only a picture. Real turtles don't carry stones in their shells. If you do an activity with real pebbles, use large stones and stay close, because small stones are a choking hazard for the youngest children. Paper pebbles work just as well. You'll find more ideas in our other reading on trust, such as [teaching trust to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-trust-to-preschoolers-finn-and-coral), which looks at what it takes for a child to rely on someone.

## When to ask for more help

Everyone has quiet days, and some children are naturally quieter than others. That is not a problem to fix. But if your child seems sad, withdrawn, or worried for a long time, or if a big feeling keeps getting in the way of sleep, school or friendships, talk to your child's doctor or educator. They can look at the whole picture in a way an article can't.

## Questions parents ask

### How do I get my child to talk about their feelings?

Usually, you don't "get" them to. You make it safe and easy. Ask specific questions at calm moments, share a little of your own day first, and accept "I'm fine" without argument. Stay close and stay curious. Children often open up later, on their own terms, once they've seen that a feeling shared is met with kindness.

### Is it a problem if my child is quiet about their feelings?

Not by itself. Temperament varies, and many thoughtful children take time to find words. What matters is that they know someone will listen when they're ready. If a child seems unhappy or withdrawn for a long time, or you're worried about their development, speak to their doctor or educator.

### What should I say when my child says "I'm fine" but clearly isn't?

Try, "Okay. I'm here if you want to talk." Then stay nearby and do something side by side. It echoes what Fern does in the story. You can come back to it later with a specific question, such as "What was the toughest part of today?"

### At what age can children start sharing feelings?

Even toddlers can start, with a few simple words and a caring adult who names what they see. By preschool, many children can say they feel sad, mad or scared, and by early school age they can talk about why. The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard notes that naming what a child feels builds language connections even before they can talk, so it is never too early to start.
