Teaching honesty to preschoolers can feel like a puzzle. One day your three-year-old tells you, with chocolate all over their face, that they didn't touch the cookies. The next day they announce that a dragon broke the lamp. Are they lying? Should you worry? And what actually helps a child choose the truth when the truth feels scary? In this article we look at what honesty means for a three- to seven-year-old, why early fibs are a normal part of growing up, what researchers have learned about what makes children more (and less) likely to tell the truth, how our picture book *The Honest Little Raccoon* shows a child-sized moment of honesty, and simple ways to grow truth-telling at home.

## What honesty means to a three- to seven-year-old

For grown-ups, honesty is a big, abstract word. For young children it shows up in very small moments:

- a child spills juice and says "I did it" instead of pointing at the dog;
- a sibling asks "Did you take my crayon?" and the answer is "Yes, sorry, here it is";
- a child admits they didn't really brush their teeth yet.

The idea at the heart of *The Honest Little Raccoon* is one we tried to put in words a small child can hold: telling the truth can feel scary, but it makes your heart feel light and helps people trust you.

That sentence has two parts, and both matter. The first part admits something we sometimes forget: the truth *is* hard to tell when you've done something wrong. The second part names the reward children can actually feel, which is not a sticker or a prize, but the relief of not carrying a secret, and the warmth of being trusted.

### Honesty is more than not lying

It helps to think of honesty for young children as three small skills rather than one big rule:

1. **Saying what happened**, even when it was my fault.
2. **Saying sorry** when what happened hurt someone or broke something.
3. **Helping to put it right**, if I can.

A child who learns all three learns what to *do* after a mistake, not just what to avoid.

## Why young children tell fibs

Almost every parent of a small child meets the first lie sooner or later, and it can be a surprise. The good news is that it's usually a sign of a growing mind, not a sign of a "bad" child.

### Lying is a thinking skill

To tell a lie, a child has to understand that other people have their own thoughts, and that those thoughts can be wrong. Psychologists call this understanding "theory of mind." In a study published in *Psychological Science* in 2015, Kang Lee of the University of Toronto and colleagues worked with three-year-olds who had always told the truth in a simple hide-and-seek game. Children who were then given a few short sessions practising how to think about what other people believe started to lie more in the game than children who did other kinds of puzzles. In the researchers' words, telling a lie successfully requires deliberately creating a false belief in someone else's mind.

So when a three-year-old tries their first fib, they're showing that they have started to understand that you can't see inside their head. We can respond calmly: nothing strange is happening for their age.

### Fantasy, wishes and fear

The American Academy of Pediatrics, on its HealthyChildren.org site, notes that children younger than about six often can't fully separate reality from fantasy. The dragon who broke the lamp may be part story, part wish that it hadn't happened. After about six, children understand the difference more clearly.

The same AAP article says that, in loving homes, children's first lies usually come after they have done something they know is wrong, out of fear of disappointing their parents or being punished. The AAP also points out something reassuring: a child who lies about a mistake knows it *was* a mistake, which means their conscience is working.

## What research tells us helps children tell the truth

Here is where the science gets useful for everyday parenting, because some of our most natural reactions turn out not to help.

### Punishment doesn't make children more honest

At McGill University in Montreal, Victoria Talwar and her colleagues studied 372 children aged four to eight. Each child was left alone in a room for a minute with a toy behind them and told not to peek. A hidden camera showed that about two in three children peeked, and of those, about two in three then said they hadn't.

Before being asked, children heard different messages. Children who expected to be punished were *less* likely to tell the truth than children who were told that the adult would be happy if they told the truth, or that telling the truth was the right thing to do. Younger children responded most to pleasing the grown-up; older children responded more to the idea of doing what's right. As Talwar put it, "punishment does not promote truth-telling." The study was published in the *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* in 2015.

From the child's side, this makes sense: if the truth leads to something frightening, hiding it is the safest choice.

### Stories that praise honesty work better than stories that warn

Kang Lee's team also tested something every parent does: telling children stories about honesty. Children aged three to seven heard one of several classic tales, then had a tempting chance to cheat in a game and were asked whether they had. Two of the stories, *Pinocchio* and Aesop's *The Boy Who Cried Wolf*, show bad things happening to characters who lie. Those stories didn't make children any more likely to own up. A story in which a child is praised for admitting a mistake did increase confessions. When the researchers changed that story's ending so that the child was punished instead, the effect disappeared. The study appeared in *Psychological Science* in 2014.

The lesson for parents is simple: children seem to learn more from seeing honesty *rewarded with warmth* than from seeing lying punished.

### Children notice our own fibs

Most of us tell little untruths to get through the day: "The playground is closed today," "If you don't come now, I'll leave without you." A study from Nanyang Technological University in Singapore, published in the *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* in 2019 with Kang Lee as a co-author, asked 379 young adults to remember how often their parents had lied to them as children. Those who remembered more lies reported lying to their parents more as adults. The study is based on memories and can't prove cause and effect, but the lead author, Peipei Setoh, suggested alternatives worth trying: acknowledge your child's feelings, tell them what to expect, offer choices, and solve problems together.

## How The Honest Little Raccoon shows honesty

*The Honest Little Raccoon* is the seventh book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and its value is truth. You can find it here: [The Honest Little Raccoon](/books/the-honest-little-raccoon).

Riley is a clever, curious young raccoon who explores the alleys and rooftops of a busy city at night. On his wanderings he meets Maggie, a calm old pigeon who seems to have seen everything, and she tells him something he doesn't fully understand yet. It becomes the book's refrain: "Truth will always set you free."

Riley's best friend is Benny, a big, strong raccoon who always looks out for him. One chilly night the smell of fresh bread leads the two friends somewhere they shouldn't be: through the open window of a bakery. That part of the story is a mistake, not an adventure, and it's worth saying so to your child. When Riley's tail sends a tray crashing to the floor and the baker comes running, Riley has a choice to make.

### A real moment of choice

What makes this moment hard is that Riley is offered an easy way out, and it comes from someone who loves him. Benny offers to take the blame so Riley can slip away. We'll let your child discover what Riley does, but it's no secret, given the title, that he remembers Maggie's words.

### Owning up is more than one word

When honesty comes in the story, it isn't only "it was me." It includes saying sorry and explaining. That's the three-part honesty we described earlier, shown in a few pages: what happened, sorry, and making it right.

### A grown-up who responds with kindness

We were careful with the baker. He is startled and cross at first, as any real grown-up would be, but he responds to honesty with kindness, not shame. This matches what the research above suggests: children learn the most from stories where telling the truth is met with warmth. The book's theme line, spoken by Riley, sums up what he learns: "Lying only traps you in more lies. But the truth? It set me free."

One note for real life: in the story, the baker is kind to the raccoons, but real raccoons are wild animals. Children should never approach, touch or feed them.

## Teaching honesty to preschoolers: ideas for home

Small habits make the truth feel safe. Pick one or two that fit your family.

### Don't set up a lie

If you saw your child draw on the wall, asking "Did you draw on the wall?" invites a fib. Try stating what you know instead: "I see crayon on the wall. Let's clean it together." This removes the temptation and keeps the focus on fixing things.

### Make the truth the safest choice

When your child does own up, notice it first, before anything else. "Thank you for telling me. That was brave." Then deal with the mistake calmly. There can still be a consequence that fits, like helping to wipe up the juice, but the honesty itself should never be the thing that gets your child in trouble.

### Stay calm when you hear a fib

If your child tells you something you know isn't true, you can say so gently: "Hmm, I don't think that's what happened. Do you want to try again?" Give them a moment and a second chance. Many children will take it.

### Tell honesty stories, the warm kind

Choose stories, from books or from your own childhood, where someone told the truth and was met with warmth.

### Model honesty, including when you get it wrong

When you make a mistake, say so out loud: "I told you we'd go to the park, and then I forgot. That's my fault. I'm sorry." Try to swap the little parenting fibs for the truth with a choice: instead of "The shop has no more treats," try "We're not buying treats today. Would you like to choose the fruit for lunch?"

If owning up and saying sorry often go together in your house, our article on [teaching forgiveness to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-forgiveness-to-preschoolers-the-fox-mistake) shows how to help children give and accept a real apology.

## Share it with your child

Things to say or do after reading *The Honest Little Raccoon* together:

- **Ask about Maggie.** "What did Maggie tell Riley about the truth?" Let your child try to say the refrain with you: "Truth will always set you free."
- **Talk about Benny.** "Why do you think Benny offered to take the blame?" There's no wrong answer. Some children will say "because he loves Riley," which is a lovely thing to notice.
- **Name the feelings.** "How do you think Riley felt just before he told the truth? How did he feel afterwards?" Help your child find words like scared, wobbly, brave, light.
- **Talk about the baker.** "What did the baker do when Riley was honest?" Point out that telling the truth helped the baker trust him.
- **Share a story.** "Can you remember a time when telling the truth was hard?" Go first, to show it's safe.
- **Play "Light or heavy?"** Hold a small cushion. Pretend to keep a secret and hug it tight ("Heavy!"), then "tell the truth" and toss it gently away ("Light!"). It's a playful way to feel what Riley felt.
- **Use the theme line.** After a real moment of honesty at home, say it together: "Lying only traps you in more lies. But the truth? It set me free."

## Questions parents ask

### Is it normal for a three-year-old to lie?

Yes. Researchers at the University of Toronto have linked early lying to children's growing understanding that other people have their own thoughts. It's a normal step in development. Respond calmly, give your child a chance to try again, and praise the truth when it comes.

### Should I punish my child for lying?

Research from McGill University found that the threat of punishment made children less likely to tell the truth, while hearing that the truth would make the grown-up happy, or that it was the right thing to do, made them more likely. You can still have a fair consequence for the mistake itself, but try not to punish the honesty.

### What should I do when I know my child isn't telling the truth?

Say gently what you know, without a trap: "I think you did eat the cookie. It's okay to tell me." Give them a second chance, thank them if they take it, and then focus on what to do next.

### Is it okay to tell my child small white lies?

Many parents do, and one small fib won't undo your teaching. But a Singapore study found that adults who remembered being lied to often as children lied to their parents more as adults. Where you can, tell the truth with a choice or a plan instead. If you're worried that your child lies often or in ways that concern you, talk to your child's doctor or educator.

### Is The Honest Little Raccoon right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The night-time city, the crash in the bakery and the friendship between Riley and Benny are easy to follow. Younger children enjoy the story and the refrain; five- to seven-year-olds can talk about the harder part: why the easy way out was tempting, and why Riley didn't take it.
