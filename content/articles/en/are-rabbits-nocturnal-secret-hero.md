Are rabbits nocturnal? It's one of those questions children ask at bedtime, and it turns out to have a lovely answer. In our picture book *Secret Hero*, a small, shy rabbit called Bunny does all his helping while the forest sleeps. Real wild rabbits really are night and twilight animals, and the other creatures in the story (a mother bird, a squirrel and a monkey) have true-life stories just as interesting. This article gives you the real facts behind each character, the places where the story playfully bends nature, and simple ways to share it all with a 3 to 7 year old, including what to do if your child ever finds a fallen nest.

## Which rabbit is Bunny?

The book never names Bunny's species, so we had to pick. We chose the **eastern cottontail** (*Sylvilagus floridanus*), the wild rabbit most North American children are likely to see on a lawn, a park edge or a bike path. Cottontails have the widest range of any rabbit in their group: from southern Manitoba and Quebec all the way down through the United States and Central America into northwestern South America.

Here in Ontario, they are a newer neighbour than you might think. Ottawa naturalists note that cottontails were once absent from southern Ontario and spread north as forests were cleared; in the Ottawa area, records only go back to after 1931. Today they live wherever they find their favourite kind of place: the edges where bushes and trees meet open grass. Think meadows, hedgerows, orchards, farm fields and the shrubby borders of parks.

A cottontail is easy to describe to a child: brown-grey fur, long ears, big dark eyes and a short, fluffy white tail that flashes like a cotton ball when it runs. That tail is where the name comes from.

### Rabbit or hare?

Children often mix up rabbits and hares, and Canada has both. One easy difference to share: the snowshoe hare turns white in winter, but the cottontail stays brown all year, which makes it very easy to spot against the snow. Another difference is in the nest. Cottontail babies are born blind and without fur, tiny enough to weigh about as much as a handful of grapes (25 to 35 grams), and they open their eyes after four or five days.

## Are rabbits nocturnal? Yes, mostly at dusk and at night

Here's the answer to the big question. Biologists describe eastern cottontails as **crepuscular and nocturnal**. *Crepuscular* is a wonderful word for children to learn: it means "busy in the twilight," at dawn and dusk. Cottontails do most of their eating in two bursts: in the two or three hours around sunrise and in the hour after sunset. During the day, they rest, crouched low under a log, in a thicket or under a brush pile.

So when Bunny slips out at night to fix things, he's doing what a real rabbit would do: moving when it's dim and quiet, when fewer eyes can see him. Night-time is safer for a small animal that so many others would like to eat. Hawks, owls, foxes, coyotes and weasels all hunt rabbits.

There's one twist that children enjoy. In cold weather, cottontails are often more active in daylight. Ottawa naturalists who watch them in winter see them warming up in sunny, sheltered spots. So if your family spots a rabbit on a bright winter afternoon, it isn't "wrong": it's just catching some sun.

### How a shy rabbit stays safe

Bunny's shyness is very true to life. A cottontail's first defence is to **freeze**: it stays completely still and trusts its colours to hide it. If that doesn't work, it **flushes**, bursting away in a fast zigzag to the nearest cover, at up to about 29 km/h (18 miles per hour). It can also **slink**, creeping low to the ground with its ears laid flat. Its sharp eyes, nose and ears warn it early, which is why you usually see a rabbit for only a second before it's gone.

That's a nice link to the story. Bunny is not a show-off, and real rabbits aren't either. Staying unseen is how they survive. In the book, staying unseen is a choice Bunny makes because he wants the help to be about the other animals, not about him.

## What do rabbits eat in winter?

This is where real rabbits and the snowy part of the story meet. In summer, cottontails eat green plants: grasses (about half of what they eat), clover, wild strawberries and, to many gardeners' dismay, vegetables. When snow covers the ground, the menu changes completely. They switch to the woody parts of shrubs and trees: **twigs, buds and bark** of plants like oak, dogwood, sumac, maple and birch. As the snow gets deeper, they can stand on it and reach higher up the stems.

Rabbits also have a surprising habit that children find either hilarious or gross. Because plant food is hard to digest, a rabbit eats some of its own soft droppings to get the goodness out on a second pass. It sounds strange, but it's how its body gets enough nutrition from twigs and grass.

In winter you can often find signs of rabbits even when you can't see them: well-worn little trails in the snow between bushes and feeding spots, nibbled stems cut off at a neat angle, and a shallow dent in the snow where a rabbit sat, which naturalists call a "form."

### Baby cottontails grow up fast

Cottontails start having babies in late winter and keep going through the summer, often several litters a year with about five babies in each. The mother makes a shallow nest in the ground, lined with grass and her own fur, hidden under a shrub or in tall grass. The babies leave the nest after only about two weeks and can look after themselves at four to five weeks. That speed matters, because many young rabbits don't survive their first year.

## Mama Bird and the missing egg

Mama Bird's species isn't named either. We picked the **American robin**, because it's the forest-edge and backyard bird most Canadian children will know, and because its nest fits the story so well.

The mother robin chooses the spot and builds the nest herself. She shapes dead grass and twigs into a cup from the inside out, then strengthens it with soft mud and lines it with fine dry grass. Nests usually sit a few metres up, on a branch in the lower half of a tree, tucked under the leaves. Some robins build on gutters, ledges or light fixtures instead.

Inside go three to five eggs of a famous colour: sky blue or blue-green, with no spots. The mother sits on them for about 12 to 14 days, keeping them warm, and the chicks leave the nest about two weeks after hatching. The father helps feed them. Robins often raise two broods a summer in southern Canada, and they eat lots of earthworms in spring and summer, plus berries and fruit.

A real robin's nest is quite sturdy thanks to that mud, but strong wind and storms do knock nests down, just as in the book. That makes this a good moment to talk about what a child should do in real life (see below).

## The squirrel who notices

In the story, a squirrel is the only one who catches Bunny in the act. We picked the **American red squirrel**, a small, chatty squirrel of spruce and pine forests.

Red squirrels are some of winter's busiest workers. In autumn, each one gathers thousands of spruce and pine cones and stores them in one central spot called a **midden**, a pile of cones and discarded cone scales that can build up over many years. The seeds inside are their main food, which lets them stay active all winter long instead of hibernating. Each squirrel defends its own stash, and you often hear one before you see it: a loud, scolding chatter that means "this spot is mine."

That's why the snowy-path scene in the book makes sense. When snow buries everything, animals that depend on reaching their food have a real problem, and a clear path really would help.

There's also a sweet "story versus real life" point here. Real red squirrels are solitary and territorial, not the sort to make friends with a rabbit. A squirrel who notices a good deed, asks about it gently and keeps the secret is the story's own invention, and a good one to talk about.

## The monkey in the snowy forest

The monkey who later starts clearing water paths looks like a chimpanzee in the illustrations, and that gives you two fun facts to share.

First, **a chimpanzee isn't a monkey at all**. Chimps are great apes, in the same family group as gorillas, orangutans, bonobos and humans. The easiest way to tell for a child: monkeys usually have tails, and chimps have none. Chimpanzees share about 98.6 percent of their DNA with us.

Second, wild chimps live in Africa, in forests and woodlands from West Africa to Uganda and Tanzania, not in snowy northern forests. So the book's monkey is a story visitor. Chimps are clever tool users: they poke sticks into termite mounds to fish out insects, crack nuts and even soak up water with leaves, which makes a chimp who helps with water a rather fitting idea. They are also in trouble. WWF estimates there are only 150,000 to 250,000 left in the wild, and they've disappeared from several countries because of habitat loss and hunting.

If your child objects that monkeys never live in snow, you can tell them about one real exception: the **Japanese macaque**, sometimes called the snow monkey. Some live in the north of Japan's main island, where more than a metre of snow can cover the ground for months. They are the most northern wild primates in the world, apart from people.

## Share it with your child

Here are some easy ways to bring these facts into your reading time.

- **Teach the twilight word.** After reading, whisper "crepuscular" together and explain it means "busy at dawn and dusk." On your next evening walk, look and listen for animals that come out as the light fades.
- **Play freeze-and-zigzag.** In a safe open space, take turns being the rabbit. When the grown-up calls "hawk!", the rabbit freezes. On "fox!", the rabbit zigzags to a "bush" (a cushion or a tree). It's a fun way to show how real rabbits stay safe.
- **Go on a winter sign hunt.** After a fresh snowfall, look for rabbit tracks, little trails between bushes, and twigs nibbled off at an angle. Take photos instead of touching anything.
- **Talk about "story or real?"** Ask: "Could a real rabbit fix a pipe? Does a real chimp live in the snow? Would a real squirrel keep a rabbit's secret?" Children love catching the playful parts, and it builds early thinking skills.
- **Spot the robin's egg blue.** Find something blue-green around the house and talk about the colour named after robin eggs.
- **Make a midden basket.** In autumn, collect fallen pine or spruce cones together and pile them in a basket, like a red squirrel getting ready for winter. (Keep small cone pieces away from children under 3.)

### If your child finds a fallen nest or a baby bird

This one matters, because a child who loves *Secret Hero* may want to rescue an egg the way Bunny does. Teach a simple rule: **look, don't touch, and tell a grown-up.** The Toronto Wildlife Centre advises that a healthy nestling can usually go back into its nest, and a fallen nest can be placed back as close as possible to where it was. Then everyone watches quietly from a distance to see if the parents return. If the parents don't come back, or the bird looks hurt, a grown-up should call a local wildlife rehabilitation centre for advice. They also point out that touching a baby bird does not make its parents abandon it. That's a myth. The grown-up does the handling; the child's job is to notice and to tell.

Like Bunny, children can be quiet helpers with animals in daylight and with a grown-up: filling a bird bath, leaving a patch of the garden wild, or picking up litter in the park.

## Questions parents ask

### Are rabbits nocturnal or diurnal?

Wild cottontail rabbits are mostly crepuscular and nocturnal: they're busiest at dawn, dusk and night, and rest hidden during the day. In cold winter weather they're often out in daylight too, warming up in sunny, sheltered places.

### What do wild rabbits eat in winter?

When snow covers the grass, cottontails eat twigs, buds and bark from shrubs and young trees, such as dogwood, sumac, maple and birch. In summer they go back to grasses, clover and other green plants.

### What should my child do if we see a wild rabbit?

Watch quietly from a distance and enjoy it. Don't chase or try to pick it up. A rabbit that sits frozen is trying to hide, not asking to be petted. If you find a nest of baby rabbits, leave it alone and keep pets away; ask a local wildlife centre if you're worried.

### Is the monkey in Secret Hero a real kind of monkey?

It looks like a chimpanzee, which is actually a great ape, not a monkey. Wild chimps live in African forests and woodlands, not in snowy forests. The only primate (other than people) that lives in deep snow is the Japanese macaque.

### Is it okay to touch a baby bird to put it back in the nest?

Wildlife centres say touching a baby bird won't make its parents reject it. Still, the job belongs to a grown-up, not a child. If the bird seems hurt or the parents don't return, call a local wildlife rehabilitation centre.
