Teaching anger management to preschoolers can feel like trying to teach swimming in the middle of a storm. The moment your child most needs a calming tool is exactly the moment they can't hear a word you say. The good news is that the work mostly happens before and after the storm, on calm days, in small steps. In this article we look at what managing anger really means for a three- to seven-year-old, why it's so hard at this age (and why that's normal), how our picture book *The Desert Buzz* shows the difference between feeling angry and acting on it, and practical, everyday ways to help your child pause, breathe and put things right.

## What managing anger means for a three- to seven-year-old

Let's start with what it doesn't mean. Managing anger is not the same as never being angry. Anger is a normal, useful feeling. The NHS describes it as "a normal and useful emotion" that can tell a child something is unfair or wrong. A child whose tower gets knocked down, whose turn gets skipped or whose drawing gets torn has every reason to feel cross.

What we're teaching is what to *do* with that feeling. For a child, we put the idea at the heart of *The Desert Buzz* like this: it's okay to feel angry, but when anger gets big, stop, take a deep breath and listen to your heart before you act.

That gives us three small skills a young child can actually learn:

- **Notice it.** "I'm getting angry." Children can learn to spot the signs in their own bodies.
- **Pause it.** A breath, a count, a step back: anything that puts a little space between the feeling and the action.
- **Repair it.** If angry words or hands already did some damage, there's a way back: saying sorry and making it right.

None of these says "don't feel it." That matters. When children hear "Stop being angry" or "Calm down right now," they often learn that the feeling itself is bad, and that the safest thing is to hide it. Hidden anger doesn't go away; it tends to come out later, sideways.

### Anger often covers another feeling

Researchers at Utah State University Extension point out that anger is often a "secondary" emotion: it sits on top of something softer, like hurt, fear or disappointment, especially when a child feels their needs or feelings haven't been noticed. A child who shouts "I hate this game!" may really be feeling "I'm scared I'll lose." Keeping that in mind helps us respond to the feeling underneath, not just the noise on top.

## Why anger is so hard to manage at this age

If your four-year-old can explain perfectly well, on a calm afternoon, that hitting is wrong, and then hits their brother an hour later, you're not failing and neither are they.

### The brain's "air traffic control" is still being built

The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University describes the skills we use to manage ourselves (holding information in mind, focusing, and self-control) as a kind of air traffic control system for the brain. These skills, called executive function, begin to develop shortly after birth. The Center describes ages three to five as "a window of opportunity for dramatic growth" in them, and notes that they keep developing through adolescence and into early adulthood.

In other words, the ages our books are written for are exactly the years when self-control is growing fastest, and it's still far from finished. A preschooler isn't choosing to be out of control; they're still building the equipment.

### Feelings arrive in the body first

The American Academy of Pediatrics notes on HealthyChildren.org that anger is intense but usually short-lived, and that children can feel it in their bodies: a racing heart, a hot face, trouble breathing or thinking clearly, a rush of energy. The NHS lists similar signs: clenched teeth and fists, tense muscles, a churning stomach.

This is useful for parents. Young children may not be able to say "I'm angry," but they can learn to notice "my fists are tight" or "my face feels hot." The body is often the earliest warning sign, and the easiest one for a child to spot.

### Words help

There's also interesting research on naming feelings. In a 2007 study published in *Psychological Science*, UCLA psychologist Matthew Lieberman and colleagues found that when adults put a feeling into words (labelling a face as "angry", for example), activity in the amygdala, a part of the brain linked to alarm, went down. The study was done with adults, not children, so we shouldn't stretch it too far. But it fits with everyday experience and with advice from children's health services: helping a child find words for a feeling seems to help them handle it.

## How The Desert Buzz shows managing anger

*The Desert Buzz* is the sixth book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and its value is managing anger. We wanted a story where anger is understandable, where it causes real trouble, and where the way back is simple enough for a small child to try.

Etoo is a tiny beetle and Bella is a busy bee, and they are best friends in a hot, sandy desert. They climb cactus plants, share nectar and rest in the shade of cool rocks. One scorching afternoon, Etoo is building a sand tower he's very proud of. Bella zooms past on her way to gather nectar, and the breeze from her wings knocks it flat.

You can probably guess what happens next, because it happens in most homes. Etoo explodes. He says hurtful things, the kind of "you always" words children (and grown-ups) say when they're angry. Bella tells him it was an accident, but he won't listen. He stomps off, and she flies away hurt. You can find the book here: [The Desert Buzz](/books/the-desert-buzz).

### The feeling is fair; the outburst isn't

We were careful about one thing. Etoo's tower really was knocked down, and he has every right to be upset. The book never says "Etoo shouldn't have been angry." It also never says the accident was Bella's fault. The story separates the feeling, which makes sense, from what Etoo does with it, which hurts his friend. That's exactly the line we hope children learn to see.

### The cost of an outburst

What changes Etoo's mind isn't a telling-off. It's a feeling. Alone under a rock, he notices how empty the desert feels without his friend, and that staying angry isn't making anything better. Young children understand loneliness very well, and this is a gentle, honest way of showing that outbursts have a cost, without shame.

### A simple calming tool

Then Etoo does something any child can copy: he takes a slow, deep breath. He remembers the advice of a wise old scorpion, which is also the book's theme line: "When anger buzzes loud, pause and listen to your heart." A breath is the whole tool. It doesn't need equipment, a special corner or a grown-up's permission.

### Repair, from both sides

We'll leave the details of the ending for your child to discover, but it's no secret that the two friends make up, and that both of them say sorry. We like that. Real repair is often two-sided, and children learn a lot from seeing that an apology is a way back into friendship, not a punishment. If you'd like to go further into this part, our article on [teaching forgiveness to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-forgiveness-to-preschoolers-the-fox-mistake) looks closely at how young children learn to say sorry and forgive.

## Teaching anger management to preschoolers: ideas for home

The calm moments are where the learning happens. Here are ideas that work in ordinary family life; pick the ones that suit your child.

### Practise the breath when nobody is angry

A deep breath is hard to remember in the middle of a meltdown if you've never practised it. Make it a game on calm days. Pretend to smell a flower and then blow out a candle. Breathe in like a bee filling up with nectar and breathe out with a long, slow "bzzzzz." The AAP and the NHS both list slow, deep breathing among the calming strategies children can use, along with counting to ten and walking away.

### Make a "pause" signal

Agree on one word or gesture that means "let's stop for a second": a hand on the heart, the word "pause", or a buzz. When you notice anger building, use it calmly: "I see tight fists. Pause?" Utah State University Extension suggests teaching children to say "stop" to themselves as a first step. The goal isn't to stop the feeling but to stop the next action.

### Name it to help tame it

Help your child find words: "You're angry because your tower fell down. That's really frustrating." For younger children, the NHS suggests creative approaches such as drawing the anger or giving it a name. Some children like to picture anger as a buzzing bee or a hot sun; once it has a shape, it's easier to talk about.

### Give the body a job

Anger comes with energy, and it often helps to spend it safely. Ideas from Utah State University Extension include jumping, running, dancing or squeezing play dough. Some families keep a "cool-down basket" with a soft ball to squeeze, a picture book and some crayons. Let your child help choose what goes in it; the same Extension guide notes that involving children in choosing their coping strategies tends to work better.

### Wait until calm before talking it through

During a big outburst, keep everyone safe and say very little. Long explanations don't land when a child is flooded. Once things settle, you can talk: what happened, what the angry feeling was like, what might help next time, and whether anyone needs a sorry. The AAP's "ACT CALM" steps follow the same order: acknowledge the feeling, calm down, then think and talk it through.

### Show them how you do it

Children learn most of what they know about anger by watching the grown-ups around them. The AAP reminds parents that "you are your child's most important role model." When you're frustrated, say it out loud: "I'm feeling really cross that the milk spilled. I'm going to take a big breath before I clean up." And when you lose your temper (every parent does), repair it: "I'm sorry I shouted. I was angry, but shouting wasn't okay."

## Share it with your child

Some things to say or do after reading *The Desert Buzz* together:

- **Ask about the tower.** "Why did Etoo get so angry?" Let your child say it was unfair. It was.
- **Talk about fairness.** "Was it fair when Etoo said Bella always ruins everything?" This opens a gentle talk about how angry words can be bigger than what really happened.
- **Imagine Bella's feelings.** "How do you think Bella felt when Etoo stomped away?" Help your child find words like sad, surprised or hurt.
- **Find the turning point.** "What helped Etoo calm down?" Point to the page where he takes a deep breath.
- **Practise together.** "Shall we try a slow breath like Etoo?" Breathe in for a count of three and out with a long buzz.
- **Make it personal.** "What helps you when you feel a big angry feeling?" Write or draw your child's answers and keep them somewhere you can both see.
- **Use the theme line.** When anger starts to rise at home, say it softly together: "When anger buzzes loud, pause and listen to your heart."

## Questions parents ask

### Is it normal for a four-year-old to have angry outbursts?

Yes. Anger is a normal emotion, and the skills young children need to control how they act on it are still growing fast between three and five, according to Harvard's Center on the Developing Child. Outbursts usually become less frequent as those skills grow and as children learn words and calming tools. Expect progress to be bumpy.

### Should I let my child be angry, or tell them to calm down?

Let the feeling be, and set limits on the behaviour. You might say, "You're allowed to be angry. You're not allowed to hit. Let's breathe together." This teaches that anger is acceptable but hurting people or things isn't.

### What can my child do instead of hitting or shouting?

Offer clear alternatives you've practised on calm days: a slow breath, counting to ten, walking away to a calm spot, squeezing a soft ball, stamping feet on the floor or telling a grown-up "I'm angry." The more often a child practises these when calm, the more likely they are to reach for one when upset.

### When should I worry about my child's anger?

If outbursts are very frequent or intense, if your child often hurts themselves or others, if anger is affecting friendships or nursery and school life, or if you're simply worried, talk to your child's doctor or educator. They can help you understand what's going on and whether more support would help.

### Is The Desert Buzz right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The story is short and clear, the knocked-down tower is a frustration every small child knows, and the deep breath is easy to copy. Three-year-olds enjoy the friends and the buzzing; five- to seven-year-olds can talk about fairness, hurtful words and how both friends made up.
