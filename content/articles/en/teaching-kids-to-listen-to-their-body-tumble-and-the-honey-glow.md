Teaching kids to listen to their body sounds like something that should come naturally. Yet most parents know the scene: a sniffly, glassy-eyed four-year-old who insists they are *not* tired, *not* sick, and absolutely must go to the party. Young children often feel that stopping means missing out, or even losing. In this article we look at what listening to your body means for a three- to seven-year-old, why it is a skill they learn rather than one they're born with, how our upcoming picture book *Tumble and the Honey Glow* shows it through a bouncy bear cub, and simple, everyday ways to practise it at home, including a few safe, cozy rituals for the days when your child is unwell.

## What listening to your body means for a young child

For a grown-up, "listening to your body" covers a lot: eating when we're hungry, sleeping when we're tired, slowing down when we're sore or sick. For a small child, it starts with one simple question: *What does my body need right now?*

That question shows up in ordinary moments:

- noticing a tummy rumble and saying "I'm hungry" instead of getting cranky;
- noticing heavy eyes and asking for a quiet story instead of another game;
- noticing a scratchy throat and telling a grown-up;
- noticing they're too hot in a coat and taking it off;
- noticing they need the toilet *before* it becomes an emergency.

We call this value *self-care*. For young children it isn't about bubble baths or treats. It's an attitude: *My body tells me things, and taking care of it is a strong thing to do.* Our book's theme line puts it in words a child can carry: "The strongest bears are gentle with themselves."

## Why young children find it hard to stop

### Body signals are a skill children are still learning

There's a name for the sense that tells us what is happening inside our bodies: interoception. The Student Wellbeing Hub, a resource from Education Services Australia, describes it as the perception, recognition and understanding of the internal physical states of the body, and notes that it is thought to be a prerequisite for self-regulation. In plain words: before a child can calm down, rest or ask for help, they first need to notice what their body is telling them.

Young children are still building that noticing. A three-year-old may feel "bad" without knowing whether the bad is hunger, tiredness, a sore ear or a big feeling. That's normal. The words and the noticing grow with practice, and grown-ups help a lot by naming what they see: "Your eyes look sleepy. I wonder if your body wants a rest."

### Stopping can feel like losing

The second reason is about feelings, not bodies. Play is a child's whole world. To a five-year-old, resting can feel like being left out, and admitting you feel unwell can feel like being weak. Some children have also picked up the idea that being tough means pushing through, from older siblings, cartoons or our own grown-up habits of carrying on no matter what.

So when a child refuses to rest, it often isn't stubbornness. It's a mix of not quite reading their body yet, and worrying about what they'll miss.

## How Tumble and the Honey Glow shows the value

*Tumble and the Honey Glow* is coming soon to our *Double the Adventure* series. You can find out more on the book's page: [Tumble and the Honey Glow](/books/tumble-and-the-honey-glow).

Tumble is a bear cub who rolls and tumbles everywhere he goes, and the whole forest is getting ready for the Last Leaf Games. Then, one golden autumn day, a tickly cough starts in his throat. Tumble decides it must be a "Tickle Monster" that he has to fight, so he roars, races the wind and climbs the tallest rock. By afternoon he is tired and sniffly. His friend Zuzu, a small and cheerful honeybee, gently wonders whether all that racing is really helping.

The Tickle Monster is never a real creature, and never scary. It's simply Tumble's idea of what's happening, the kind of story a young child tells themselves to make sense of a strange feeling. That makes it a lovely thing to talk about: "Was there really a monster? What was really going on in Tumble's body?"

### The question that turns the story

The heart of the book is Mama Bear. She doesn't scold Tumble, rescue him or tell him what to do. She asks him what his body needs right now, a question he has never asked himself. And then she lets him answer.

We think this is the most useful thing in the book for parents. When we only give orders ("Get into bed!"), a child learns to obey, which is fine for today. When we ask the question and help them answer it, they start to learn to listen for themselves, which is what they'll need when we're not there.

In the cozy den, Tumble listens and chooses what helps: warm soup, berries and a soft blanket. Zuzu brings honey from the hive, and Mama stirs it into warm milk. The book's refrain, "Warm, slow, honey-glow", captures that feeling of slowing down and being cared for. Children love to say it softly, and it can become a family phrase for "time to rest".

### Rest is not the end of the fun

When the games begin outside, Tumble stays in to rest, and he coughs into his elbow to keep his friends well. The book is careful about a few things. Fighting the cough doesn't make Tumble better; resting does. His body does the healing work, and he helps it by resting. Honey is a comfort, not a cure. And resting doesn't mean he loses everything: without giving the ending away, he still gets a chance to play, and he carries his new kindness with him.

## Teaching kids to listen to their body: ideas for home

Pick one or two to begin with. Small, repeated moments do more than a big talk.

### Ask the question, every day

Make "What does your body need right now?" an everyday question, not just a sick-day one. Ask it after school, before dinner, when your child is wild and wound up, or when they seem flat. Accept any honest answer: "a snack", "a hug", "to run around", "quiet". The goal is the habit of checking in.

### Name what you notice, kindly

Young children borrow our words before they have their own. Try "Your cheeks look hot. Is your body too warm?" or "You keep rubbing your eyes. I think your body is telling us something." Say it as a wondering, not a verdict, so your child gets to check for themselves.

### Do a body check-in together

Lie down side by side and take a slow "tour" from toes to head. "How do your toes feel? Your tummy? Your throat? Your eyes?" Keep it short and playful. Over time, children get better at noticing small signals before they turn into big meltdowns.

### Model your own listening

Children copy what we do far more than what we say. Say it out loud when you take care of yourself: "I'm tired, so I'm going to sit down for a few minutes." "My head hurts, so I'm having some water and a quiet break." If you always push through, your child learns that's what strong people do.

### Separate rest from punishment

If "go lie down" is only ever used as a consequence, rest will feel like a punishment. Give rest its own warm rituals instead: a special blanket, a soft song, a favourite quiet book, the "Warm, slow, honey-glow" words. Rest can feel safe and good.

### Talk about strength in a new way

When your child does choose to rest, notice it: "You listened to your body. That was a strong choice." "You stayed in to get better. That was brave." Over time, these words help change the story from "resting is weak" to "resting is something strong bears do".

## When your child is unwell: small comforts and a safety note

Colds are part of childhood. The American Academy of Pediatrics' HealthyChildren.org notes that most children have around eight to ten colds in their first two years alone, and that colds usually get better slowly over about 7 to 10 days. AboutKidsHealth, from The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids) in Toronto, adds that a cough can hang on for up to three weeks.

We're not doctors, and this isn't medical advice. These are simply comfort ideas that fit the book:

- **A cozy den.** A blanket on the sofa, a pillow, a stuffed animal and a few picture books. Let your child help build it.
- **Warm, slow drinks.** Warm (never hot) milk, water or soup, sipped slowly with a grown-up.
- **Quiet play.** Puzzles, stickers, audiobooks, drawing. Resting doesn't have to mean lying still and bored.
- **Coughing into the elbow.** The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says to cover your mouth and nose with a tissue when you cough or sneeze, or use your elbow if you don't have one, and to wash your hands afterwards. It's a simple way children can care for others, just like Tumble.

**About honey:** never give honey to a baby under 1 year old. AboutKidsHealth is clear on this: do not give honey to babies under 12 months. For older children, honey can be a soothing comfort, but it isn't a cure; rest and time do the healing. Leave it out for any child with an allergy you've been told about.

**When to call the doctor:** if a cough lasts, if breathing is hard, or if you're worried at all, talk to your child's doctor. Trust your instincts; you know your child best.

## Share it with your child

Here are some things to say and do after reading *Tumble and the Honey Glow* together:

- **Ask about the monster.** "Why did Tumble think he had to fight the Tickle Monster? Was there really a monster?"
- **Feel the difference.** "What happened when Tumble raced and climbed? How do you think his body felt?"
- **Ask Mama Bear's question.** "What would your body say if you asked it right now?" Let them answer, even if it's "jump!"
- **Practise the elbow.** Play a game of pretend sneezes and coughs into the elbow. Children often find this very funny, and it sticks.
- **Say the refrain.** Whisper "Warm, slow, honey-glow" together as you snuggle under a blanket. Use it on real rest days, too.
- **Talk about bravery.** "Was resting brave? Why do you think so?"
- **Learn from real animals.** Real bears eat a huge amount in autumn: Yellowstone Forever, the nonprofit partner of Yellowstone National Park, says grizzlies can take in up to 20,000 calories a day before winter, then rest in their dens for months without eating. Real honeybees work hard too: the University of Florida's IFAS Extension says a single bee makes only about 1/12 of a teaspoon of honey in its whole life, and the University of Maryland's entomology department explains that in winter the bees huddle in a ball-shaped cluster and eat the honey they stored. Ask: "Even the strongest animals rest and store up energy. Why do you think they do that?"
- **Draw what you need.** Fold a paper in four. In each square, draw something your body might need: food, water, sleep, a hug.

## Questions parents ask

### At what age can children learn to listen to their body?

Toddlers can already tell us some things, like "hungry" or "owie", with lots of help. Between about 3 and 7, children get much better at noticing and naming what they feel inside, because the skills behind self-regulation are growing fast. Expect lots of help at three, and more independent check-ins ("I need a break") by six or seven.

### My child refuses to rest when they're sick. What can I do?

Try asking rather than telling: "What does your body need right now?" Make rest cozy and interesting with a blanket den, quiet play and a special story, so it doesn't feel like a punishment. Remind them, kindly, that they won't miss everything forever. If you're worried about how unwell your child is, call their doctor.

### Is it okay to give my child honey for a cough?

Never give honey to a baby under 1 year old. For older children, honey can be soothing, but it doesn't cure a cough; rest and time help most. If a cough lasts, breathing seems hard, or you're unsure, talk to your child's doctor.

### How do I help my child tell the difference between "I'm tired" and "I'm upset"?

Name what you see in a gentle, wondering way ("Your eyes look sleepy, and you're feeling grumpy too"), and do body check-ins together on calm days. Children get better at sorting body signals from feelings with practice and with words from the grown-ups around them.

### Is Tumble and the Honey Glow right for a three-year-old?

Yes. Little ones enjoy the bouncy bear cub, the tiny bee and the soft "Warm, slow, honey-glow" refrain. Older children, around 5 to 7, are ready to talk about the Tickle Monster idea, why fighting a cough didn't help, and what makes resting brave.
