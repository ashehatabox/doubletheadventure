How do acorns grow into oak trees? It's a question that tends to come up on an autumn walk, with a pocket full of acorns and a child looking up at a very big tree. In our picture book *Becoming a Tree*, a tiny oak seed called Oakie waits in the dark soil, gets advice from a kind worm, loses a leaf to a hungry deer and grows into a tree full of nests. Every one of those moments has a true story behind it. This article walks you through the real life of an oak, from acorn to giant, and the real earthworms, birds and deer that share its world, with simple ways to share it all with a 3 to 7 year old.

## Which oak is Oakie?

The book never says which kind of oak Oakie is, so we had to choose. Canada has 12 native oak species, according to *The Canadian Encyclopedia*, out of about 500 around the world. Most of ours grow in Ontario and eastward. The one a child in southern Ontario, Quebec or the Maritimes is most likely to stand under is the **northern red oak** (*Quercus rubra*), so that's the one we follow here, with a few side trips to its cousin, the **white oak** (*Quercus alba*).

Canadian oaks come in two families, and children can learn to tell them apart by the leaves:

- **Red oaks** (northern red, black, pin and others) have leaves with pointy tips on their lobes, like little bristles.
- **White oaks** (white, bur, swamp white and others) have leaves with rounded lobes, smooth like fingertips.

The northern red oak gets its name from its deep red autumn leaves and its pinkish-red wood. It usually grows to about 25 metres tall and lives around 150 years, and some white and bur oaks can reach about 200. In child terms: an oak that sprouts today could still be growing when your child's great-grandchildren are small. That's a lovely way into the book's refrain, "Good things take time."

## How do acorns grow into oak trees?

This is the heart of the story, and real life is just as patient as the book.

### Step 1: the acorn ripens

An acorn is the oak's seed, snug in a little scaly cup. Here's a fact that surprises most grown-ups: a red oak acorn takes **two years** to ripen on the tree. White oak acorns ripen in just one. So before Oakie ever reached the soil, he had already done a lot of waiting.

### Step 2: the long wait in the ground

What happens next depends on the family.

- **White oak acorns** are in a hurry. They are sweet, so squirrels, deer, chipmunks, blue jays and wild turkeys gobble them up fast. To beat the crowd, they sprout almost as soon as they land in autumn and push out a root before they can be eaten. A scientist at Harvard's Arnold Arboretum dug up a young mountain chestnut oak (a white oak cousin) in late autumn and found the root system already well grown, though the shoot would not appear until spring.
- **Red oak acorns** wait. They are bitter, full of natural chemicals called tannins, so animals often skip them in autumn. Rain and snow slowly wash some of the bitterness out over winter, and the acorn sprouts the following spring.

Either way, a lot of the oak's story happens underground, in the dark, where no one can see. That's exactly where *Becoming a Tree* begins.

### Step 3: root first, then shoot

When the acorn sprouts, the first thing out is the **root**, which grows down. Only then does a shoot push up toward the light. Botanists call this kind of sprouting "hypogeal," which means the two fat halves of the seed stay below ground and feed the young plant while it gets going. The book shows the same order: a root reaching down and then a green shoot breaking through. It's one of the places where the story follows nature closely.

### Step 4: years of growing

A young oak grows a little each year. In a forest, a northern red oak starts making its own acorns at about 25 years old and doesn't make lots of them until it's about 50, according to the US Forest Service's guide to North American trees. Some years are "mast years," when oaks produce a huge crop all at once, usually every two to five years. No one can predict exactly when.

### The most patient number in the forest

Here's a fact to share with older children. The same Forest Service guide says that even in a good year, only about 1 percent of red oak acorns get the chance to become trees, and it can take **500 or more acorns to produce one seedling** that survives its first year. Insects, squirrels, mice, deer and birds eat the rest.

That makes Oakie a very lucky acorn, and every big oak the one acorn in hundreds that made it.

## The worm: a real underground helper (with a twist)

The kind worm who tells Oakie to be patient is the book's mentor. Real earthworms are fascinating, and they have a surprising Canadian story.

### What earthworms do

Earthworms make tunnels as they move through the soil. Those tunnels loosen hard ground and let air and water reach plant roots. As worms eat bits of dead leaves and other plant matter, it passes through their bodies and comes out as **castings** (worm poop, which children always enjoy hearing). Castings release nutrients that plants can use and help soil clump into the crumbly texture gardeners love.

Children love these worm facts, all from the gardening education group KidsGardening:

- Earthworms have **no lungs**. They breathe through their skin.
- They have **no eyes**, but they can sense light with a light-sensitive patch, which helps them stay out of drying sunshine.

So a worm living quietly in the dark soil, right beside a seed, is a very fitting neighbour for Oakie.

### The twist: Ontario's worms are newcomers

Here's something most families don't know. According to the Royal Botanical Gardens in Ontario, **none of the 19 kinds of earthworms found in Ontario are native** to the province. During the last ice age, glaciers covered the land and wiped out the worms that lived here. The forests that grew back over thousands of years did so without them. The worms we see today arrived from Europe and Asia with people, in ships and in farm goods.

In gardens and compost, these worms are helpful. In some northern forests, though, they can cause trouble. Native forest floors depend on a thick, slow-rotting layer of fallen leaves. Large numbers of worms eat that layer quickly, and they eat small seeds too, which can mean fewer native plants and fewer tree seedlings.

This is a gentle "story or real life?" conversation for older children: the same creature can be a helper in a garden and a problem in a wild forest. Worms aren't villains, just animals in a new place. There's one easy thing families can do: the Royal Botanical Gardens suggests that if you have leftover fishing worms, you put them in your garden or compost, where worms already live, rather than dumping them in the woods.

## The birds: nests, and the jays who plant oaks

In the story, a passing bird tells Oakie about sunlight and wind, and later birds nest in his branches. Real oaks are favourite homes and feeding places for many birds, and one bird in particular has a special job.

### Blue jays, the oak planters

Blue jays love acorns. They fill their beaks, fly off and bury the acorns one by one in the ground to eat later. An Illinois Extension naturalist notes a lovely bit of jay behaviour: if another jay is watching, a jay will keep flying until no one can see where it hides its treasure.

Jays don't dig up every acorn they bury. The forgotten ones are in a perfect spot to sprout: planted at the right depth, away from the parent tree. One study described by Illinois Extension found that 50 blue jays moved and buried about 150,000 acorns in 28 days, roughly 110 acorns a day for each bird, and that acorns buried by jays sprouted more often than those carried off by other animals.

So when your child finds a young oak growing somewhere odd, like a garden bed, a jay or a squirrel may well have planted it.

## The deer: a hungry visitor, not a bad guy

The deer who bites off one of Oakie's leaves is the story's big setback. It helps to explain to children that the deer isn't being mean. It's just having lunch.

### What white-tailed deer eat

Hinterland Who's Who, the wildlife guide from the Canadian Wildlife Federation and Environment and Climate Change Canada, describes its menu: in spring and summer, leaves from woody plants, grasses and other green plants, plus treats like fiddleheads, mushrooms and blueberries. In autumn and winter, when green plants fade, deer depend mostly on the **twigs and buds** they can reach. And in eastern Canada, **acorns are a favourite autumn food**.

So a deer nibbling a young oak is perfectly natural. Researchers at Penn State have filmed a deer stripping the leaves off an oak seedling. Seedlings that are nibbled again and again grow up shorter and stubbier, which is why foresters and gardeners sometimes protect young trees with fences or tubes. Still, the same study found that young oaks were shaped more by light, soil and their size before a gap opened in the forest than by deer. A nibbled seedling isn't necessarily a lost one.

### Fun deer facts for children

- The white-tailed deer is named for its tail. When it's alarmed, it lifts its tail like a flag and shows the bright white underside as it bounds away.
- Fawns are born in late spring, covered in white spots and wobbly on their legs at first.
- In deep snow, deer gather in sheltered areas called **deer yards**, where they can find food and shelter from storms and deep snow.

Seen through nature's eyes, the oak and the deer belong to the same forest: the oak feeds the deer and shades it.

## Share it with your child

Here are some easy ways to bring the real nature of *Becoming a Tree* into your days.

- **Go on an acorn hunt.** In autumn, gather a few acorns and look closely: the cap, the smooth shell, maybe a tiny root poking out. Count how many you find under one tree and talk about the 500-acorns-for-one-tree fact. Acorns are not a snack (they're bitter and hard), and they're a choking hazard, so keep them away from children under 3. Some parks don't allow collecting, so check the rules first.
- **Pointy or round?** Collect a few fallen oak leaves and sort them into "pointy tips" (red oak family) and "round fingers" (white oak family).
- **Play buried treasure.** Like a blue jay, your child hides a few "acorns" (pine cones or balls) around the garden or living room, then tries to find them all the next day. Did any get "forgotten"? Those are the ones that would grow into trees.
- **Be the seed.** Your child curls up small on the floor as an acorn. You narrate: the cold winter, the rain, the warm spring sun. First a foot slides out (the root), then slowly the arms and head rise up (the shoot), until they're standing tall with arms out like branches.
- **Visit the worms.** After a rain, look for earthworms on a path or in the garden. Watch them, help any stranded ones back onto the soil, and wash hands afterwards. Talk about why they like it damp: they breathe through their skin.
- **Find your oak.** Pick one oak near home and visit it every season. Take a photo each time. It's a small family project in patience, and the photos make a lovely book of their own.

## Questions parents ask

### How long does it take an acorn to grow into an oak tree?

The sprouting part takes months: many white oak acorns push out a root in autumn and a shoot in spring, while northern red oak acorns usually sprout the spring after they fall. Growing into a big tree takes decades. A northern red oak in a forest starts making acorns at about 25 years old and makes lots of them from about 50.

### Can we grow an oak tree from an acorn at home?

Many families do. Choose a fresh, uncracked acorn from the ground under a local oak (check your park's rules about collecting), and ask a local garden centre, conservation authority or tree-planting group for advice about your area. Remember that most acorns never become trees, so plant a few and treat any sprout as a celebration.

### Why do deer eat young trees?

Because twigs, buds and leaves are a big part of their natural diet, especially in autumn and winter when green plants are scarce. Deer also love acorns. It's not wrongdoing, just feeding. Foresters protect some young trees with fences or tree tubes when there are a lot of deer.

### Do squirrels and jays really plant oak trees?

Yes. Blue jays and squirrels bury acorns to eat later, and the ones they don't come back for can sprout. Blue jays are especially good at it, carrying acorns away from the parent tree and burying them at a good depth for sprouting.
