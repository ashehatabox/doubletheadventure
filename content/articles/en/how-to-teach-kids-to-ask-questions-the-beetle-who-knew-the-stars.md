How to teach kids to ask questions is not a problem most parents of preschoolers think they have. Young children ask questions all day long. But there is a second kind of asking that doesn't come as easily: asking *other people* what they know, think or want, especially when you're sure you already know the answer. Many bright, chatty children go through a phase of telling everyone how things should be done. In this article you'll find what "asking instead of telling" means for a child of 3 to 7, what researchers have learned about children's questions (and about grown-ups who tell too much), how a proud little hoopoe on the banks of the Nile learns this lesson in our picture book *The Beetle Who Knew the Stars*, and easy ways to practise it at home.

## What asking instead of telling means for a three- to seven-year-old

The value at the heart of this book fits in one sentence a child can carry around: asking takes you farther than telling.

Inside that sentence are a few simple ideas:

- **Knowing things is great.** Children should be proud of what they've learned. The point is never "don't know things" or "don't share what you know".
- **Other people know things too.** The quiet friend, the little brother, the neighbour, the grandparent: everyone knows something you don't.
- **A question makes room.** When you ask "How do you do it?" or "What do you think?", the other person gets a turn to speak. Telling, all the time, leaves no room.
- **Being asked feels good.** People light up when someone wants to hear what they know. Asking is a kind thing to do.
- **Asking helps you grow.** Every real question is a chance to learn something new, even about something you thought you knew.

For grown-ups, this is a communication habit: ask before you tell. For a five-year-old, it can be as simple as "Ask your friend first."

## Why it matters at this age

### Children are born askers

The good news is that young children already ask a lot. In a large study published in 2007, psychologist Michelle Chouinard analysed around 25,000 questions from recordings of children talking at home with familiar adults. The children asked one to three questions a minute, and about two-thirds of those questions were looking for information. The preschoolers asked for an explanation (a "why" or "how") about 25 times an hour.

Those questions aren't just a way to keep the conversation going. In a 2009 study in *Child Development*, Brandy Frazier and colleagues at the University of Michigan found that children aged 2 to 5 seemed more satisfied when an adult answered a "why" question with a real explanation. When they didn't get one, they often repeated the question or offered their own idea instead. In the researchers' words, children are "trying to get to the bottom of things."

### But asking *people* is a different skill

Asking a grown-up "why is the sky blue?" is one thing. Stopping in the middle of a game to ask a friend "How do *you* want to build it?" is another. That second kind of question means pausing your own big ideas, wondering what someone else thinks, and then actually listening to the answer. It also means being willing to find out you were wrong.

Around ages 4 to 7, many children are proud of how much they know, and they love to show it. That's healthy. It just helps to show them that asking is also a way to be clever, and often a more friendly one.

### Questions can fade if we're not careful

Children's questions don't always survive the move into busier, more structured settings. A 2015 article in ASCD's *Educational Leadership* pulled together several studies on this. In a classic study by Barbara Tizard and Martin Hughes, published in 1984, four-year-olds asked many more questions at home than at nursery school, where teachers usually did the asking. Home is a wonderful place to keep the asking habit strong.

### Telling too much can narrow what children discover

Here's a finding that applies to us as parents. In a 2011 study in the journal *Cognition*, a team including Elizabeth Bonawitz and Laura Schulz of MIT gave preschoolers a new toy that could do several hidden things. When an adult *taught* one of the toy's functions, children mostly stuck to that one thing. Children who weren't taught that way explored more widely and found more of the toy's other features. The researchers concluded that teaching helps children learn efficiently, but they're also less likely to discover something new on their own.

That doesn't mean we should stop teaching. It means a question ("What do you think this does?") can sometimes open more doors than an instruction.

## The story: a hoopoe who knows everything, until she doesn't

*The Beetle Who Knew the Stars* is book 19 in our *Double the Adventure* series. It's set in Egypt, on the green banks of the Nile and in the golden desert right beside it, over one day and one starry night.

Its hero is Kemi, a young hoopoe with a cinnamon-pink body, black-and-white striped wings and a long curved bill. She also has an orange crest that pops up like a fan every time she says she knows something, which is often. Kemi knows a lot and loves telling it. She tells the kingfisher how to fish and the jerboas how to dig. What she never does is ask them anything. Nobody scolds her. Her friends simply go a little quieter whenever she comes near.

At the edge of the desert, Kemi meets Sabu, a small, shiny dung beetle who keeps stopping to look up at the sky while he rolls his ball. Kemi tells him he's going the wrong way, without asking why he looks up. The next day she flies far over the dunes to show that she knows the desert too. But at sunset every dune looks the same, and for the first time Kemi doesn't know the way home.

The turning point is small and brave. Hearing Sabu's star song, Kemi starts to say she knows, then stops, and asks her very first real question. Sabu is delighted to be asked, and what he shows her changes the whole night. When clouds get in the way, Kemi doesn't wait to be rescued: she thinks about who else she could ask, and asks a jerboa family herself. We'll let you and your child discover the rest.

The refrain children love to join in with is "Roll, roll, look up!", and the theme line sums up the book: "Telling shows what you know. Asking helps you grow." You can find the book here: [The Beetle Who Knew the Stars](/books/the-beetle-who-knew-the-stars).

### A real science surprise

Sabu's star-gazing isn't only make-believe. In 2013, researchers from Wits University in South Africa and Lund University in Sweden showed that some dung beetles use the bright band of the Milky Way to roll their balls in a straight line at night. Their eyes are too weak to pick out single stars, so they follow the light of the galaxy as a whole. It's the first animal shown to use the Milky Way to find its way.

## Three things the story shows about asking

### Knowing a lot isn't the problem

Kemi is smart, and she stays smart. At the end of the book she still knows many things. What changes is the order: she asks first. That matters for children who love facts and are proud of them. They don't need to give that up.

### A question is a gift to the other person

When Kemi finally asks, Sabu is thrilled. Later, the jerboas are happy to share their shortcut. Being asked makes them feel that what they know matters. It's a nice moment to point out: "Look how happy Sabu is that she asked!"

### Asking goes both ways

Once Kemi starts asking, Sabu asks her things too, and the two of them become real friends. Questions open up a conversation instead of a lecture. Children can see that the best talking is a back-and-forth.

## Where the value comes from

Every book in our series starts from one value, and this one comes from ancient Egypt. The **Maxims of Ptahhotep** are a book of advice traditionally attributed to Ptahhotep, an official of the pharaoh Djedkare Isesi, more than four thousand years ago. It is often called one of the oldest books of wisdom in the world. The very first maxim tells the reader not to be proud of what they know, and to talk with the ignorant as well as the wise, because no one ever reaches the limits of skill, and good words can be found even among the women grinding grain.

In other words: ask everyone, because wisdom can turn up anywhere. Our story turns that into a simple, secular tale about two animals in the desert. The names come from Egypt too: Kemi from *Kemet*, the ancient Egyptians' name for their own land, and Sabu, an ancient Egyptian name. If you'd like to see how other traditions say something similar, our [values map](/values-map) is a good place to start.

## How to teach kids to ask questions at home

Pick one or two ideas and keep them going for a few weeks. Little moments, repeated often, work better than a big talk.

### Ask your child real questions

Children learn to ask by being asked. Try questions you don't already know the answer to: "How did you build that?" "What was the best part of your day?" "What do you think we should do?" Then listen to the whole answer before you add anything. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child calls this kind of back-and-forth "serve and return", and it's one of the simplest things we can do with young children.

### Catch yourself telling

This one is for us. Next time you're about to say "No, do it like this," try "What are you trying to make?" or "Want to show me how you're doing it?" first. You'll often find there was a good reason behind what looked like a mistake, just like Sabu looking up at the sky.

### Give your child some asking words

Some children tell because they don't have the words to ask. Practise a few together, like a game:

- "How do *you* do it?"
- "What do you think?"
- "Can you show me?"
- "Why do you do it that way?"
- "Do you want to hear my idea, or try yours first?"

### Play "ask before you tell"

During pretend play or building, make a little family rule: before you tell someone your idea, you ask them one question. Grown-ups have to follow it too. Children often enjoy catching parents who forget.

### Notice and name it

When your child asks a friend a question, say what you saw: "You asked Maya how she wanted to play. She looked really happy."

## Share it with your child

A few things to say or do right after reading *The Beetle Who Knew the Stars*:

- **Talk about the quiet friends.** "Why do you think Kemi's friends stopped talking when she came near?" Accept any answer.
- **Remember the lost feeling.** "How did Kemi feel when she didn't know the way?" You might share a time you didn't know something either.
- **Celebrate the first question.** "What happened when Kemi asked Sabu a question?" Look at Sabu's face in the pictures together.
- **Think about the jerboas.** "Who knew the shortcut? How do you think they felt being asked?"
- **Say the refrain together.** Pretend to roll a big ball across the floor, then point up and say "Roll, roll, look up!"
- **Make a question plan.** "What question could you ask a friend tomorrow?" Help your child choose one and remember it.

## Questions parents ask

### How do I teach my child to ask questions instead of bossing other kids around?

Start by asking your child lots of real questions, so they hear what asking sounds like. Give them a few simple phrases, like "How do you want to play?", and practise them in pretend play. When they ask a friend instead of telling, notice it out loud. Most children grow out of the bossy phase as they get more practice.

### My child asks "why" all day. Isn't that enough?

Those "why" questions are wonderful, and research suggests children really want the answers. The skill in this book is a little different: asking other people what *they* know, think or want. Both kinds of asking are worth encouraging.

### Is it bad that my child loves to show what they know?

Not at all. Being proud of what you've learned is healthy. You can simply add a step: "That's a great fact! Want to ask your sister if she knows one too?"

### What if my child is shy and finds asking hard?

Start small and stay close. Let them ask a familiar grown-up first, or practise the question at home before trying it out. If shyness seems to be getting in the way of everyday life, it's worth talking to your child's doctor or educator.

### Is The Beetle Who Knew the Stars right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The bright animals, the popping crest and the refrain "Roll, roll, look up!" work well for little ones. Children of 5 to 7 often connect more with Kemi's big moment of stopping and asking, and with the true story of beetles that follow the Milky Way.
