Teaching kids to respect elders can sound old-fashioned, like making a child sit still and be quiet while the grown-ups talk. But respect for the people who came before can be something warmer and more alive than that: being curious about what grandparents and older friends know, asking them, and really listening to the answer. In this article you'll find what learning from elders means for a child of 3 to 7, what research says about family stories and time spent across generations, how a young pangolin in a Ghanaian forest learns to value her grandmother's old song in our picture book *The Old Path Song*, and simple ways to help your child connect with the older people in their life.

## What respecting elders means to a three- to seven-year-old

For a young child, the value at the heart of this book can be put in one simple sentence: the old ones know things we still need, and it's never too late to go back and ask.

That sentence holds a few ideas a small child can grasp:

- **Older people have been here longer.** They have seen more seasons, more problems and more solutions.
- **What they know can help us.** Not just in a "be polite" way, but practically. A family recipe, a song, a trick for fixing a wobbly chair, a story about how a parent behaved at age four: these are useful, and they are often only kept in people's heads.
- **Listening is the respectful part.** For a little child, respect is less about rules (standing up, not interrupting) and more about attention. Looking at someone, asking a question, waiting for the answer.
- **Old and new can go together.** Learning from elders doesn't mean new ideas are bad. The best result is when children take something old and make it their own.
- **It's never too late.** If a child didn't listen the first time, or forgot, they can go back and ask again. Nobody needs to be ashamed.

Notice what's missing: obedience for its own sake, and the idea that children's ideas don't count. In our book, nobody scolds the young hero for finding the old song boring. She discovers for herself why it matters.

## Why it matters at this age

### Family stories help children know who they are

At Emory University in the United States, psychologists Marshall Duke and Robyn Fivush, with Jennifer Bohanek, studied how much young people knew about their family's history. They made a short "Do You Know" scale of 20 yes-or-no questions, such as whether the child knew how their parents met or where their relatives grew up and went to school. In a 2010 paper, they reported that teenagers aged 14 to 16 who knew more of these family stories reported greater emotional well-being and a stronger sense of identity, even after taking into account how well their family functioned in general. The researchers were careful to say more research was needed. As they put it, family stories "provide a sense of identity through time".

That study was with teenagers, not preschoolers. But stories don't arrive at fourteen out of nowhere. They are told at bedtime, at the dinner table, on long car rides and on visits to grandparents, starting when children are very small. The early years are when the habit of telling and asking begins.

### Time with older people changes how children see them

A review published in the journal *International Psychogeriatrics* in 2018 gathered studies of intergenerational programmes, where preschool or primary-age children spend regular time with older adults. Across the studies that looked at children, these programmes improved how children perceived older people, both in the short and long term. The older adults generally benefited too.

More recently, a 2026 study from UNSW Sydney's Ageing Futures Institute tried a 10-week programme where preschoolers and older adults met weekly for games, music, storytelling and group tasks. It was a feasibility study, so it didn't measure proven effects, but the researchers described the children as confident, enthusiastic and highly interactive, and families kept coming: average attendance was about 80 per cent, and no family dropped out.

The message for parents is gentle and hopeful. Children don't respect elders because they're told to. They come to value them by spending real time together, doing ordinary, enjoyable things.

## The story: a young pangolin and the old path song

*The Old Path Song* is book 17 in our *Double the Adventure* series. It takes place in a green forest in southern Ghana, and its hero is Ama, a young tree pangolin with brown overlapping scales like a pinecone and one golden scale at the tip of her long tail.

Ama loves anything new: new paths, new games, new ways through the trees. Her best friend, Kwesi the bushbaby, with his huge round eyes, is always ready to explore with her. Every evening, her grandmother Nana sits by her hollow log, taps her tail on it, "Ka-tum, ka-tum", and sings an old song. It's a kind of song-map, full of landmarks in the forest: stones, a bent old palm, a fig tree. Ama finds it boring. She has better things to do.

For a while, the new paths are wonderful. Then the dry, dusty wind arrives. The fruit disappears and the forest animals grow hungry. None of Ama's clever new paths leads to food. When Kwesi asks whether the old ones might have known a place, Ama realises, with hot cheeks, that she only half-remembers Nana's song. She never really listened.

This is the heart of the book. Ama doesn't try yet another new path. She chooses to go back, and ask. She sits with Nana, hears the song again, and listens to every word, and Nana tells her the song has passed from grandmother to grandmother.

What happens next is Ama's own doing: she carries the old knowledge forward in her own inventive way, and the forest animals find what they need. We'll leave the details for you to discover together. The book's theme line says it all: "It's never too late to go back for what you left behind." You'll find the book here: [The Old Path Song](/books/the-old-path-song).

## Four things the story shows about learning from elders

### Finding the old ways boring is normal

Ama isn't a "bad" child for thinking her grandmother's song is dull. Children are drawn to what's new; it's part of how they learn. The story doesn't shame her, and you don't need to shame your child either. If your child yawns through Grandpa's story, that's fine. The aim is to plant a seed, not to force a harvest.

### Going back takes courage

The turning point is a choice. Ama could have pretended she knew the way, or kept trying new paths. Instead she admits she forgot and goes back to ask. That's a big thing for a small child (and for adults): saying "I didn't listen, can you tell me again?" The book shows that this is brave, not embarrassing.

### The elder keeps the knowledge, the child uses it

We were careful that Nana doesn't swoop in and save the day. She keeps the song and shares it when asked. Ama is the one who leads, solves problems and makes the key choices. For children, that matters: learning from elders isn't about being rescued by them, it's about being given something you can use yourself.

### Old and new belong together

Ama doesn't throw away her love of new ideas. She adds them to the old song. By the end, the old song has become something new as well. This is a lovely message for children who might worry that respecting the past means giving up their own imagination. It doesn't. The two work best together.

## Where the value comes from

Every book in our series starts from one value, and this one draws on **Sankofa**, from the Akan people of Ghana. Sankofa is an Adinkra symbol, often shown as a bird walking forward with its head turned back, carrying an egg. It is linked to an Akan saying usually translated as "it is not wrong to go back for what you have forgotten". We named it as the source of the book and wrote the story as a simple, secular tale of animals learning a life lesson. The text uses no Twi words, though the characters' names are Akan: Ama, Kwesi, and Nana, which is used for a grandparent or elder.

Ama herself is a real kind of animal. The white-bellied, or tree, pangolin lives in forests of West and Central Africa, including Ghana. It climbs with a prehensile tail that can grip branches, and it feeds on ants and termites. The species is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List, and in 2016 all eight pangolin species were given the highest level of protection under CITES, the international agreement on wildlife trade, because pangolins are so heavily trafficked.

## How to practise learning from elders at home

Pick one or two of these ideas and keep them going for a few weeks. Small, regular moments work better than one big project.

### Make an "Ask a grandparent" question jar

Write simple questions on slips of paper and put them in a jar. Before a visit or video call, your child picks one to ask. Good questions for little ones:

- What games did you play when you were my age?
- What was your favourite food when you were little?
- What song did your mum or dad sing to you?
- What did my mum or dad do that made you laugh when they were small?
- What's something you know how to do that I could learn?

If your child's grandparents aren't nearby or aren't living, an older neighbour, great-aunt, family friend or a librarian at a community reading time can be just as wonderful.

### Learn one old song, game or recipe

Ask an older relative to teach your child one thing that has been passed down: a lullaby, a clapping rhyme, a skipping game, a simple recipe. Then do it often, the way Nana sings her song every evening. Children love repetition, and a song learned from Grandma becomes a little thread that ties the generations together. If you cook together, choose a recipe that's safe for small helpers, keep them away from heat and knives, and check for allergies first.

### Tell your own family stories

You are an elder to your child too. Tell them true, small stories: the day they were born, the house you grew up in, the time you got lost, a mistake you made and how you fixed it. Stories of things going wrong and turning out alright are especially good for children. Short and real beats long and polished.

### Practise "asking again"

Make it normal to go back and ask. When your child forgets something, say, "That's okay. Let's go back and ask." When you forget something, model it yourself: "I don't remember how Granddad fixed this. I'm going to call and ask him." Children learn more from watching us than from being told.

### Let your child make the old thing new

Once your child knows an old song or game, invite them to change it: add a new verse, make up actions, turn a lullaby into a clapping game. This is exactly what Ama does, and it's a great way to show that respect for the old and love of the new can live side by side.

## Share it with your child

A few things to say or do right after reading *The Old Path Song*:

- **Ask the first big question.** "Why did Ama think the old song was boring?" Let your child answer honestly. Many children will agree with her at first.
- **Talk about the hard moment.** "How do you think Ama felt when she couldn't remember the song?" Talk about the hot-cheeked feeling, and that it's okay to feel it.
- **Find the brave choice.** "What did Ama decide to do instead of trying another new path?" Help your child notice that going back to ask was her idea.
- **Join the refrain.** Tap "Ka-tum, ka-tum" together on a table, a box or your knees, slow and steady like Nana's tail on the hollow log.
- **Spot the new ideas.** "What new ideas did Ama add to the old song?" Celebrate that she used her own imagination.
- **Make it personal.** "Who in our family knows old songs or stories? What would you like to ask them?" Then plan to ask, this week.

## Questions parents ask

### How do I teach my child to respect elders without just demanding obedience?

Focus on curiosity and connection rather than rules. Help your child spend real time with older people, ask them questions and listen to the answers. Respect grows naturally when children discover that older people know interesting, useful things. Basic politeness matters too, but it lands better when it comes with genuine interest.

### My child finds grandparents' stories boring. What can I do?

That's very normal for young children, and it's exactly how Ama feels at the start of the book. Keep stories short, ask the grandparent to tell ones about when they were your child's age, and link them to things your child cares about, like games, food or animals. Doing something together, such as baking or singing, often works better than sitting and listening.

### What if my child's grandparents live far away or have died?

Video calls, voice messages and letters can keep connections going across distance. If grandparents have died, you can share their stories, photos, songs and recipes yourself, so your child still receives what they knew. Older neighbours, family friends and community programmes can also give children time with older adults.

### Is The Old Path Song right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The tapping refrain "Ka-tum, ka-tum", the animals and the simple idea of going back to ask are easy for little ones to enjoy. Children of 5 to 7 often connect with the deeper ideas: that knowledge passes between generations, and that old and new ideas can work together.
