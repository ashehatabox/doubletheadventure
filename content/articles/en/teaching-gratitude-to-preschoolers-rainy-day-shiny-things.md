Teaching gratitude to preschoolers usually starts with the same reminder: "What do you say?" The child says "thank you," we nod, and the moment passes. Good manners matter, but gratitude is something bigger and warmer: noticing the good things around us, and the people who make them happen. In this article we look at what gratitude means for a three- to seven-year-old, what researchers have learned about how it grows in young children, how our picture book *Rainy Day, Shiny Things* shows it through three grumpy cats on a wet afternoon, and simple ways to practise it at home without lectures or guilt.

## What gratitude means to a three- to seven-year-old

For a grown-up, gratitude can feel like a big idea. For a young child, it lives in small moments:

- a child notices that Grandpa saved her the last strawberry, and gives him a hug;
- after a dull, rainy morning, a boy remembers that his friend shared a puddle-splashing game with him;
- a child sees a parent packing a favourite snack in the lunch box and says, without being asked, "You remembered!";
- at bedtime, a little one says the best part of the day was building a blanket fort.

When we wrote *Rainy Day, Shiny Things*, we put the idea into words a child can hold: even on a grey, boring day there are good things around us, like people who love us, a warm home or a fun surprise, and noticing them and saying thank you makes us feel happier.

For us as parents, the key word in that sentence is *noticing*. Gratitude isn't a polite phrase we pull out on cue. It's a way of looking at the day. A child who can spot the good in an ordinary afternoon has learned something that will help them for the rest of their life.

### What gratitude is not

It helps to be clear about what we're not teaching, too.

Gratitude isn't pretending everything is fine. A child can be disappointed that it's raining and still find something good in the day. Both feelings can be true at once.

Gratitude isn't being told off for being grumpy. "You should be grateful, other children have nothing" is a sentence many of us heard growing up. It tends to bring guilt, not thankfulness, and young children can't do much with guilt.

And gratitude isn't the same as manners. "Thank you" is one way to show it, but a child can say the words without feeling anything, and another child can feel deeply thankful and show it with a hug or a drawing instead.

## How gratitude grows: notice, think, feel, do

Some of the most useful research on children's gratitude comes from the Raising Grateful Children project at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, led by psychology professor Andrea Hussong. Her team describes gratitude as four parts that work together:

- **Notice:** see what you have been given, however small.
- **Think:** wonder who made it possible, and why they did it.
- **Feel:** enjoy the warm feeling that comes from someone else's kindness.
- **Do:** show your appreciation in some way.

### What parents tend to focus on

In one part of the project, about 100 parents of six- to nine-year-olds kept a diary for ten days of how they encouraged gratitude. Writing about it for the Greater Good Science Center at UC Berkeley, Hussong reported that 85 percent of parents encouraged good manners, but only 39 percent encouraged gratitude beyond manners. Only about a third asked how a gift made their child feel, and just 22 percent asked their child *why* they had received it.

In other words, most of us concentrate on the "do" part, the thank-you, and skip the noticing, thinking and feeling that make it real. The good news is that the other three parts are easy to add, and they fit naturally into everyday talk.

### Why the preschool years matter

Hussong also notes that the skills behind gratitude, such as understanding feelings and seeing things from someone else's point of view, start to develop more quickly between about three and five. Younger children may show only some parts of gratitude, and often only when prompted. Older children and adults are more likely to do all four on their own.

A study published in the *British Journal of Developmental Psychology* by Jackie Nelson and colleagues followed children from age three to five. Children who understood emotions better at three, and who understood other people's thoughts better at four, tended to understand gratitude better at five. Most five-year-olds connected receiving something good with feeling good. Fewer than one in five understood the idea of returning a kindness because someone had helped them.

So if your four-year-old forgets to say thank you, or your five-year-old doesn't yet see why they might do something nice for the neighbour who fed the cat, that's normal. Gratitude is built slowly, on top of feelings words and perspective-taking, and those are things we can practise every day.

### Why it's worth the effort

Maryam Abdullah, parenting program director at the Greater Good Science Center, points to studies with older children that link feeling grateful with feeling more supported by parents and teachers. She also describes research with families of six- to nine-year-olds: parents who felt more grateful themselves were more likely to set goals for their children's gratitude, and their children expressed more of it. Children learn this one mostly by watching us.

## How Rainy Day, Shiny Things shows gratitude

*Rainy Day, Shiny Things* is the fifth book in our *Double the Adventure* series. Three cats share a cosy house on Maple Street with kind Mrs. Maple, who tucks each of them in at night. Whiskers is curious and explores every cupboard and shelf. Shadow is quiet and loves napping in a warm sunbeam. Mittens is playful and chases anything that moves.

Then comes a rainy day, and all three are grumpy. Whiskers wants to be outside chasing a butterfly. Shadow misses the sun and can't settle. Mittens finds her toy mouse terribly dull. Many children will recognise this mood straight away: the "there's nothing to do" feeling of a wet afternoon indoors.

A sudden crash from the kitchen changes everything. A glass has fallen, and among the sparkling mess the cats spot something surprising that belongs to Mrs. Maple. Their bad mood disappears, and the rest of the day looks different. We'll let your child discover the details as you read together. You can find the book here: [Rainy Day, Shiny Things](/books/rainy-day-shiny-things).

A note for grown-ups: in the story the cats get close to broken glass. When you read it, it's a good moment to say, "If a glass breaks at our house, we stay back and a grown-up cleans it up."

### From grumbling to noticing

The heart of the book is a change in how the cats look at things. Nothing big happens to fix the weather. It's still raining at the end. What changes is that the cats stop thinking about what they can't have and start noticing what they do have: a warm home, each other, a small adventure and, above all, Mrs. Maple's care.

That's the "notice" step in action. We wanted children to see that gratitude often begins when we look up from what's missing.

### Saying thank you, cat style

At dinner, the cats feel how much love surrounds them, and they show it in the way cats do: purring and rubbing against Mrs. Maple. They don't make a speech. This is a lovely thing to point out to young children. Thank you can be a word, but it can also be a hug, a smile, a drawing or helping to set the table.

### A new way to see the rain

The book ends with the three cats curled up together on the windowsill. The rain that annoyed them in the morning now sounds soothing. Whiskers sums it up with the book's theme line: "Every day—rainy or sunny—has something special."

We like that the rain doesn't stop. The lesson isn't "cheer up, the sun will come out." It's that good things are there even on the grey days, if we look.

## Teaching gratitude to preschoolers: ideas for home

You don't need a big programme. Pick one or two of these and fold them into the days you already have.

### Point out the "who" behind the good things

The "think" step is the one most often missed, and it's easy to add. When something good happens, wonder aloud about who made it possible: "Who do you think made this soup?" "Grandma came all the way across town to see your concert. Why do you think she did that?" You're helping your child connect good things with the people behind them.

### Ask how it felt

After a kind act, a gift or a fun outing, try "How did that make you feel?" instead of (or as well as) "What do you say?" Naming the warm feeling is the "feel" step, and it helps children notice it next time.

### Find one special thing a day

Borrow Whiskers' idea. At dinner or bedtime, each person shares one special thing from the day. On hard days the special thing can be tiny: a good sandwich, a funny dog on the walk, the sound of rain on the roof. Go first sometimes, so your child hears how it's done.

### Let thank you take many forms

Some children find words hard, especially shy ones or very young ones. Offer other ways to show thanks: a drawing for the bus driver, a high five for a sibling, helping a parent carry the groceries. Like the cats in the book, children can say thank you without words.

### Model it, out loud

Let your child hear your own gratitude: "I'm so glad the neighbour shovelled our path." "Thank you for helping me find my keys, that made my morning easier." And when your child thanks you, let them see that it matters: "That made me feel really happy."

### Try a mealtime thank you

Many families have a small way of marking the start or end of a meal. In Japan, for example, people say *itadakimasu* before eating and *gochisōsama deshita* afterwards, words of thanks for the food and for everyone whose work made the meal possible. You don't have to borrow those words, but a family habit of naming who made the meal, grew it or brought it home is a natural fit with the cats' dinner scene.

### Go easy on the guilt

If your child is grumpy about the rain or a cancelled playdate, let them be grumpy first. "You really wanted to go outside. That's disappointing." Once the feeling has been heard, children are often much more ready to find something good. Gratitude grows best when it isn't forced.

## Share it with your child

Some things to say or do after reading *Rainy Day, Shiny Things* together:

- **Talk about the grumpy cats.** "Why were the cats grumpy on the rainy day?" Let your child say what they think. Then ask, "Have you ever felt like that?"
- **Find the turning point.** "What changed the cats' mood?" Notice together that the weather stayed the same.
- **Spot the cat-style thank you.** "How did the cats say thank you to Mrs. Maple without words?" Then: "How could you say thank you without words?"
- **Think about who looks after you.** "Who takes care of you, like Mrs. Maple takes care of the cats?" Make a little list together, out loud or with drawings.
- **Hunt for a special thing.** On the next rainy or boring day, go on a "special thing hunt" around the house: a cosy blanket, a favourite book, a good smell from the kitchen.
- **Make a thank-you picture.** Choose someone from your child's list and help them draw a picture to give that person.
- **Use the theme line.** At the end of the day, say it together: "Every day—rainy or sunny—has something special." Then each share what yours was.

## Questions parents ask

### At what age can children understand gratitude?

The skills behind it start to grow quickly between about three and five. Research following children from three to five found that most five-year-olds connect receiving something good with feeling good, but fewer than one in five understand the idea of returning a kindness. Expect gratitude to come with prompting at first and more on its own as children get older.

### My child says thank you but doesn't seem to mean it. Is that a problem?

Not really. Saying thank you is a good habit, and the feeling often grows into the words over time. You can help by adding the other parts of gratitude: noticing who made something possible, wondering why they did it, and asking how it felt. Those are the parts most parents skip.

### Should I tell my child how lucky they are compared with other children?

It's usually better to focus on what your child can notice and enjoy in their own life, and on the people who care for them. Comparisons can bring guilt rather than gratitude, which isn't much help to a small child. As children get older, helping others together can widen their view more gently.

### How can I teach gratitude without nagging?

Model it out loud, ask open questions ("Who made this possible?" "How did it feel?"), and build in small routines like sharing one special thing at dinner. Children pick up gratitude far more from watching us than from being reminded.

### Is Rainy Day, Shiny Things right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The three cats are easy to tell apart, and a bored, rainy day indoors is something even very young children know well. Younger children enjoy the cats' personalities and the surprise in the kitchen; children of five to seven enjoy talking about why the cats felt differently at the end, even though it was still raining.
