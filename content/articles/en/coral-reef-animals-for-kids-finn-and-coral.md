Coral reef animals for kids are a gift to any parent who reads aloud: fish that change colour as they grow up, a dolphin with a whistle that works like a name, and a whole underwater city built by animals smaller than a fingertip. Our picture book *Finn & Coral* takes place on one of those reefs, where a little fish and his big sister learn about keeping promises. Here are the real animals behind the story, where the book playfully bends nature, and simple facts and games to share with a 3 to 7 year old.

## Which fish are Finn and Coral?

The book never says what kind of fish Finn and Coral are. On the cover, one is a warm orange and the other a deep blue, and both have the round, friendly shape of a cartoon reef fish. We needed a real fish to follow, so we picked one that fits them surprisingly well: the **Atlantic blue tang** (*Acanthurus coeruleus*).

Here's why. According to the Reef Environmental Education Foundation (REEF), young blue tangs are a vivid lemon yellow all over, with a thin blue ring around the eye. As they grow, they pass through an in-between stage, with a yellow body and blue fins, before they turn fully deep blue. So a small, warm-coloured fish and a bigger blue one could, in real life, simply be a younger and an older blue tang. That's a nice match for a little brother and his big sister.

Blue tangs live on shallow reefs in the western Atlantic Ocean. The University of Michigan's Animal Diversity Web says they range from New York down to Brazil, out to Bermuda, and are most common in the Caribbean, Florida and the Bahamas, usually between 2 and 40 metres deep. A fully grown blue tang can reach about 39 centimetres long.

### Not Dory, but a cousin

Many children will shout "Dory!" when they see a blue reef fish. Dory is a different species, the **palette surgeonfish** (*Paracanthurus hepatus*), which the Aquarium of the Pacific says lives in the Indian and Pacific Oceans. It's a cousin in the same family, which is why the two look a little alike.

## Real blue tang facts children love

Once your child knows Finn and Coral might be blue tangs, there's a lot to discover.

**They're reef gardeners.** REEF describes blue tangs as dedicated plant-eaters that graze algae off the reef all day. That job matters. Seaweed grows faster than coral, and if nothing eats it, it can smother the coral.

**They carry a "scalpel."** Look closely at the base of a blue tang's tail and you'll find a small, sharp, pale spine on each side. It looks like a surgeon's scalpel, which is why this family is called surgeonfish. Animal Diversity Web adds that the scientific name *Acanthurus* means "thorn tail," and that the fish can flick the spine out when it's defending itself.

**Their name means sky blue.** REEF points out that *coeruleus* is Latin for "sky blue" or "dark blue," a good name for the grown-up, if not the yellow youngster.

**They go to bed in the reef.** Blue tangs are busy in the daytime. At night they tuck themselves into cracks and crevices in the reef, where predators can't easily reach them.

**There's safety in numbers.** Adults may feed alone, in small groups, or in big crowds of more than a hundred fish. Animal Diversity Web says schooling helps protect them while they eat, and that predators attack a fish on its own more often than a fish in a group.

That last fact connects nicely with the story. When Coral promises to stay close, she's promising something real reef fish already know: out on the reef, staying together is safer than swimming off alone.

## Why small reef fish stay close to home

Reef fish have a surprising start in life. The Australian Museum explains that most reef fishes have a two-part life: a mostly settled adult life on the reef, and a baby stage spent drifting in open water. Adult reef fishes take little or no care of their young. The tiny larvae, typically 1 to 20 millimetres long, end up out in the open sea and fend for themselves for anywhere from two to twenty weeks, then must find a reef to settle on.

Once a small fish arrives on a reef, the reef's nooks, branches and crevices are its best protection. Animal Diversity Web notes that young blue tangs are solitary and keep to their own small home range, which grows as they grow.

This is the biggest place where *Finn & Coral* bends nature. Real reef fish don't grow up in families with a mother who sends them on errands, and fish don't collect seashells. A young fish doesn't grow up with a big sister to guide it. The family in the book is a story idea, and a lovely one, because it lets children see a big sister and a little brother working out what it means to count on each other. You can say this to your child plainly: "Real fish don't have families like ours, but the people who made this book gave them one so we could think about promises."

## The dolphin: a kind stranger with a whistle for a name

When Finn is alone, a big shadow passes over him. It turns out to be a curious, gentle dolphin who helps him stay calm. The book doesn't name the species, but the dolphin most often seen around warm reefs and coasts is the **common bottlenose dolphin** (*Tursiops truncatus*), so that's the one we'll follow.

### A dolphin is not a fish

This is the fact many young children find most surprising. NOAA's National Ocean Service explains that even though dolphins live in the ocean all the time, they are mammals, like us. They are warm-blooded. They don't have gills like fish; they breathe air with lungs, so they must swim up to the surface often to take a breath. The blowhole on top of a dolphin's head works like a nose. Dolphin mothers give birth to live babies and feed them milk.

### Big, smart and sociable

NOAA Fisheries describes bottlenose dolphins as living in warm and temperate waters all over the world: in harbours, bays and river mouths, near coasts, and far out in the open ocean. They are big animals. NOAA gives their length as roughly 2 to 4 metres (6 to 13 feet), so the "big shadow" in the story is no exaggeration next to a little reef fish.

They eat fish, squid, crabs and shrimp, and they hunt in clever ways. NOAA describes dolphins working as a team to herd fish into a tight bunch, then taking turns charging through it. To find food they listen carefully and also use **echolocation**: they send out high-pitched clicks and listen for the echoes bouncing back.

### Babies who stay close

Here's a fact that fits the book's theme beautifully. NOAA says dolphin calves swim in what's called "baby position," just behind and beside their mother, where they can easily nurse and where her movement through the water makes swimming easier for them. Calves generally stay with their mothers for three to six years.

### A whistle that works like a name

Children love this one. The University of Texas Marine Science Institute explains that each bottlenose dolphin makes its own unique sound, called a **signature whistle**. Baby dolphins create their own signature, which other dolphins repeat back to them, and they keep using it all their lives. In the murky water where many bottlenose dolphins live, a "name" you can call out helps a group keep track of each other.

### Where the story bends

Dolphins eat fish. A real dolphin meeting a small fish like Finn would more likely see a snack than someone to comfort, so a dolphin offering kind words is a gentle story bend. It works because dolphins really are curious and social, and because children need to see that helpers exist when they feel lost.

## The coral reef: a city built by tiny animals

Coral looks like rock, or maybe like a plant. It's neither. NOAA Fisheries explains that coral reefs are built by tiny sea animals called **polyps**. Each polyp has a small, soft body, and it builds a hard skeleton of calcium carbonate around itself, the way a clam grows its shell. Thousands of polyps living together make a coral colony, and colonies over many, many years make a reef.

The Smithsonian's Ocean portal adds more details children enjoy:

- Corals are related to **sea anemones**. A polyp is shaped a bit like a tin can open at one end, with a mouth surrounded by a ring of tentacles.
- Those tentacles have stinging cells that catch tiny creatures swimming too close.
- Most polyps on a reef are smaller than 1.5 centimetres across, about the size of a child's fingernail.
- Warm, shallow-water corals have tiny partners living inside them: single-celled algae called **zooxanthellae** (say it "zo-o-zan-THELL-ee"). The algae use sunlight to make food and share some with the coral, and the coral gives the algae nutrients in return. That teamwork is what lets corals grow big enough to build reefs.
- The largest coral reef, the Great Barrier Reef, stretches about 2,600 kilometres along the coast of Australia. The reef as it is today began growing about 20,000 years ago.

Coral grows slowly, yet reefs are home to a huge share of ocean life: according to NOAA, they cover less than 1 percent of the ocean but are home to nearly a quarter of all ocean species.

## Why reefs need looking after

Coral reefs are in trouble in many places. NOAA's National Ocean Service explains **coral bleaching**: when corals are stressed by changes such as warmer water, too much sunlight or pollution, they push out the algae living inside them. Without those algae, the coral loses its main source of food, turns white or very pale, and is more likely to get sick. Warmer ocean temperatures caused by climate change are the leading cause. If the stress isn't too severe, coral can recover.

NOAA Fisheries lists other threats too: pollution from land, trash in the sea, and careless diving and boating. In the Caribbean, where blue tangs live, two important reef-building corals, elkhorn and staghorn coral, have declined by more than 90 percent over about 25 years.

For a young child, the message can stay simple and hopeful: coral is alive, so we treat it gently. If your family ever visits a reef, look but don't touch or stand on coral. Keep trash out of the sea and off the beach. At home, an aquarium visit or a library book about the ocean can start a lifelong love of the sea.

## Share it with your child

Here are some facts and small games to bring the reef into your home after reading *Finn & Coral*.

**Facts to share, in kid words:**

- "Some blue fish start out yellow! They change colour as they grow up."
- "Fish that swim together are safer than fish swimming alone."
- "Dolphins aren't fish. They breathe air, just like you."
- "Every dolphin has its own whistle, like a name."
- "Coral looks like rock, but it's made by tiny animals."

**Games and ideas:**

- **Yellow to blue.** Draw a fish together and colour it yellow. Then draw it again "all grown up" and colour it blue. Talk about how your child has changed since they were a baby.
- **Whistle names.** Each person in the family makes up a special whistle, hum or clap pattern. Take turns calling someone with their sound. Can they find you with their eyes closed?
- **School of fish.** In the living room or the park, everyone is a fish. One grown-up is the "big shadow." When the shadow appears, all the fish swim close together.

**Questions to ask:**

- Why do you think little fish like to stay close to the reef?
- Coral promised to stay close to Finn. What does it feel like when someone stays close to you?
- If you were a dolphin, what would your whistle sound like?

## Questions parents ask

### Are Finn and Coral real types of fish?

The book doesn't name a species. We follow the Atlantic blue tang because young blue tangs are yellow and adults are deep blue, which matches a small warm-coloured little brother and a bigger blue big sister. It's our pick, not something the book states.

### Are dolphins fish?

No. Dolphins are mammals. They breathe air through a blowhole on top of the head, they are warm-blooded, and mothers give birth to live babies and feed them milk. Fish breathe through gills.

### Do fish really have brothers and sisters who look after them?

Not the way the book shows. Most reef fish take little or no care of their young, and the babies drift out into open water before settling on a reef. A fish family with a mother and two children is a story idea that lets children think about promises and trust.

### Can we see a coral reef in Canada?

Warm, shallow coral reefs like the one in the book need clear, warm tropical water, so you won't find them along Canada's coasts. An aquarium is the easiest way for most families to see reef fish and coral up close.
