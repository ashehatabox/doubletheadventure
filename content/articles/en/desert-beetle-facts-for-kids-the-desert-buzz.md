Looking for desert beetle facts for kids? Our picture book *The Desert Buzz* stars Etoo, a tiny beetle, and Bella, a busy bee, two best friends who climb cactus plants, rest under cool rocks and, one scorching afternoon, have a very big falling-out over a sand tower. Children often finish the story with questions that have nothing to do with feelings at all: Can beetles really live in the desert? Where do bees get water there? Is the wise old scorpion real? This article gives you the true facts about the animals and plants behind the story, shows where the book playfully bends nature, and ends with easy things to share with a 3 to 7 year old, including a few safety rules for meeting desert creatures in real life.

## Where does The Desert Buzz take place?

The book doesn't name its desert, but it leaves a big clue: cactus plants. According to Encyclopaedia Britannica, cacti are native to the Americas, growing through most of North and South America, from British Columbia and Alberta down into Chile and Argentina. Only one cactus, the mistletoe cactus (*Rhipsalis baccifera*), also grows wild in the "Old World," in tropical Africa, Madagascar and Sri Lanka, and it usually perches on trees in wet forests, not in sand dunes.

So a sandy desert full of cactus flowers points to places like the **Sonoran Desert**, which stretches across parts of Arizona, California and northwestern Mexico. That's a handy place to "visit" with your child, because scientists there have studied exactly the kinds of creatures that appear in the book: beetles, bees and scorpions.

## Desert beetle facts for kids: who is Etoo?

The book never says what kind of beetle Etoo is. But if you go looking for beetles in a desert, the family you're most likely to meet is the **darkling beetles** (scientists call the family Tenebrionidae).

Here's what the Missouri Department of Conservation's field guide says about them:

- **There are a lot of them.** About 20,000 species of darkling beetles live around the world, and about 1,200 in North America north of Mexico, most of them west of the Mississippi River.
- **They love dry places.** Darkling beetles live worldwide, but they're most abundant and varied in dry regions such as the American Southwest and the Namib Desert in southern Africa.
- **They look like a "beetle's beetle."** Most are dull black or brown, oval or long, with a hard, thick outer shell. Many can't fly, and in deserts it's especially common for the two hard wing covers to be joined together, with no split down the back. That helps keep water inside the body.
- **They clean up.** Most darkling beetles are scavengers that eat bits of dead plants. Some eat fungi, and some eat dead animals, including other insects.
- **They have a smelly trick.** When something bothers them, some darkling beetles play dead, and some lift their back end high in the air, nearly doing a headstand, and give off a bad-smelling spray, a little like a skunk.
- **You may have met their babies.** Mealworms, sold as food for pet lizards and birds, are the young (larvae) of certain darkling beetles.

### How do beetles stay cool in the desert?

In the book, Etoo and Bella rest under cool rocks, and Etoo later sits alone under a rock to think. That's one of the most true-to-life parts of the story. In a Smithsonian Books excerpt, entomologist Erica McAlister and Adrian Washbourne explain that most desert beetles spend their time about 20 centimetres (8 inches) down in the sand, sheltered from the heat and wind, and only come to the surface when they have to. Under a rock or under the sand is where a desert beetle would really be on a scorching afternoon.

### The beetle that drinks fog

This is the fact children remember. In the Namib Desert in southern Africa, where it almost never rains, fog rolls in from the ocean some nights. A darkling beetle called *Onymacris unguicularis*, sometimes nicknamed the "head-stander beetle," has a clever way to drink it.

The Smithsonian excerpt describes it step by step. The beetles come up out of the sand a few hours before midnight, before the fog arrives, and climb to the top of a dune where the fog is thickest. Then they do a headstand, with their back pointed into the foggy wind. Tiny droplets of fog collect on their backs and run down little grooves towards their mouths. The same excerpt reports that in measurements of how much the beetles could take in, the best result was a gain of 30 percent of the beetle's body weight in water in a single fog.

Etoo doesn't do this in the book, but it's a wonderful answer when your child asks how a beetle survives without rain.

## Is Bella a real desert bee?

Bella isn't a named species either. When we picture a bee, most of us think of the **honey bee**, the striped, hive-living bee that makes honey. Here's a surprise: in the Americas, the honey bee isn't native. The Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum describes it as an introduced bee, brought by people.

The desert's own bees are a much bigger story. The Desert Museum says the Sonoran Desert region may have as many as 1,000 species of bees. Some quick facts from them that children love:

- **Most desert bees live alone.** Unlike honey bees, which live in big colonies, most desert bees are "solitary": each mother bee builds her own nest.
- **Most nest underground.** The great majority of desert bees dig burrows in the ground for their babies. Some nests are just a few centimetres deep; in sandy soil, some go down 2 metres (6 feet) or more.
- **They come in every size.** The world's smallest bee, *Perdita minima*, lives in this desert and is less than 2 millimetres long, smaller than a grain of rice. At the other end are carpenter bees, which the museum calls "gentle giants," nearly 4 centimetres long.
- **There's a cactus bee.** A bee called *Diadasia rinconis*, the common cactus bee, pollinates prickly pear, cholla and saguaro cacti. So a bee flying from cactus flower to cactus flower, as Bella does, is exactly right.
- **Only females sting.** The stinger evolved from the tube a female uses to lay eggs, so male bees can't sting at all.

### What makes a bee buzz?

The book's title is a buzz, so this one's worth knowing. Britannica explains that most of the buzzing we hear is the sound of a bee's tiny wings beating very fast: some bees beat their wings about 230 times every second. A University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources entomology blog gives the same figure for honey bees, and adds that they fly at about 24 kilometres (15 miles) an hour on average.

### Could a bee's wings knock over a sand tower?

In the story, the breeze from Bella's wings as she zooms past knocks Etoo's sand tower flat. That's a playful exaggeration. A real bee's wings make a breeze far too gentle to knock down sand, even a beetle-sized tower. Ask your child, "Could that really happen?" Then: even if it was an accident, would it still feel bad to Etoo? (Yes, and that's the point of the book.)

## The wise old scorpion

The scorpion in *The Desert Buzz* doesn't appear in the action. Etoo only remembers its advice: "When anger buzzes loud, pause and listen to your heart." It's a good choice for a wise old desert character, because scorpions are true desert specialists with a few amazing secrets.

According to Britannica:

- There are about 1,500 species of scorpions.
- They live on every major landmass except Greenland and Antarctica, from Canada and central Europe to the southern tips of South America and Africa.
- They're mainly **nocturnal**, which means they hunt at night.
- They eat insects, spiders and other small creatures.
- **Scorpion mothers give piggyback rides.** After the babies are born, they climb onto their mother's back and stay there for anywhere from 1 to 50 days.

### Do scorpions really glow?

Yes, and this is a fact that makes children gasp. Under ultraviolet (UV) light, scorpions glow a bright blue-green. National Geographic explains that the glow comes from chemicals in their skin, covering the whole body from the pincers to the tail. And the best part: **nobody knows for sure why they glow.** Scientists have suggested many ideas: it might help them find shade, or recognise each other, or it might just be an accident of their body chemistry. It's a perfect example to share with a child that scientists are still finding things out.

### Look, don't touch

Scorpions have a venomous stinger at the tip of the tail, and some kinds can sting dangerously. The rule for children is simple: never touch or pick up a scorpion. In fact, the same rule goes for the beetles and bees in this book when you meet them outdoors. Watch them, wonder about them, and let them get on with their day.

## Story or real? How the book bends the desert

Here's a quick guide for when your child asks, "Is that what real ones do?"

- **Real:** beetles and bees both live in deserts with cactus plants.
- **Real:** desert beetles shelter from the heat under rocks and sand during the hottest part of the day.
- **Real:** bees drink nectar from cactus flowers, and some desert bees specialise in cacti.
- **Real:** scorpions live in deserts and are mostly out at night.
- **Story bend:** a bee's wing breeze can't knock down a sand tower.
- **Story bend:** beetles don't build towers out of sand for fun, and beetles and bees aren't friends. They don't talk, argue or say sorry.
- **Story bend:** real scorpions don't hand out advice. The book borrows desert animals to talk about something people do: get very angry, then learn to cool down and make up.

## Share it with your child

These are easy ways to bring the real desert into your reading time.

- **Do the fog beetle headstand.** Your child kneels on a soft floor, puts their hands down and lifts their bottom high, like the Namib beetle facing the fog. Spray a gentle mist of water on their back with a plant sprayer and watch the drops run "towards the mouth." Keep it short and silly, and only on a safe, soft surface.
- **Buzz like a bee.** Ask your child to flap their arms as fast as they can for five seconds. How many flaps? Then tell them a bee beats its wings about 230 times every second. Try humming as you flap to make your own buzz.
- **Hide from the heat.** On a sunny day, put two ice cubes outside: one in full sun and one under a rock or in deep shade. Check back in ten minutes. Which melted more? Talk about why Etoo and Bella rest under cool rocks. (Keep ice cubes away from children under 3, who could choke on them.)
- **Ask "story or real?"** After reading, ask: "Do real bees have beetle friends? Can a bee's wings knock down sand? Do beetles hide under rocks?" Children love catching the made-up parts, and it builds early thinking skills.
- **Take a slow breath like Etoo.** When you finish the book, try the slow, deep breath that helps Etoo calm down. Smell a pretend cactus flower, then blow out like a cool desert breeze. Practising when everyone is calm makes it easier to use when a big feeling arrives.

### If you meet these creatures outside

You don't need a desert to find beetles and bees. Darkling beetles live in many gardens, under rocks, logs and mulch, and bees visit flowers almost everywhere in spring and summer. A few simple rules keep everyone safe:

- **Look, don't touch.** Watch insects where they are. Don't pick up beetles, bees or any creature you can't name.
- **Stay calm around bees.** Bees are usually busy with flowers. Walk slowly away rather than swatting.
- **Scorpions are a grown-up job.** If you live in or travel to an area where scorpions live, children should never pick one up or reach under rocks with bare hands. Let a grown-up check first.

If anyone is stung and has trouble breathing, swelling of the face or a strong reaction, get medical help right away, and ask your doctor about any worries about stings or allergies.

## Questions parents ask

### What do desert beetles eat?

Most darkling beetles, the most common beetles in deserts, are scavengers. They eat bits of dead plants, and some eat fungi or dead animals, including other insects. That makes them part of the desert's clean-up crew.

### How do beetles survive in the desert without water?

Many hide in the sand or under rocks during the heat to save water, and their hard, often fused wing covers help keep moisture in. In the Namib Desert, one darkling beetle does a headstand in the night fog so drops collect on its back and run down to its mouth.

### Do bees live in the desert?

Yes. The Sonoran Desert region may have as many as 1,000 kinds of bees. Most live alone and nest in burrows in the ground, and some, like the common cactus bee, specialise in visiting cactus flowers.

### Are scorpions dangerous to children?

Some kinds can sting dangerously, so children should never touch or pick up a scorpion. Scorpions are mostly out at night and hide during the day, which is another reason not to reach under rocks without a grown-up.

### Why do scorpions glow in the dark?

They don't glow on their own; they glow blue-green under ultraviolet light because of chemicals in their skin. Scientists still don't know why. Ideas include helping them find shade or recognise each other.
