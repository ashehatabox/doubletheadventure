Teaching courage to preschoolers sounds like it should be about big, bold moments: the first jump into the pool, the first day at school, the first time on stage. But for many young children, the bravest thing they do all week is much quieter. It's saying "hello" to a new friend. It's telling the teacher they need the bathroom. It's speaking up when another child is being left out. In this article we look at what courage really means for a three- to seven-year-old, why quiet children are often braver than we notice, what child-health experts suggest (and warn against) when a child holds back, how our picture book *Pip the Brave Parrot* shows a small voice finding its strength, and everyday ways to grow courage at home without pushing.

## What courage means to a three- to seven-year-old

Ask a small child what "brave" means and you'll often hear "not scared." It's an understandable answer, and it's one worth gently turning around. The idea at the heart of *Pip the Brave Parrot* is the one we most want children to take away: being brave doesn't mean you don't feel scared. It means doing the right thing even when your voice shakes.

That shift matters for two reasons.

First, it's true. Everyone feels fear, and young children feel it often, because so much of the world is new. If courage meant "never scared," children would quickly decide they simply aren't the brave type.

Second, it puts courage within reach. A child can't choose whether to feel scared, but they *can* choose, with support, to take one small step anyway. That's something they can practise, like riding a bike.

### Three small kinds of courage

For young children it helps to think of courage as a few small, practical skills rather than one heroic trait:

1. **Trying something new**, even when you're not sure it will go well.
2. **Using your voice**: asking for help, saying "no, thank you," saying "stop, I don't like that."
3. **Standing up for someone else**, even if it's just going to get a grown-up.

The second and third are the kinds of courage our book is about, and the ones quiet children are often quietly practising.

## Quiet isn't the opposite of brave

Many families have one child who joins the party game right away, and another who stands at the edge, holding a grown-up's leg. It's tempting to call the first one brave and worry about the second.

Child-health organisations describe this kind of holding back as part of a child's **temperament**, the natural way they respond to the world. The Australian government's Raising Children Network says plainly that there's nothing wrong with shyness, and that shy children often warm up once they get to know a person or a situation. The American Academy of Pediatrics, on its HealthyChildren.org site, also describes shyness as a normal difference between children; some shy children grow more social with time, and others stay most comfortable in familiar settings.

The Victorian government's Better Health Channel notes something parents of quiet children will recognise: shy children are often good listeners, behave well and do well at school. They are most uncomfortable when they feel "on show," such as meeting someone new or speaking in front of a group.

### Why the label matters

Both the Raising Children Network and Better Health Channel advise against calling a child "shy." Better Health Channel points out that children may come to live up to the labels they're given. Raising Children Network suggests describing a child as "slow to warm up" instead, because "shy" can make a child feel something is wrong with them. If someone else calls your child shy in front of them, it suggests correcting it gently, with something like "Lou takes a little while to warm up."

This is the thinking behind our book. We didn't want to tell children that quiet is bad or that you have to be loud to be brave. A slow-to-warm-up child who finally says "can I play?" has done something courageous.

### What being laughed at does

Better Health Channel lists teasing, harsh criticism and pressure to perform among the things that can feed shyness. It also describes a cycle many of us remember: a child feels awkward, criticises themselves afterwards, feels more self-conscious next time, and holds back even more.

That cycle is exactly where Pip begins.

## How Pip the Brave Parrot shows courage

*Pip the Brave Parrot* is the ninth book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and its value is courage. You'll find it here: [Pip the Brave Parrot](/books/pip-the-brave-parrot).

Pip is a young parrot who lives in the oldest tree of a bright green jungle. He has shiny feathers and strong wings. What he doesn't have, or doesn't think he has, is a big voice. His voice is small, and he remembers being laughed at for it. So Pip hums to himself when he's alone and stays silent when the other, louder birds are around.

We wanted children to see that Pip isn't weak. What's holding him back is a memory and a feeling, which is often what holds a real child back too.

### A reason to be brave

One afternoon Pip hears tiny, frightened cries and finds a baby bird that has fallen from its nest, far too small to fly back up. Pip tries to help on his own first, and it doesn't work. The only way to get help is the one thing he's most afraid of: calling out, loudly, so the whole jungle can hear.

This is the turning point we built the book around. Pip isn't pushed onto a stage; he finds his courage because someone needs him. Your voice isn't for showing off; it's for the moments that matter.

### Brave with a wobbly voice

When Pip finally sings out, his voice shakes. We made sure of that. Children need to see that the brave moment doesn't feel smooth or confident from the inside. It wobbles, and then, as Pip keeps going, it grows. We'll leave the rest for your child to discover, except to say that his call is heard, and the jungle that once laughed starts to listen.

The book's theme line sums up what we hope stays with children: "Your voice doesn't have to be loud to be strong. It just has to be truly yours."

### A note for real life

In the story Pip helps a fallen chick, but children shouldn't copy what Pip does. The National Audubon Society explains that many baby birds found on the ground are fledglings: young birds that have left the nest on purpose, can hop, and are still being looked after by their parents nearby. Its advice for a healthy fledgling is to walk away. If your child finds a baby bird, the brave and kind thing to do is the same thing Pip does in spirit: tell a grown-up, and let the grown-up check what's needed. For a bird that seems hurt or in danger, Audubon recommends contacting a wildlife rehabilitator.

## What helps, and what doesn't

The advice from child-health organisations lines up closely, and it can be summed up as: support the feeling, encourage the small step, and don't push or rescue too much.

### What helps

- **Name the feeling and take it seriously.** "You feel nervous about saying hi. That makes sense; they're new." Better Health Channel suggests asking your child what they're afraid of and letting them talk about it.
- **Prepare together.** Raising Children Network suggests talking through new situations ahead of time. Rehearsing what to say ("Can I play too?") turns a scary unknown into something your child has already done once, at home.
- **Take small steps.** HealthyChildren.org recommends starting with low-pressure moments, like greeting a neighbour or joining a game for a short while. Better Health Channel suggests setting small goals your child can actually reach.
- **Praise the specific brave thing.** Not "good job" but "You asked the librarian for the dinosaur book all by yourself. That was brave." Raising Children Network suggests praising specific behaviours like saying hello or playing a little way away from you. HealthyChildren.org encourages recognising even small steps, because they can be hard for a shy child.
- **Show them how.** Children learn by watching. Both HealthyChildren.org and Better Health Channel suggest modelling the confident behaviour you hope to see, and Better Health Channel adds that it can help to share your own memories of feeling shy and how you managed.
- **Build on what they love.** HealthyChildren.org notes that activities matching a child's interests give shy children a natural way to connect.

### What to avoid

- **Mocking or teasing**, even gently. "Cat got your tongue?" can feel like being laughed at, which is exactly what made Pip go quiet.
- **Pushing too far, too fast.** Better Health Channel warns against pushing children beyond what they can manage and then making them feel bad when they fall short. Asking a slow-to-warm-up child to sing for the relatives is usually a step too big.
- **Comparing.** Raising Children Network advises against comparing a quiet child with a more outgoing sibling or friend.
- **Rescuing every time.** It's natural to speak for a quiet child. But Better Health Channel notes that overprotecting can teach children that new situations are something to fear, and Raising Children Network cautions that too much comforting can signal that a situation really is scary. Try waiting a few seconds longer than feels comfortable before you step in.

## Teaching courage to preschoolers: ideas for home

Courage grows in tiny daily moments. Here are some ways to practise without turning anything into a test.

### Give them "voice jobs"

Small, real tasks that need a voice: telling the waiter "water, please," asking the librarian where the animal books are, saying "thank you" to the bus driver. Start with the ones that feel easiest and stand right beside your child.

### Practise with toys first

Pretend play is a safe rehearsal space. Let a stuffed animal be the "new kid" and have your child show it how to ask to join a game. Then swap roles. Many children find it easier to be brave for a toy than for themselves.

### Make a "brave words" list

Together, choose two or three short phrases your child can keep ready: "Can I play?", "I need help, please," "Stop, I don't like that." Practise them at dinner in silly, whispery and big voices. The goal isn't loudness; it's knowing the words are ready.

### Notice courage for others

Pip's courage came from wanting to help. When your child stands up for a sibling, gets a grown-up for a friend who's hurt, or invites someone left out to play, name it: "That took courage. You used your voice for someone else." If you'd like to explore this further, our article on [teaching kindness to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-kindness-to-preschoolers-secret-hero) looks at how small acts for others grow a child's sense of who they are.

## Share it with your child

Things to say or do after reading *Pip the Brave Parrot* together:

- **Ask about the quiet part.** "Why do you think Pip didn't sing when the other birds were around?" Let your child connect it to being laughed at, in their own words.
- **Talk about the wobble.** "How do you think Pip felt when his voice shook? Why did he keep going?" Help your child find words like nervous, wobbly, brave, proud.
- **Find the reason.** "What made Pip decide to sing out loud?" Point out that he did it because someone needed help.
- **Say the theme line together.** "Your voice doesn't have to be loud to be strong. It just has to be truly yours." Try it in a whisper and then in a normal voice, to show that both count.
- **Play "Pip's song."** Take turns humming a tiny tune and having the other person copy it, the way a song can travel from one bird to another. Quiet humming counts.
- **Remember a brave time.** "When did you use your voice, even though you felt shy?" If your child can't think of one, offer one you noticed.
- **Make a plan.** "If you ever found a baby bird, what would you do?" The answer you're hoping for: tell a grown-up.

## Questions parents ask

### Is it a problem if my preschooler is very shy?

Usually not. Child-health organisations describe shyness as part of temperament, and many shy children warm up once they know a person or place. If shyness causes your child a lot of distress, keeps them from everyday activities, or comes with worries about separating from you, talk to your child's doctor or educator.

### Should I make my child say hello to people?

Encouraging is fine; forcing usually backfires. Practise greetings at home, offer easier options like a wave or a smile, and praise the attempt. Pushing a child further than they can manage, or making them feel bad when they freeze, tends to make the next time harder.

### How do I explain courage to a three-year-old?

Keep it simple: "Brave means you feel scared, and you do it anyway." Then point out real examples: trying a new food, going down the slide, telling you they need help.

### My child is brave at home but silent at school. Is that normal?

Many children are much more comfortable in familiar places, and child-health sites note that shy children often feel most uneasy when they feel "on show." Share what you see at home with your child's educator. If your child consistently can't speak in certain settings, or you have other worries, your child's doctor is the right person to ask.

### Is Pip the Brave Parrot right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The jungle, the fallen chick and Pip's song are easy to follow. Five- to seven-year-olds can also talk about the harder ideas: why being laughed at made Pip go quiet, and why a shaking voice didn't make him any less brave.
