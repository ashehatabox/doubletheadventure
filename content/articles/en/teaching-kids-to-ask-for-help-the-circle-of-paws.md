Teaching kids to ask for help sounds like the easy half of raising a generous child. We spend so much time encouraging our children to share, to help a friend, to pitch in at home, that we rarely stop to notice the other side of the circle: letting someone help *them*. Yet many children (and plenty of grown-ups) find receiving much harder than giving. In this article you'll find what asking for and accepting help means for a child of 3 to 7, what researchers have learned about how young children feel about helping and being helped, how a fiercely independent chinchilla in the high Andes learns this lesson in our picture book *The Circle of Paws*, and simple ways to practise it at home.

## What asking for help means to a three- to seven-year-old

The value at the heart of this book can be said in one short sentence a child can hold on to: help is a circle; asking for help is brave, and letting others help you is a gift to them.

There are a few ideas tucked inside that sentence:

- **Everybody needs help sometimes.** Grown-ups too. Needing help doesn't mean you're small, slow or not good enough.
- **Asking is a skill.** It takes words ("Can you help me with this?"), timing and a bit of courage. Like any skill, it gets easier with practice.
- **Saying yes to help is kind.** When a friend offers and you accept, you let them feel the good feeling of helping. Saying no every time can leave a friend feeling shut out.
- **Help goes around.** Today a friend helps you; another day you help them, or someone else. Nobody needs to keep score.
- **Everyone has something to give.** Even the smallest person in the room. A child who can't lift the heavy box might be the one who spots where it should go.

Notice what the value is *not*. It isn't "never do things yourself", and it isn't "you owe people back". Independence is a wonderful thing for young children. The trouble only starts when "I can do it myself" turns into "I must always do it myself".

## Why it matters at this age

### Young children love to help

Helping comes early. In a well-known 2006 study published in *Science*, Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello of the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig showed that 18-month-old toddlers would spontaneously help an adult who seemed to need it, for example by picking up a clothes peg the adult had dropped and couldn't reach. They weren't rewarded or even asked.

Giving also seems to feel good from very early on. In a 2012 study in *PLoS ONE*, Lara Aknin, Kiley Hamlin and Elizabeth Dunn at the University of British Columbia found that toddlers under two looked happier when giving treats to a puppet than when receiving treats themselves, and happier still when they gave away one of their *own* treats.

That second finding is worth sitting with as a parent. If giving feels good to a toddler, then a child who never lets anyone help them is quietly taking that good feeling away from the people around them. This is exactly the idea our story explores.

### Worries about looking "not smart" start early

Asking for help can start to feel risky surprisingly young. In studies with 576 children aged four to nine, reported in *Scientific American* by researchers Kayla Good and Alex Shaw, children heard short stories about two kids who had both done poorly on a test: one who wanted to *be* smart and one who wanted to *seem* smart. Four-year-olds thought both kids were equally likely to raise a hand and ask the teacher for help. By about seven or eight, children predicted that the child who wanted to look smart would avoid asking in front of classmates. When help could be asked for privately, that difference went away.

In other words, by the early school years many children already link asking for help with looking less capable in front of others. The preschool years are a good window to build the opposite habit: asking is normal, sensible and even admirable.

### How we offer help matters too

There's another side to this. A 2025 review by Jellie Sierksma of Utrecht University and Kristin Shutts of the University of Wisconsin–Madison looked at what researchers call the unintended costs of help. Help that children haven't asked for, or help that takes over, can sometimes leave them feeling less capable. In one study described in the review, children whose puzzles were finished for them kept trying for less time on a later task than children who were shown how to solve the puzzles. Gentler help, like a hint or a question, was seen more positively than simply giving the answer.

So the circle runs both ways. We want children who feel free to ask, and grown-ups who answer in a way that leaves the child feeling more capable, not less.

## The story: a chinchilla who helps everyone but herself

*The Circle of Paws* is book 18 in our *Double the Adventure* series. It's set high in the Andes, on rocky golden slopes above old stone terraces, where everybody on the mountain helps everybody else.

Its hero is Killa, a silver-grey chinchilla with enormous round ears and a striped woven bag in red, orange and pink. Killa is the fastest helper on the mountain. She carries seeds and grass for her neighbours all day long. But whenever someone offers to help *her*, she laughs and says she can manage on her own. Her best friend Sami, a gentle golden vicuña with a little red tassel on his ear, keeps offering, and keeps being turned down. You can see the disappointment in his drooping ears.

Then a hailstorm knocks down Killa's wall and fills her burrow with ice. Even then, she refuses help, and works alone through a long, cold night. In the morning, Sami finds a kind way to explain what she's been missing: he compares helping to a game of catch, where you can't only throw. Asking turns out to be harder for Killa than lifting any stone. But she does it, and her neighbours come running, paw to paw.

Being helped feels strange to her at first, then warm. And to Killa's surprise, her helpers thank *her* for letting them help. Later, when a much bigger snow buries burrows all over the mountain, Killa becomes the one who goes around asking who needs help and who can give it, including a tiny hummingbird who thinks she's too small to have anything to offer. We'll let you discover the rest together.

The refrain children love to join in with is "Paw to paw, round and round!", and the theme line sums up the whole book: "Help is a circle. Let it come back around." You'll find the book here: [The Circle of Paws](/books/the-circle-of-paws).

## Four things the story shows about giving and receiving

### Being very independent can shut friends out

Killa isn't selfish. She's the most generous animal on the mountain. That's what makes her such a useful hero for children who are proud of doing things themselves. The story gently shows that always saying no to help has a cost, not for Killa alone but for Sami, who wants to be part of her life.

### Asking takes courage

The turning point isn't the storm. It's the moment Killa decides to ask. The book treats that as a brave act, harder than any heavy lifting. That framing can help children who feel embarrassed to ask: it isn't weakness, it's courage.

### Receiving is half of giving

When the helpers thank Killa for letting them help, children see the circle close. Accepting help isn't taking something away from others; it lets them give. This links neatly with what the toddler research suggests about how good giving feels.

### Everyone has something to give

The tiny hummingbird who thinks she's too small to help, and so can't possibly ask, is there for every child who has felt like the littlest one in the room. Her gift turns out to be something only she can do. No one in the circle is too small to count.

## Where the value comes from

Every book in our series starts from one value. This one draws on **ayni**, a word from the Quechua and Aymara peoples of the Andes in Peru and Bolivia. Ayni is usually explained as reciprocity, often summed up as "today I help you, tomorrow you help me". Families still take turns working each other's fields and helping to build each other's houses, with the help returned in turn. The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian describes it as a core value that has served generations of Andean people since before the time of the Inka.

We named ayni as the source of the book and wrote the story as a simple, secular tale of animals learning a life lesson. The characters' names come from Quechua: Killa means "moon" and Sami means "luck" or "happiness". If you'd like to see how other traditions put a similar idea into words, our [values map](/values-map) is a good place to start.

## How to practise asking for help at home

Choose one or two ideas and keep them going for a few weeks. Small, everyday moments work better than one big lesson.

### Ask for help out loud yourself

Children learn most from watching us. Say it plainly and often: "Can you help me carry the groceries?" "I can't reach the top shelf, can you pass me the bowl?" "I'm stuck on this, I'm going to ask Grandma." Then thank them warmly. You're showing that asking is normal for grown-ups too.

### Give your child the words

Some children don't ask because they don't know how. Practise a few simple phrases together, almost like a game:

- "Can you help me, please?"
- "I tried, and I'm stuck."
- "Can you show me how?"
- "Yes, please!" (the hardest one for a little Killa)
- "No thank you, I want to try first, but can I ask you later?"

That last one matters. Children can learn to say "not yet" to help without saying "never".

### Praise the asking, not just the result

When your child asks for help, notice it: "You asked me when you got stuck. That was a smart thing to do." This echoes the advice in the *Scientific American* piece: adults can praise children for seeking help and treat it as part of learning, not a sign of failing.

### Help with a hint before you take over

When your child does ask, try a question or a small hint first: "What do you think the next piece might be?" "Shall we try turning it around?" Step in more only if they need it. This keeps the help on their side, so they finish feeling capable.

### Make giving and receiving a family circle

Look for jobs where everyone both gives and receives: one person holds the dustpan while another sweeps; your child helps set the table and someone else helps them pour a drink. At the end of the day, you might ask, "Who helped you today? Who did you help?" Over time children see the circle for themselves.

## Share it with your child

A few things to say or do right after reading *The Circle of Paws*:

- **Ask the first big question.** "Why do you think Killa never let anyone help her?" Accept any answer. Many children understand her very well.
- **Think about Sami.** "How do you think Sami felt when Killa said no?" Look at his ears in the pictures together.
- **Talk about the brave moment.** "Why was asking so hard for Killa?" You might share a time when asking was hard for you.
- **Find the small helper.** "What did the little hummingbird have to give?" Then: "What's something *you* are good at that could help someone?"
- **Say the refrain together.** Hold hands or touch paws and say "Paw to paw, round and round!" while you go round in a little circle.
- **Make it personal.** "When is it hard for you to ask for help?" Listen without fixing. Then agree on a signal or phrase your child can use next time.

## Questions parents ask

### How do I teach my child to ask for help without making them dependent?

Help-seeking and independence go together. Encourage your child to try first, then ask when they're stuck, and answer with a hint or a question before doing it for them. That way they learn to ask *and* keep building their own skills.

### My child refuses all help and melts down when things go wrong. What can I do?

Many young children go through a strong "I do it myself" stage, and that's healthy. Offer help without pushing ("I'm here if you want me"), name the feeling ("That's really frustrating"), and praise them when they do ask. If the frustration seems very intense or isn't easing with time, it's worth talking to your child's doctor or educator.

### Why does my seven-year-old suddenly hide when they don't understand something at school?

Research suggests that by about seven, many children start to worry that asking for help in front of others might make them look less smart. Let them know that asking is what good learners do, and talk to their teacher about quieter ways to ask, such as a private signal or a quick check-in.

### Is The Circle of Paws right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The animals, the refrain "Paw to paw, round and round!" and the simple idea of saying "yes, please" to help work well for little ones. Children of 5 to 7 often connect with the deeper ideas: that help goes around, and that everyone, even the smallest, has something to give.
