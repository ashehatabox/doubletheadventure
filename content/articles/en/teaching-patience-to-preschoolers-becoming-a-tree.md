Teaching patience to preschoolers can feel like asking the impossible. Small children live in the now: they want the snack, the turn, the birthday, this minute. The good news is that patience isn't a trait a child either has or lacks. It's a set of skills that grows slowly, with practice and with grown-ups who make waiting feel safe and worth it. In this article we look at what patience really means for a three- to seven-year-old, what research says about why some children wait more easily than others, how our picture book *Becoming a Tree* shows patience as something active rather than "sit still and be quiet", and simple ways to practise it at home.

## What patience means to a three- to seven-year-old

For an adult, patience might mean staying calm in traffic. For a young child, it shows up in much smaller, much harder moments:

- waiting for a turn on the swing while another child is still on it;
- not opening a present until everyone is at the table;
- trying a puzzle again after the piece won't fit;
- waiting for paint to dry, cookies to bake, or a seed to sprout;
- hearing "not now, in five minutes" and finding something to do in the meantime.

Look closely at that list and you'll see two kinds of patience. One is *waiting*: holding back from something you want now. The other is *persisting*: keeping at something that isn't working yet. Children need both, and they are linked. A child who can wait a little can also try a little longer.

It also helps to remember what patience is not. It isn't silence, stillness or swallowing every feeling. A child who says "I hate waiting!" and then waits anyway is being very patient indeed. Feelings are allowed; what we're growing is the ability to carry them while time passes.

## Why waiting is hard (and why it gets easier)

Waiting draws on what psychologists call executive function: the brain's set of "air traffic control" skills, which include working memory, flexible thinking and self-control. Harvard University's Center on the Developing Child explains that children aren't born with these skills; they are born with the potential to develop them through interactions and practice. The preschool and early school years are a period of big growth, which is why a four-year-old who melts down over a queue can, a couple of years later, wait with a little more grace.

That means two things for parents. First, a three-year-old's impatience is normal, not a sign of a spoiled child. Second, practice really does help. Every small, successful wait is a rep at the gym.

## What the marshmallow test does (and doesn't) tell us

You may have heard of the famous "marshmallow test" from the 1960s and 70s: a child is offered one treat now, or two if they can wait alone for a while. Early follow-ups suggested that children who waited longer did better years later, and the idea spread that patience at four predicts success for life.

More recent research paints a more interesting picture.

In 2013, researchers at the University of Rochester ran a version of the test with 28 children aged 3 to 5. Before the marshmallow, each child did an art project, and an adult promised better art supplies if they waited. For half the children, the adult came back with the supplies as promised. For the other half, the adult came back empty-handed. Then came the marshmallow. Children whose adult had kept their promise waited about 12 minutes on average; children whose adult had not waited about 3 minutes. Nine of the 14 children in the reliable group waited the full 15 minutes, compared with only one in the unreliable group. Waiting, it seems, is partly a sensible judgement: *is the good thing actually going to come?*

In 2018, a larger study of more than 900 children found that the link between waiting in preschool and later school achievement was much smaller than the early studies suggested, and that it mostly disappeared once the researchers took the child's family background and early environment into account.

For parents, this is reassuring. Your child's patience isn't fixed destiny, and one tense afternoon in a waiting room says nothing about their future. And there is a very practical takeaway hidden in the Rochester study: **children wait better for grown-ups who keep their word.** When "after lunch" really means after lunch, children learn that waiting pays off.

## How Becoming a Tree shows patience

*Becoming a Tree* is the second book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and patience is at its heart.

Oakie is a tiny oak seed lying in the dark soil. He has never seen the sun or felt the wind, and when a passing bird describes them, he wants them right away. He pushes and strains, but the earth above him is far too heavy. A kind, calm worm tells him something that becomes the refrain of the book: "Good things take time."

Oakie doesn't love that answer, and that's part of why children relate to him. But time passes, rain and sun do their work, and Oakie grows the way real oaks do: a root reaching down first, then a green shoot breaking through into the light. Later, a hungry deer nibbles one of his new leaves, and Oakie has to decide whether being out in the open is worth the risk. He chooses to keep growing. By the last pages, a new little seed is dreaming of the sky, and Oakie has become the one who knows what to say.

You can find the book here: [Becoming a Tree](/books/becoming-a-tree).

### Patience as active, not passive

What we love about Oakie is that his patience is never just sitting still. He keeps growing. He faces a setback and chooses courage. And at the end he passes on what he learned to someone smaller. That's the version of patience we want children to see: not "be quiet and wait", but "keep going, little by little, even when it's slow."

The book's theme line puts it simply: "Be patient. Good things take time, but they're worth it."

### A setback isn't failure

The deer moment is small but important. Oakie loses a leaf, and for a moment he wonders whether he should have stayed safe in the ground. Children face the same kind of moment when a tower falls, a drawing goes wrong or they lose a game. The story lets you talk about the idea that a setback is part of growing, not proof that you can't do it. (The book also touches gently on forgiveness when the same deer later rests in Oakie's shade, but patience is the main thread.)

### A real-world truth inside the story

Oakie's journey matches real oaks closely. According to Natural Resources Wales, acorns fall in autumn, usually from October; roots grow over the winter and the shoot comes up in spring. So when Oakie waits in the dark, there's real growing happening that nobody can see yet. That's a lovely idea to share with a child who feels stuck: sometimes you're growing on the inside before anything shows.

## Teaching patience to preschoolers: ideas for home

These habits work with real children, not perfect ones. Pick one or two to start.

### Keep your promises about waiting

This is the lesson of the Rochester study. If you say "We'll go to the park after your nap", go to the park after the nap. If plans change, explain why and offer the next real time. Children who trust that the good thing will come find it easier to wait for it.

### Make time visible

"Five minutes" means very little to a four-year-old. Use something they can see: a sand timer, a kitchen timer, "two more songs", "when the big hand gets to the bottom". A waiting chain made of paper links, one torn off each night before a big event, turns days into something a child can hold.

### Give waiting something to do

Patience is easier with a plan. In a line or a waiting room, play I spy, count red things, hum a song, or squeeze hands in a secret code. You're not distracting your child from waiting; you're teaching them the trick adults use too.

### Name the feeling, then wait together

"It's so hard to wait for your turn. You really want that swing." Saying the feeling out loud doesn't make the wait longer; it often makes it lighter. Then stay close. Waiting next to a calm grown-up is a skill children borrow before they own it.

### Choose slow projects on purpose

Some of the best patience teachers are things that simply can't be hurried: bread rising, a puzzle over several days, a block tower built a little each morning, and, of course, a seed growing. Late autumn is acorn season in much of Canada and Europe, which makes this a wonderful moment to plant one with your child (check your local park's rules before collecting, and keep acorns away from children under three, who might put them in their mouths). Or plant a bean in a clear cup so you can watch the root appear first, just like Oakie's.

### Notice the waiting, not just the result

"You waited for your sister to finish talking. That was hard and you did it." Describing the effort helps your child see patience as something they *can do*, not something they either have or don't.

### Use the refrain

Short phrases stick. When your child is frustrated that something isn't working yet, "Good things take time" can become a gentle family code, said kindly, never as a scold. Over time, you may hear your child say it to a younger sibling, just as Oakie does.

## Share it with your child

Some things to say or do after reading *Becoming a Tree*:

- **Feel it together.** "How do you think Oakie felt when he pushed and pushed and nothing happened?" Let your child show you with their body: curled up tight, then stretching slowly.
- **Ask about waiting.** "What's something you're waiting for right now?" Birthdays, losing a tooth, being tall enough for a ride: all count.
- **Ask about growing.** "What are you getting better at, little by little?" Riding a bike, writing their name, swimming. Help them see their own slow growth.
- **Talk about the deer.** "Why do you think Oakie kept growing after the deer ate his leaf?" There's no wrong answer; listen for ideas about bravery and trying again.
- **Be the worm.** "What would you tell a little seed who wants to grow up fast?" Children love being the wise one.
- **Grow your own Oakie.** Plant a seed together and check it each day. When nothing shows yet, remind each other that roots grow first.

## Questions parents ask

### At what age can children learn to be patient?

Children start practising patience very early, but the skills behind it (self-control, working memory, flexible thinking) grow a great deal between about 3 and 7. Expect a three-year-old to manage only short waits with lots of help, and a six-year-old to manage longer ones, especially with a plan. Progress is uneven, and tired or hungry children of any age wait less well.

### Is my impatient four-year-old normal?

Very likely, yes. Impatience is typical in the preschool years because the brain skills that support waiting are still developing. If you have real worries about your child's attention, behaviour or development, talk to your child's doctor or educator, who can look at the whole picture.

### Does the marshmallow test predict my child's future?

Not in the simple way people often think. A 2018 study of more than 900 children found only a small link between preschool waiting and later school achievement, and it mostly disappeared once family background and early environment were taken into account. Another study found that children waited much longer when the adult had been reliable. Patience grows with experience and trust.

### Should I make my child wait on purpose?

Short, kind, predictable waits are good practice, such as finishing your sentence before you answer, or waiting until everyone is seated to eat. Waits that feel endless or unpredictable aren't. Keep them small, make the time visible, and always follow through.

### Is Becoming a Tree right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The story is simple and gentle, the refrain is easy to join in on, and the idea of a seed growing is something even young children can watch in real life. Older children, 5 to 7, enjoy talking about the deer moment and about Oakie becoming a helper to the new seed at the end.
