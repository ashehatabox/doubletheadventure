## Story in short
Kemi, a young hoopoe on the green banks of the Nile, knows lots of things and loves to tell them; her crest fans up every time she says she knows. She tells the kingfisher how to fish and the jerboas how to dig, but never asks them anything, and little by little her friends go quiet around her. At the desert's edge she meets Sabu, a small dung beetle who keeps stopping to look at the sky, and she tells him he's pushing his ball the wrong way without asking why he looks up. Next day she flies far over the dunes to show she knows the desert, and at sunset every dune looks the same: for the first time she doesn't know the way home. Hearing Sabu's star song, she starts to say she knows, stops, and asks her first real question. Sabu, delighted to be asked, shows her the river of stars that guides him. Each question opens like a door. When a cloud hides the stars, she asks a jerboa family, who happily show a shortcut, and from the last dune she sees the Nile shining below. Back home she still knows many things, but she asks first.

## Characters
- **Kemi** (Eurasian hoopoe): cinnamon-pink body, black-and-white striped wings and tail, long curved bill, an orange black-tipped crest that fans up when she says she knows (and later when she learns).
- **Sabu** (dung beetle / scarab): shiny black with a blue-green sheen, rolls a round ball, a tiny golden star-shaped spot on his back; sings a star song his grandmother taught him; asks questions too.
- **Jerboas:** sandy desert jumpers with huge ears and long tufted tails; the youngest has a bent ear tip.
- **Nile friends:** a pied kingfisher, little green frogs.

## The real animals
- **Hoopoe** (*Upupa epops*): widespread across Africa, Europe and Asia, common in Egypt; erectile crest; probes soil for insects with its long bill. It is the national bird of Israel (avoid if irrelevant) and appears in ancient Egyptian art (check).
- **Dung beetles:** roll balls of dung backwards, pushing with their hind legs while standing on their front legs (head down); some species (e.g. *Scarabaeus satyrus*, studied in South Africa in 2013) orient using the Milky Way at night. The ancient Egyptian scarab (*Scarabaeus sacer*) was linked to the sun god Khepri.
- **Jerboas:** desert rodents with long hind legs for hopping (e.g. lesser Egyptian jerboa *Jaculus jaculus*).
- **Pied kingfisher** (*Ceryle rudis*): black-and-white kingfisher that hovers over water; common on the Nile.

## Setting and environment
The Nile valley and the desert beside it, over one day and night: green fields, palms and reeds by the river, golden dunes at noon, sunset, a starry night with the Milky Way, silver pre-dawn, back home in the morning.

## The value
For a child: asking takes you farther than telling. For grown-ups: a communication habit, asking before telling: questions make room for others' words, make people feel valued, and help you learn. Knowing things is fine; asking first is better. Avoids: "knowing things is bad", the beetle rescuing her (she asks the jerboas herself), shaming (her friends just go quiet), and a scary night.

## Where the value comes from
The **Maxims of Ptahhotep** (ancient Egypt): a book of advice attributed to Ptahhotep, vizier of the pharaoh Djedkare Isesi (about 2400 BC), one of the oldest wisdom books in the world. Its first maxim: don't be proud of your knowledge; consult the ignorant as well as the wise, because the limits of skill are never reached, and good speech can be found even among women at the grindstones. Sources: `values-library.json` key "19" (row egypt-01: Lichtheim, Ancient Egyptian Literature I; Papyrus Prisse; UCL Digital Egypt). Names: Kemi from *Kemet*, ancient Egypt's name for itself ("black land"); Sabu, an ancient Egyptian name.

## Refrain and theme line
- Refrain: "Roll, roll, look up!" (and the crest's "Pop!")
- Theme line: "Telling shows what you know. Asking helps you grow."
