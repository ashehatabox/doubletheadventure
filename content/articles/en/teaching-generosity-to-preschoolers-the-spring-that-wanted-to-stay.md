Teaching generosity to preschoolers can feel like an uphill walk. You ask your four-year-old to share the crayons, and the whole box disappears behind their back. You suggest giving a cousin one of the stickers, and the answer is a firm "they're MINE." It's easy to worry that you're raising a little hoarder. You almost certainly aren't. In this article we look at what generosity really means for a three- to seven-year-old, why holding on is such a normal stage, what researchers have found about how small children feel when they give, how our picture book *The Spring That Wanted to Stay* turns a child's biggest fear about sharing into a story about water, and simple ways to grow generosity at home without forcing it.

## What generosity means to a three- to seven-year-old

For grown-ups, generosity is a big word. It covers money, time, patience and forgiveness. For a young child it starts much smaller and much more concrete: giving someone a piece of my snack, letting my brother hold my truck, drawing a picture for Grandma, helping a friend pick up the blocks they spilled.

At this age, generosity has three simple parts:

1. **Noticing someone else.** Seeing that a friend has no red crayon, or that a sibling looks sad.
2. **Choosing to give something that's mine.** A toy, a turn, a hug, a bit of help.
3. **Feeling what happens next.** The friend's smile, the game that gets better because two people are playing it.

That third part matters most. Children don't become generous because we tell them sharing is good. They become generous because, over and over, they notice that giving feels good and that the people around them are happier for it.

### The fear behind "it's mine"

When a young child refuses to share, it's tempting to see selfishness. But there's often a worry underneath: if I give some away, I'll have less. If I lend my truck, maybe I won't get it back. If I give my friend a turn, maybe the turn will never end.

For a small child that worry is real, because the world is still full of things they don't control. Toys get put away, snacks run out, grown-ups decide when it's time to go home. Holding tight to what's theirs is one of the few ways they can feel in charge.

That fear is exactly what our book is about. Instead of telling children "don't be greedy," we wanted to answer the question they're really asking: *what if giving means I disappear?*

## Why holding on is a normal stage

If your preschooler finds sharing hard, you're in good company. Child-development organisations are clear that sharing is a skill children grow into, slowly.

ZERO TO THREE, a US early-childhood organisation, explains that very young children haven't yet developed the ability to put themselves in someone else's shoes. They're focused on their own wants and feelings, and being unwilling to share at this age doesn't mean a child is a bad person. It notes that many parents expect sharing by age two, but that these skills develop between about three and a half and four years old, and calls learning to share with grace "a long process."

The American Academy of Pediatrics, on its HealthyChildren.org site, describes how, around age three, children become more aware of other people's feelings and start cooperating more in play. It says children will learn to take turns and share toys in small groups, and that three-year-olds can start to solve disputes by taking turns or trading toys, though they usually need a grown-up's encouragement at first.

So when your three-year-old clutches the crayons, they aren't failing at generosity. They're right on schedule, at the very beginning of learning it.

## What research says about small children and giving

Here's the encouraging part: even very young children seem to enjoy giving.

In a study published in *PLoS ONE* in 2012, psychologists Lara Aknin, Kiley Hamlin and Elizabeth Dunn at the University of British Columbia gave toddlers under two some treats, then asked them to give one to a puppet. Researchers who watched the videos rated how happy the children looked. The toddlers looked happier giving treats away than receiving them. And they looked happier giving away one of *their own* treats than giving away an identical treat that the experimenter had just handed them. In other words, the giving that cost them something seemed to feel best.

Another study, by Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello, published in *Developmental Psychology* in 2008, looked at 20-month-olds who helped an adult with small tasks. Children who were given a toy as a reward for helping were later *less* likely to help than children who got praise or no reward at all. The researchers concluded that even early helping comes from inside the child, and that material rewards can weaken it.

Put together, these studies suggest something useful for parents: the good feeling of giving is already there in small children. Our job is less about installing generosity and more about protecting that feeling and giving it room to grow, without turning it into a deal ("share and you'll get a treat").

HealthyChildren.org also reminds parents that children give more freely when they see the adults around them doing the same, and suggests helping children focus on the purpose of a gift: it's meant to help someone else, not to earn a thank-you.

## How The Spring That Wanted to Stay shows generosity

*The Spring That Wanted to Stay* is the tenth book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and its value is generosity. You can find it here: [The Spring That Wanted to Stay](/books/the-spring-that-wanted-to-stay).

The hero isn't an animal this time. She's water. High on a mountain, melting snow bubbles up as a tiny spring called Nura. Close by grows Willo, a young willow tree. Nura gives Willo water; Willo gives Nura shade and the songs of the birds in his branches. It's the simplest picture of generosity we could think of: two friends, each giving what they have.

### The fear of running out

Then Nura learns that her water doesn't stop with Willo. It flows all the way down the mountain to the whole valley. And she gets scared. If she keeps giving herself away, won't she spread thin and vanish?

That's the same fear a child has about the sticker book. We wanted children to recognise it, and to see that it's a reasonable feeling, not a bad one.

### What happens when we hold on

After a stormy night, rocks block Nura's path, and she decides to keep all of herself. At first she grows into a big, proud pond. But water that doesn't move grows still and murky. The frogs leave. Down below, the meadow turns brown, the otters and bees struggle, and Willo droops as his birds fly away. Nura is fuller than she's ever been, and lonelier.

We were careful here. The book never calls Nura greedy or bad. It simply shows what happens: keeping everything doesn't make her happy, and it doesn't make anyone else happy either. Children can see that for themselves, which is far more powerful than being told.

### A gift to begin with

Grandmother Cloud, a wise elder, reminds Nura of something she'd forgotten: she was a gift to begin with, and gifts are meant to keep moving. We'll let your child discover the rest, except to say that when Nura flows again she doesn't shrink at all; she becomes more than she ever was.

That's the heart of the book, and its theme line sums it up: "Water that is held grows still. Water that is shared stays alive."

### A story that's also true

Part of why we chose water is that the idea is true in the real world. The US Geological Survey explains that a spring is a place where water moving underground finds an opening to the surface; the water comes from rain and snowmelt that soaked into the ground earlier. Water that flows away to rivers and the sea rises again as clouds and falls again as rain and snow. Nothing given in the water cycle is truly lost. It comes back in a new form, which is exactly what we hope children come to feel about kindness.

## Teaching generosity to preschoolers at home

Generosity grows in small, ordinary moments. Here are some ways to practise without turning sharing into a battle.

### Don't force it; make it safe

Forcing a child to hand over a toy they're still playing with usually teaches them that sharing means losing. Instead, try what ZERO TO THREE calls a "long turn": the child keeps playing until they're done, and the waiting child gets help finding something else to do. A kitchen timer can also help, because then it's the bell that says the turn is over, not you.

Before friends visit, let your child put away one or two very special things they don't want to share, and choose some toys that are for everyone. Knowing that a few treasures are safe often makes children more willing to share the rest.

### Show them how you give

Children copy what they see. Let them catch you giving: sharing your dessert, lending a neighbour a ladder, making soup for a friend who's ill. Say it out loud, simply: "Ana's been tired. I'm bringing her some soup. I think it'll make her day better."

### Notice the giving, skip the prize

When your child shares, describe what happened and how it felt for the other person: "You gave Leo the blue car. Look how big his smile is." That kind of noticing helps your child connect giving with the good feeling. Try not to pay for generosity with treats or toys; the research above suggests rewards can make children less keen to help later on.

### Give more than things

Generosity isn't only about stuff. Young children can give time ("Let's read Grandpa a story on the phone"), help ("Can you hold the door for the lady with the stroller?"), attention ("Your sister's tower fell down. What could help her feel better?") and creativity (a drawing, a song, a card). Children who don't have much to give in toys still have a lot to give.

### Make it a family habit

Pick one small giving ritual and keep it going: a "share box" where everyone puts one toy they've outgrown to pass on, a once-a-month treat baked for a neighbour, or picking a book to donate to the library sale. Small, regular giving does more than one big gesture.

If gratitude is something you're also working on, our article on [teaching gratitude to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-gratitude-to-preschoolers-rainy-day-shiny-things) pairs well with this one: children who notice what they've been given often find it easier to give.

## Share it with your child

Things to say or do after reading *The Spring That Wanted to Stay* together:

- **Ask about the fear.** "Why did Nura want to keep all her water?" Let your child put her worry into their own words. Then ask, "Have you ever felt like that about something of yours?"
- **Look at the pond.** "What happened to the frogs, the otters and Willo when Nura stopped flowing?" Help your child see that keeping everything affected everyone, including Nura.
- **Talk about full and lonely.** "Nura was so full. Why do you think she still felt empty?" This is a big idea, and even a simple answer ("because her friends were sad") is a great start.
- **Say the theme line together.** "Water that is held grows still. Water that is shared stays alive." Try saying it slowly like a still pond, then quickly like a rushing stream.
- **Play "pass the water."** Take turns pouring water from one cup to another over a sink or bowl, saying who it's for: "This is for Willo, this is for the bees." Show your child that the water doesn't disappear; it just moves.
- **Find a gift that comes back.** "Can you think of something you gave that came back in a new way?" A shared toy that became a shared game, a smile that got a smile back.
- **Choose something to share.** "What's one thing you could share this week?" Let your child choose. A choice they make is worth more than one you make for them.

## Questions parents ask

### At what age do children start sharing?

Gradually, between about three and five. ZERO TO THREE notes that sharing skills develop between around three and a half and four years old, and HealthyChildren.org says three-year-olds start to solve disputes by taking turns or trading, usually with a grown-up's help. Before that, refusing to share is normal and not a sign of a problem.

### Should I make my child share their toys?

Encouraging is helpful; forcing usually isn't. Let your child finish their turn, use a timer if it helps, and let them keep a few special things put away. When sharing is safe and their choice, children tend to do it more often.

### How do I explain generosity to a three-year-old?

Keep it simple and concrete: "Generous means giving something of yours to make someone else happy." Then point it out when it happens: "You gave Mia a cracker. That was generous."

### Should I reward my child for sharing?

It's better to notice and describe it than to pay for it. One study found that toddlers who got a toy as a reward for helping were later less likely to help than those who got praise or nothing. A warm "Look how happy you made her" is reward enough.

### Is The Spring That Wanted to Stay right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The spring, the tree and the valley animals are easy to follow, and the change from a happy flowing spring to a still, lonely pond is clear in the pictures. Five- to seven-year-olds can also talk about the bigger ideas: why holding on made Nura lonely, and how the water cycle means nothing given is really lost.
