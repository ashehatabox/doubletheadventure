Teaching resilience to preschoolers can sound like teaching them to be tough. It isn't. For a child of 3 to 7, resilience is something smaller and kinder: falling down, feeling the disappointment, and then getting up to try again, a little wiser than before. In this article you'll find what resilience really means at this age, what research says actually helps it grow, how a little mountain goat in our picture book *Eight Times Up* shows it (without anyone pushing her), and a set of simple, everyday ways to practise it at home.

## What resilience means for a three- to seven-year-old

The American Psychological Association describes resilience as "the ability to adapt well to adversity, trauma, tragedy, threats, or even significant sources of stress." That's the grown-up version. For a young child, the "adversity" is usually much smaller: a tower that keeps falling, a zip that won't zip, a bike that tips over, a drawing that doesn't look like the picture in their head.

Those small moments are where resilience starts. For a preschooler, it has a few parts:

- **Feeling it.** Being allowed to be sad, cross or embarrassed when something goes wrong. Resilience doesn't mean not caring.
- **Stopping and resting.** Calming down enough to think again. Sometimes that takes a hug, a snack or a few minutes away.
- **Noticing what happened.** "The tower fell because the big blocks were on top." This is the step that turns a fall into a lesson.
- **Choosing to try again.** Not because someone says so, but because the child still wants the thing they were reaching for.

That last word matters: *choosing*. A resilient child isn't one who never gives up on anything. It's one who knows that a fall is not the end of the story, and that getting up is something they can do.

## Why resilience matters so much at this age

### It is built, not born

It's tempting to think some children are just "the persistent type" and others aren't. Temperament does play a part. Zero to Three, a non-profit focused on early childhood, describes how some children are easily frustrated and give up quickly, while others keep trying and are slower to get upset. But researchers at Harvard's Center on the Developing Child are clear that resilience is not a fixed trait. A summary of their work by the Harvard Graduate School of Education explains that resilience can be built, and that it grows from a mix of a child's own disposition and the experiences around them.

### One steady grown-up makes a huge difference

The same research points to one ingredient above all others. In the words of the Harvard summary, "every child who winds up doing well has had at least one stable and committed relationship with a supportive adult." Resilience, it says, depends on supportive, responsive relationships and on mastering a set of skills, such as planning, managing feelings and adapting to change.

That's good news for parents: you don't need a special program. The everyday back-and-forth of noticing, responding, comforting and encouraging builds the foundation. Harvard's researchers call this "serve and return": the child reaches out, and the adult responds.

### Small, manageable struggles are useful

The research also suggests that learning to cope with manageable challenges, with support close by, helps build resilience. This is one reason we don't need to rescue young children from every frustration. A puzzle that's a little too hard, with you sitting nearby, is a safe place to practise. (Big, overwhelming hardship is different, and children going through it need much more support. If you are worried about how your child is coping with something serious, talk to their doctor or educator.)

## The story: a little goat and a toy that always pops back up

*Eight Times Up* is book 15 in our *Double the Adventure* series. It takes place in the snowy mountains of Japan, from deep winter to spring. Its hero is Yuki, a young mountain goat with a white star on her forehead, who dreams of climbing Cloud Rock to watch the sunrise the way the big goats do.

Her friend Taro, a snow monkey who loves hot springs, carries a little red roly-poly toy everywhere. Whenever it tips over, it rocks and rocks and rights itself. That toy, and the sound it makes, becomes the book's refrain: "Wobble, wobble... POP!"

On her very first try, Yuki slips on a shiny rock and tumbles into soft snow. She wonders whether she just isn't a climber. Then she watches the toy pop back up, and she notices something: the fall taught her about shiny rocks. She tries again. And falls again. By sunset she has fallen four times, and here's the part we love most: she rests. Only then does she count what each fall taught her.

Day after day she practises, getting a little higher each time. Along the way she comforts a ptarmigan chick named Hina, who is crying after her own tumble, and shares what she has learned. Then comes the big climb, a gust of wind, and the biggest fall of all. Without giving too much away: Yuki gets up an eighth time, uses everything her falls have taught her, and sees the sunrise from Cloud Rock.

The book's theme line is a Japanese saying, put simply: "Fall seven times, get up eight." You'll find the book here: [Eight Times Up](/books/eight-times-up).

## What the story shows about resilience (and what it avoids)

We wrote this book carefully, because some messages about "never giving up" can do more harm than good for small children. Here's what we tried to show, and why.

### Falls are information, not verdicts

After her first fall, Yuki thinks the fall says something about *her*: maybe she's not a climber. The turning point is when she starts to see falls as saying something about the *climb*: shiny rocks slip, this path is steep, the wind comes from that side. That shift, from "I'm bad at this" to "now I know something," is the heart of resilience for young children.

### Resting is allowed

Yuki doesn't push through exhaustion. After four falls, she stops. She rests. Only then does she look back on what happened. Children need to hear that taking a break is part of trying again, not the opposite of it.

### Nobody gets hurt, and nobody has to prove anything

Every fall in the book lands in soft snow. This is a story about trying, not about pushing through pain. The big goats never coach or praise Yuki, and Taro doesn't solve the climb for her. She isn't climbing to impress anyone; she wants to see the sunrise, and the learning is hers.

### She still falls at the end

By spring, Yuki is a good climber, and she still falls sometimes. And she laughs. Resilience doesn't mean you stop falling. It means falling stops being scary.

## How to practise resilience at home

Here are habits that work with real three- to seven-year-olds, drawn from Zero to Three, the American Psychological Association and Harvard's Center on the Developing Child.

### Name the feeling first

Before any lesson, acknowledge the disappointment. "That was really frustrating. You worked so hard on that tower." Zero to Three suggests acknowledging how hard a task feels before coaching a child toward a solution. A child who feels understood calms down faster, and a calm child can think.

### Ask "What did that fall teach you?"

This is Yuki's question, and it works beautifully with young children. After the bike tips, the tower tumbles or the puzzle piece won't fit, wonder out loud together: "What happened? What could you try next time?" Keep it light. The goal isn't a lecture; it's noticing.

### Praise the trying, not the child

A well-known study led by Elizabeth Gunderson, with Carol Dweck and colleagues, filmed 53 families at home when their children were 1, 2 and 3 years old. The researchers counted how parents praised. Praise for effort, strategies and actions ("you worked hard on that") is called process praise. Praise for a fixed quality ("you're so smart") is called person praise. When the children were 7 or 8, those whose parents had used a higher share of process praise were more likely to enjoy challenges and to believe they could get better at things. The total amount of praise didn't matter; the kind did.

In practice: "You kept trying different ways!" says more than "You're so clever!"

### Break it into steps

Zero to Three recommends breaking a hard task into manageable steps, and the APA suggests setting reasonable goals and working toward them one step at a time. Yuki doesn't reach Cloud Rock on day one; she gets a little higher each day. Try the same with shoelaces, swimming or writing a name: celebrate the next step, not just the finish line.

### Coach, don't take over

When we do the task for them, children miss the moment of getting there themselves. Sit close, offer a hint ("What if you tried the corner pieces first?"), and let them do the doing. A silly "Wobble, wobble... POP!" as they get back up can ease the tension too; Zero to Three suggests humour for exactly this.

### Remind them of past successes

The APA suggests reminding children of times they've already managed something hard: "Remember the monkey bars? You practised and practised."

### Let them see you fall, too

Tell your child about something you're learning and getting wrong, like a recipe that flopped, and say what you'll try next. Children learn more from watching us recover than from hearing about it.

### Make room for rest

When a child is overwhelmed, the most resilient move may be a break. "Let's stop, have a snack, and come back to it." Like Yuki, they can count their lessons after they've rested.

## Share it with your child

A few things you can say or do right after reading:

- **Ask how Yuki felt.** "How did Yuki feel after her first fall?" Let your child name it: sad, embarrassed, cross. All of those are okay.
- **Count the lessons.** "What did each fall teach her?" Go back through the pages together and find what she learned each time.
- **Talk about the rest.** "Why do you think Yuki rested before trying again?" Help your child see that resting is part of trying.
- **Remember Hina.** "How did Yuki help Hina?" Ask if your child could share something they've learned with a younger friend or sibling.
- **Make it personal.** "What's something you're learning that makes you fall sometimes?" Then share one of yours.
- **Do the refrain.** Crouch down low, wobble side to side, and spring up on "POP!" It's a fun way to practise getting back up, and a silly signal to use the next time something tips over.

If your child likes stories about brave tries, *Pip the Brave Parrot* is a good next read, and our article on [teaching courage to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-courage-to-preschoolers-pip-the-brave-parrot) looks at the courage it takes to try something hard in the first place.

## Where the value comes from

Every book in our series starts from one value, and this one draws on a Japanese proverb, *nana korobi ya oki*, often translated as "fall down seven times, get up eight." The saying has long been linked with Japan's self-righting folk toys, such as the round, bottom-weighted Daruma doll, which rocks back upright when you tip it, and the small *okiagari-koboshi* of the Aizu region, which has been made for centuries. Taro's red roly-poly toy in the story is a nod to them. The heroes' names come from Japanese too: Yuki means "snow" and Hina means "chick," while Taro is a classic boy's name. The story itself is a simple, secular tale of animals learning a life lesson, and uses no Japanese words.

## Questions parents ask

### At what age can you start teaching resilience?

Much earlier than you might think. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes how the everyday "serve and return" between babies and caregivers lays the foundation for the skills behind resilience. The study by Gunderson and colleagues found that the kind of praise parents gave children between ages 1 and 3 was linked to how those children approached challenges at 7 and 8. With 3- to 7-year-olds, you can start talking about it directly, using stories like this one.

### My child gives up the moment something is hard. Is that normal?

Yes, very often. Some children are naturally more easily frustrated than others, and frustration tolerance grows slowly. Name the feeling, break the task into smaller steps, and offer challenges that are hard but achievable. If you have real worries about your child's behaviour or development, talk to their doctor or educator.

### Should I let my child fail?

Small, safe failures are some of the best teachers, especially with a caring grown-up nearby. That doesn't mean leaving a child alone with something far too hard or unsafe. Think of Yuki's soft snow: the falls are real, but the landing is safe. Stay close, comfort, and help them notice what they learned.

### Is telling my child "never give up" a good idea?

It can backfire. Young children may hear it as "you must keep going even when you're tired or hurt." A gentler message is closer to the book's: falling is normal, resting is allowed, and getting up again is your choice. Sometimes a resilient choice is to stop, rest, and try another day, or try a different way.

### Is Eight Times Up right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The refrain is fun to join in with, the falls are gentle and easy to follow, and counting them is a game in itself. Older children (5 to 7) enjoy the bigger ideas: what each fall taught Yuki, why she rested, and how she helped Hina.
