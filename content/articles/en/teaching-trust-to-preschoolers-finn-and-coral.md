Teaching trust to preschoolers sounds like a big, serious job, but most of it happens in tiny moments: "I'll be right back," "We'll read one more page after your bath," "I'll hold your hand at the crossing." Each time we do what we said, a child learns that words can be counted on. And each time a promise slips, whether it's ours or a big sibling's, a child learns something too. In this article we look at what trust means for a three- to seven-year-old, what research tells us about how young children decide who to rely on, how our picture book *Finn & Coral* shows a broken promise and the slow work of mending it, and practical ways to build trust, and rebuild it, at home.

## What trust means to a three- to seven-year-old

For grown-ups, trust is a feeling about someone's character. For young children it is much more concrete. Trust is:

- knowing that when Mom says she'll pick you up after snack time, she will;
- knowing that a big brother who says "stay with me" won't run off;
- knowing that if you tell a friend a secret about your favourite toy, they won't laugh at it in front of everyone.

The idea at the heart of *Finn & Coral* is one we wanted a small child to be able to hold in one hand: when we promise something, people count on us. If we break a promise, we can earn trust back by keeping our word again and again.

That sentence has two halves, and both matter. The first is about being someone others can rely on. The second is about what to do when we get it wrong, which every child (and every parent) eventually will.

### Trust is built by what we do, not only by what we say

The book's theme line says it simply: "Trust wasn't just about promises. It was about showing up for each other, no matter what."

This is worth pausing on with young children, because they often think that saying sorry, or making a new promise, should fix things instantly. Trust doesn't work like that. It grows from many small actions over time. A child who understands this has learned something useful for every friendship they will ever have.

## Why trust matters so much in the early years

Young children depend on other people for almost everything, so working out who is reliable is one of the most practical things their minds do.

### Reliable grown-ups help children feel safe

The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University describes the back-and-forth between young children and the adults who care for them as "serve and return": a child reaches out with a sound, a look or a question, and the adult responds. Jack Shonkoff, the center's director, explains that the brain is "wired to expect" this kind of back-and-forth, and that it's the way the brain builds its circuits. When a child reaches out and nobody answers, the body's stress system switches on.

Trust, at its simplest, is a child's growing confidence that when they reach out, someone will be there.

### Children change their choices based on who keeps promises

One of the clearest studies on this comes from the University of Rochester. Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri and Richard Aslin revisited the famous "marshmallow test," in which a child can eat one treat now or wait to get two. Before the test, 28 children aged about three and a half to almost six did an art activity with a grown-up who promised to bring better art supplies if they waited.

For half the children, the grown-up came back empty-handed, twice. For the other half, the grown-up came back with the promised supplies. Then came the marshmallow. Children whose grown-up had broken promises waited about three minutes on average. Children whose grown-up had kept promises waited about twelve minutes. The study was published in the journal *Cognition* (2013).

The researchers' point was that waiting is a sensible choice only if you believe the reward will actually come. Young children are quick to notice who keeps their word, and they adjust what they do. That's a strong reason to keep our small promises, and to make only the ones we can keep.

### Young children already understand that a promise is special

At the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Germany, Patricia Kanngiesser, Bahar Köymen and Michael Tomasello studied 278 three- and five-year-olds. In one game, a puppet promised to share stickers and then kept them all. Children protested, and some of them pointed to the promise itself, saying in their own words that the puppet was supposed to share. In other games, children who had promised to keep helping tidy up stuck with the job longer, even when something more fun was on offer, than children who hadn't promised. The effect was stronger at five than at three. The study, published in the *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* (2017), is titled "Young children mostly keep, and expect others to keep, their promises."

So by preschool, children already feel that a promise creates a duty. They also feel it sharply when someone else breaks one, which is exactly where Finn is in our story.

## How Finn & Coral shows trust

*Finn & Coral* is the eighth book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and its value is trust. You can find it here: [Finn & Coral](/books/finn-and-coral).

Finn is a young fish still learning to swim in the ocean currents. He adores his big sister Coral, who is fast and strong and knows every hiding place on the reef. One morning they set off to explore somewhere new, and Coral promises to stay close.

### A promise broken by excitement, not unkindness

We were careful with Coral. She isn't a bad sister. She gets carried away, the way any excited child might, and she races ahead. Finn is left alone in open water, calling for her. When a big shadow passes overhead, he is frightened. It turns out to be a curious dolphin, who is kind and tells him to stay calm because his sister will come back.

This matters for the conversation you'll have with your child. Most broken promises in a young child's life don't come from someone mean. They come from someone who forgot, got distracted, or didn't think. Children can learn that a person who let them down can still love them, and that the hurt is still real.

### Finn is allowed to be hurt

When Coral comes rushing back, Finn tells her he doesn't trust her any more. She feels awful. And then, for several days, Finn keeps his distance.

We chose not to rush this. Many stories end a conflict with a quick "sorry" and a hug. In *Finn & Coral*, the hurt child is not pushed to forgive straight away. His feelings are treated as reasonable, because they are.

### Trust earned back, day by day

A small errand gives the two fish a second chance, and Coral invites Finn to come along and promises again to stay by his side. What happens next isn't a single big gesture. It's the quiet repetition of keeping her word, one day after another. We'll let your child see how it ends, but the clue is in the theme line: trust comes back through showing up.

### A gentle note about the dolphin

In real oceans, dolphins eat fish, so a dolphin comforting a small fish is a friendly story bend. If your child knows this, it can be fun to talk about: "Do you think a real dolphin would be so kind to a little fish?"

## Teaching trust to preschoolers: ideas for home

You don't need special lessons. Trust grows from the ordinary rhythm of the day. Pick one or two ideas that fit your family.

### Make small promises, and keep them

"After I finish this email, I'll come and see your tower." Then do it, and say so: "I said I'd come, and here I am." Naming the kept promise helps your child notice the pattern.

### Only promise what you can keep

It's tempting to say "We'll definitely go to the beach tomorrow" to end a whine. If rain might cancel it, try "We're hoping to go to the beach tomorrow. If it rains, we'll make a beach picnic in the living room instead." Children cope well with honest uncertainty; they cope less well with promises that keep falling through. For more on swapping little fibs for honest choices, see our article on [teaching honesty to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-honesty-to-preschoolers-the-honest-little-raccoon).

### When you break a promise, own it

You will forget sometimes. When you do, say it plainly: "I promised we'd play after lunch, and I didn't. That wasn't fair to you. I'm sorry." Then, if you can, set a new time and keep it. Your child sees that grown-ups make mistakes too, and learns what repair looks like.

### Don't force instant trust

If a sibling or friend has let your child down, it's natural to want everyone to make up quickly. Try to give the hurt child a little room, as Finn has in the story. You might say: "You felt scared when your brother ran ahead. It makes sense that you're not sure about playing with him right now." Then help the other child show, not just say, that they've changed.

### Coach the one who broke the promise

A child who broke a promise often feels bad, as Coral does, and may not know what to do with that feeling. Help them think of something concrete: "What could you do tomorrow so your sister knows she can count on you?" A plan turns guilt into action.

### Give big siblings a fair share of responsibility

Older children often love being trusted to look after a younger one, but four or five years old is still very young. Keep jobs small and safe, like holding hands on the walk to the car, and keep a grown-up close. When the older child manages it, notice it out loud: "You stayed right next to your sister the whole way. She could count on you."

### Keep a simple routine

Predictable rhythms, such as the same bedtime steps or the same goodbye at the school door, are a form of promise kept every day. They give young children a steady sense that the world makes sense and the people in it will be there.

## Share it with your child

Things to say or do after reading *Finn & Coral* together:

- **Remember the promise.** "What did Coral promise Finn at the start?" Let your child find the answer in the pictures.
- **Name Finn's feelings.** "How did Finn feel when he was alone and saw the big shadow?" Help your child find words like scared, lonely, worried, surprised.
- **Talk about the waiting.** "Why didn't Finn trust Coral straight away when she came back?" There's no single right answer. Some children will say "because she might do it again," which is a wise thing to notice.
- **Look for the repair.** "What did Coral do to earn Finn's trust again?" Point out that it took more than one day.
- **Say the theme line together.** "Trust wasn't just about promises. It was about showing up for each other, no matter what." Ask: "What does showing up mean?"
- **Make a promise for this week.** "What is a promise you can keep for someone this week?" Help your child choose something small and doable, like feeding the cat with you every morning or sharing a toy with a sibling at bath time. At the end of the week, celebrate it together.
- **Play "Stay with me."** Hold hands and swim like fish around the living room. One of you is the big sister or brother who has to stay close, the other calls out "Current!" or "Shadow!" and the big sibling stays right beside them. Swap roles.

## Questions parents ask

### At what age do children understand promises?

Research from the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology found that by about three, many children already treat a promise as something that creates a duty, and they protest when someone breaks one. Five-year-olds were more consistent than three-year-olds at keeping their own promises, so expect this skill to grow through the preschool years.

### Does breaking a small promise really matter?

One forgotten promise won't harm your child. But a University of Rochester study found that young children who saw a grown-up break promises waited much less time for a reward afterwards than children who saw promises kept. Children notice patterns. Keeping most of your small promises, and owning the ones you miss, teaches them that your word means something.

### How do I help my child trust a sibling again after a fight?

Let your child have their feelings first, without pushing them to make up straight away. Then help the sibling who let them down choose a small, concrete thing to do, and to keep doing it. Trust usually comes back the way it does in *Finn & Coral*: slowly, through actions.

### My child doesn't trust easily. Should I worry?

Many children are cautious with new people or after a hurt, and that's often healthy. Give them time, keep your own routines steady, and keep your promises small and reliable. If you're worried that your child seems very fearful, withdrawn, or unable to settle with familiar people, talk to your child's doctor or educator.

### Is Finn & Coral right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The bright reef, the frightening shadow and the kind dolphin are easy for young children to follow. Three-year-olds enjoy the adventure and the feelings; five- to seven-year-olds can talk about the harder part: why saying sorry wasn't enough, and what it really took for Coral to be trusted again.
