Looking for raccoon facts for kids? After meeting Riley, the curious young raccoon in our picture book *The Honest Little Raccoon*, many children want to know what real raccoons are like. Do they really wear a mask? Do they wash their food? Why do they turn up in city alleys and on rooftops? In this article we follow the real raccoon from its forest beginnings to its life in our cities, look at the wise old pigeon who gives Riley his big piece of advice, point out where the story playfully bends nature, and finish with simple things to share with a 3 to 7 year old, including what to do when your family meets a raccoon after dark.

## Meet the real raccoon

The raccoon in the story is the **common raccoon** (*Procyon lotor*), the one most families in Canada and the United States know. Hinterland Who's Who, the wildlife series from the Canadian Wildlife Federation and Environment and Climate Change Canada, describes it as usually a grizzled grey, with a tail marked by five to ten alternating dark and lighter rings. An adult measures about 80 centimetres from nose to tail tip, and males are generally bigger than females.

Then there's the face. The black mask across the eyes is the raccoon's best-known feature, and it's the first thing children point to in the pictures. Hinterland Who's Who adds a detail children enjoy: baby raccoons already have their little masks by about ten days old.

### A name that comes from its hands

Here's a fact that fits a clever character like Riley. According to Hinterland Who's Who, the English word *raccoon* comes from an Algonquian word, *arakun*, which it translates as "he scratches with his hand." The scientific name, *lotor*, means "the washer," which brings us to one of the best-loved raccoon facts of all. We'll come back to that in a moment.

### Where raccoons live

Animal Diversity Web, from the University of Michigan, says raccoons are found across southern Canada, through most of the United States and south into Central America and northern South America. People also brought them to parts of Europe and Asia, where they now live in the wild too.

What does a raccoon need? Surprisingly little: water nearby, food, and a safe place for a den. Their favourite wild homes are wet woodlands, swamps, marshes and farmland, but Hinterland Who's Who notes they're also very common in many North American cities, which is exactly where Riley's story takes place.

## Clever paws: the raccoon's superpower

If you only share one fact with your child, make it this one. A raccoon's front paws look a little like small human hands, with five slim fingers. Animal Diversity Web says these paws make raccoons "unusually dexterous" and that their sense of touch is highly developed. Raccoons usually pick food up with their front paws before they eat it, and they use those paws to feel for food, pry things open and climb.

That's why the story's Riley is so nimble on rooftops and windowsills. Real raccoons are excellent climbers too. Animal Diversity Web reports that they climb with great agility, can run at about 24 kilometres an hour when they need to, and are strong (if reluctant) swimmers.

### Do raccoons really wash their food?

This is the question every child asks, and the answer is "not exactly." Raccoons are often seen dipping and rubbing their food in water, which is how they got the name "the washer." But Hinterland Who's Who explains that this habit is probably linked to how raccoons find and catch food in water, such as crayfish, by feeling for it with their sensitive paws. It also notes that raccoons in captivity have been seen going through the "washing" movements even when there's no water at all. So the raccoon isn't really cleaning its dinner. It's exploring it with its fingers.

### How smart are raccoons?

Very curious, at least. In 2017, scientists led by Lauren Stanton at the University of Wyoming gave raccoons a tall tube of water with a piece of marshmallow floating out of reach, a puzzle based on the old fable of the thirsty crow who drops stones into a jug. Two raccoons learned to drop stones in to bring the treat up. A third invented her own answer: she tipped the whole heavy tube over. The raccoons didn't always pick the best objects to drop in, so how much they really understand is still an open question, which makes it a lovely "scientists are still finding out" fact.

## Raccoon facts for kids: what do raccoons eat?

Raccoons are **omnivores**, which means they eat both plants and animals. Hinterland Who's Who says they'll eat practically anything, but their favourites are corn, crayfish, fruit and nuts. In spring they eat mostly insects, worms, snails, crayfish and eggs; in summer, more berries and fruit along with frogs and grasshoppers; in autumn, corn, acorns, nuts and grapes.

In cities, they add whatever people leave around. The City of Toronto notes that raccoons will eat garbage, food waste and pet food left outside, and that they dig up lawns after rain, rolling back the grass to get at grubs underneath.

So when the smell of fresh bread pulls Riley and Benny towards a bakery window, the story is drawing on something real. What it doesn't do is celebrate it: climbing into the bakery is the mistake Riley has to own, and that's worth saying out loud if your child thinks it looks like fun.

### Do raccoons hibernate?

No. Hinterland Who's Who explains that in the north, raccoons spend the autumn eating as much as they can; by late fall about half a raccoon's weight can be fat. In the coldest weather they sleep for long stretches in a den, living off that fat, but their body temperature doesn't drop like a true hibernator's, and on mild nights they come out again. The story's chilly evening is a believable time to meet a hungry raccoon.

### Raccoons in the city

Riley lives among alleys, rooftops and streetlights, and real raccoons have become remarkably good at city life. Hinterland Who's Who notes that city raccoons can get by in a much smaller area than country raccoons, and that there can be far more of them in one place: as many as 100 per square kilometre have been recorded in some urban areas.

Where do they sleep? In the wild, raccoons like hollow trees, logs, caves and old burrows. In cities, Hinterland Who's Who lists chimneys, sewers, garages, attics, trees and culverts, and the City of Toronto adds the space under decks.

And they're mostly out at night. Animal Diversity Web describes raccoons as nocturnal, with excellent night vision and sharp hearing, which is why Riley's adventures happen after dark.

### Best friends, or mostly alone?

In the story, Riley's best friend Benny is big, strong and protective, and the two roam together. Real adult raccoons are mostly **solitary**. Animal Diversity Web says the only real social group raccoons form is a mother and her young. Hinterland Who's Who describes those families as quite sociable: the mother and her babies, called **kits**, search for food together at night and sleep together by day, and she teaches them to climb, find food and swim. The family usually stays together until the next spring. So best-friend raccoons are a sweet story invention.

## Maggie the pigeon: the real city bird

Maggie, the calm old bird who gives Riley the words he'll need, is a **pigeon**, most likely a rock pigeon (*Columba livia*), the pigeon of city streets all over the world. The Cornell Lab of Ornithology's All About Birds says rock pigeons were brought to North America from Europe in the early 1600s, and that city pigeons nest on buildings and window ledges. In the countryside they also use barns, bridges and natural cliffs, which is where their wild ancestors lived.

Here are some pigeon facts children love, all from All About Birds:

- **They always find their way home.** Pigeons can find their way back even when released far away. Scientists think they use the Earth's magnetic field, the position of the sun, and perhaps sound and smell.
- **They're very old friends of people.** Ancient writings from Mesopotamia and Egypt suggest people kept pigeons more than 5,000 years ago.
- **They come in many colours.** Look for the usual blue-grey bird with two dark bars on each wing, but also spotted "checker" birds, rusty-red ones, all-dark ones and white-splotched ones.

There's one gentle story bend here. Pigeons are daytime birds that roost at night, so a wise pigeon awake for an evening chat with a raccoon is the story's invention. It's a good one, though: pigeons and raccoons really are two of the most familiar city neighbours.

## Story or real? How the book bends the raccoon

Here's a quick guide for when your child asks, "Is that what real raccoons do?"

- **Real:** raccoons live in cities, climb rooftops and windowsills, come out at night, and are drawn to the smell of food.
- **Real:** raccoons have clever, sensitive front paws and a black mask, and a ringed tail long enough to knock things over.
- **Real:** autumn and early winter are hungry times, when raccoons are busy eating before the coldest weeks.
- **Story bend:** adult raccoons mostly live alone, so best-friend raccoons are an invention.
- **Story bend:** pigeons sleep at night, so Maggie's evening wisdom is a storybook choice.
- **Story bend:** real raccoons don't decide whether to tell the truth, apologise or explain themselves. The book borrows a raccoon, an animal famous for its "mask" and its sneaky reputation, to talk about something only people (and storybook animals) do: owning a mistake. As Maggie tells Riley, "Truth will always set you free." There's more on that side of the book in our article on [teaching honesty to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-honesty-to-preschoolers-the-honest-little-raccoon).

With older children, it's worth adding that real raccoons aren't "bandits" when they look for food; they're just being raccoons. Riley is different: he's a storybook character who knows the bakery isn't his.

## Share it with your child

Here are some easy ways to bring these raccoon facts into your reading time.

- **Play "raccoon fingers."** Put a few safe, everyday objects in a cloth bag or under a towel: a spoon, a sock, a block, a pine cone. Your child reaches in without looking and guesses each one by touch, just as a raccoon feels for crayfish. For children under 3, use only objects too big to fit in the mouth.
- **Make a mask.** Cut a raccoon mask from a paper plate (the grown-up does the cutting), and let your child colour the black patches around the eyes. Talk about how real raccoons have their masks from when they're tiny babies.
- **Count the rings.** Draw a long tail together and add stripes. Real raccoon tails have about five to ten dark rings. How many does Riley have in the pictures?
- **Ask "story or real?"** After reading, ask: "Do real raccoons have best friends? Do pigeons stay up at night? Do raccoons really have clever hands?" Children love catching the made-up parts.
- **Talk about Riley's choice.** "Riley could have run away. What did he do instead? How do you think he felt afterwards?" The book's theme line says it simply: "Lying only traps you in more lies. But the truth? It set me free."
- **Spot city pigeons.** On your next walk, look for pigeons on ledges and count how many colours you see.

### If your family sees a raccoon

Raccoons live in most Canadian and American cities, so a sighting is likely. The City of Toronto gives clear rules that work well for children: **never feed raccoons, and never approach or touch them, even if they seem tame, sick or hurt.** Watch from a distance and let the raccoon go on its way; healthy raccoons would rather run off than face you, and they only become aggressive when cornered.

The story's kind baker hopes to help the raccoons, but in real life, feeding wild animals isn't kind. It teaches them to come close to people and homes. Instead, keep pet food indoors, keep bins closed, and, as Toronto suggests, store your green bin in a garage or shed and put it out close to collection time.

A few safety points for grown-ups. Raccoons can carry rabies, and Toronto advises seeing a doctor right away if anyone is bitten or scratched. Hinterland Who's Who also warns that raccoon droppings can carry a roundworm that is dangerous to people, especially children, so children should never touch raccoon droppings, and anyone cleaning them up should wear gloves and a mask and wash hands well afterwards. If you see a raccoon that seems sick, confused or very young and alone, call your local animal services (in Toronto, 311) rather than going near it.

## Questions parents ask

### What do raccoons eat?

Raccoons are omnivores. They eat insects, worms, crayfish, frogs, eggs, fruit, berries, nuts and corn, depending on the season. In cities they also eat garbage, food waste and pet food left outside, which is why bins should stay closed.

### Are raccoons nocturnal?

Yes. Raccoons are mostly active at night and sleep during the day in a den. You may occasionally see a healthy raccoon in daylight, during breeding season or if it has been disturbed, but one that seems confused or sick should be avoided and reported.

### Why do raccoons "wash" their food?

They aren't really cleaning it. Raccoons have very sensitive front paws, and dipping and rubbing food is probably linked to how they feel for food in water, such as crayfish. Raccoons have even been seen making the same movements with no water around.

### Is it safe for my child to get close to a raccoon?

No. Watch quietly from a distance and never feed or touch a raccoon, even one that seems friendly. Raccoons can carry rabies and a roundworm that is harmful to people. If anyone is bitten or scratched, see a doctor right away.
