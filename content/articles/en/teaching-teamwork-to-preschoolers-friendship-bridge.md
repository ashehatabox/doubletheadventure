Teaching teamwork to preschoolers usually starts in the least promising place: an argument. Two children want to build the same tower, but one wants it tall and the other wants it wide. Within a minute someone is crying and the blocks are on the floor. It's tempting to see that as a failure of teamwork, but it is actually where teamwork begins. In this article we look at what teamwork really means for a three- to seven-year-old, what research tells us about how early children start to cooperate (it's earlier than you might think), how our picture book *Friendship Bridge* shows teamwork growing out of a real disagreement, and practical ways to help your child combine ideas with other people at home.

## What teamwork means to a three- to seven-year-old

For a young child, teamwork shows up in small, concrete moments:

- carrying a heavy basket of toys with a sibling, one handle each;
- building a sandcastle where one child digs the moat and the other fills buckets;
- agreeing on the rules of a made-up game before it starts;
- tidying up together so the park comes sooner;
- finishing a puzzle where each person does a corner.

All of these share one thing: the goal belongs to both children, and neither can reach it as well alone. That is the heart of teamwork, and it's the idea we put at the centre of *Friendship Bridge*. For a child, we say it this way: when we put our ideas together, we can do more than either of us could alone.

Teamwork is *not* one child doing whatever the louder child says, or always agreeing. Real teamwork includes different ideas, sometimes strongly held, and the work of turning them into one plan. That last part is a skill, and it needs practice.

## How early children start to cooperate

Parents of toddlers sometimes assume cooperation comes much later. Research from the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology suggests otherwise.

### Toddlers already treat partners as partners

In a 2012 study published in *Developmental Science*, Felix Warneken, Maria Gräfenhain and Michael Tomasello played simple joint games with toddlers aged 21 and 27 months. Partway through, the adult partner stopped. Sometimes the adult *couldn't* continue (for example, they accidentally dropped something); sometimes they simply *chose* to stop. The toddlers were more likely to encourage the partner to keep going, with gestures or words, when the adult was unable to continue than when they were unwilling. They did this even in games where they didn't need the adult to reach their own goal.

The researchers' conclusion is lovely for parents to hear: even before age two and a half, children don't treat a partner as a tool for getting what they want. They treat them as a partner, someone whose intentions matter.

### Working together changes how children share

A second Max Planck study, published in *Nature* in 2011 by Katharina Hamann, Felix Warneken, Julia Greenberg and Michael Tomasello, looked at pairs of two- and three-year-olds. To win some marbles, the children had to pull the two ends of a rope at the same time. The reward then arrived unevenly: three marbles for one child, one for the other.

Here's what they found. Three-year-olds, and to some degree two-year-olds, tended to even things out and share equally, but mainly when they had won the marbles by working together. When the same uneven reward simply fell into their laps, or when each child had pulled their own rope separately, they mostly kept what they got. The researchers suggest that one of children's earliest ideas of fairness may be just this: if we did it together, we split it fairly.

In other words, teamwork and fairness seem to grow side by side.

### Cooperation grows through the preschool years

The American Academy of Pediatrics notes on its parenting site, HealthyChildren.org, that three-year-olds become better at sharing and cooperating, learn to take turns in small groups, and can start to solve disputes by taking turns or trading toys, though grown-ups still need to encourage this at first. As children become more aware of other people's feelings, they gradually compete less and cooperate more when they play with friends.

So if your four-year-old still turns every game into a contest, that's normal. The ability is there early; the smooth version takes years of practice.

## Why disagreeing is part of teamwork

For young children, the hard part of teamwork usually isn't wanting to work together. It's what happens when two good ideas collide. In that moment a child has to hold their own idea while listening to someone else's, manage the frustration of not getting their way, and imagine that the other plan might have something good in it. That's a lot to ask of a four-year-old all at once.

That's why we didn't want a teamwork story where one character is right and the other learns to go along. We wanted both characters to have a good idea with a weak spot, so that children could see the real turning point: not giving in, but noticing what's good in someone else's plan.

## How Friendship Bridge shows teamwork

*Friendship Bridge* is the third book in our *Double the Adventure* series. Its subtitle is simply "A story about Teamwork".

Venny the beaver and Sammy the squirrel live beside a wide, rushing river. One morning they spot an enormous strawberry bush on the far bank, and they agree at once: they'll build a bridge. What they can't agree on is how. Venny, strong and practical, wants heavy, solid logs that will last. Sammy, quick and light on his feet, wants light branches so the bridge goes up fast. After a long argument, each friend stomps off to build his own.

You can probably guess how that goes. Venny's logs are too heavy to manage alone and float away on the current; Sammy's branches wobble and give way. Soaked and cross, the two friends start blaming each other. Then Sammy wonders whether they should try together, and something shifts: each of them starts to see what's good in the other's idea, and the weak spot in his own. From there, they find a way to use both plans at once, and the ending, which we'll leave for your child to discover, is a happy one with strawberries in it.

You can find the book here: [Friendship Bridge](/books/friendship-bridge).

### Neither friend is wrong

What we love most about Venny and Sammy is that the book never picks a winner. Logs really are strong; branches really are light and bendy. Each idea fails on its own for a real reason, and the bridge only works when both are part of it. For children, this is a gentle way into a big idea: *your idea can be good and still need someone else's.*

### The turn from blaming to noticing

The most important moment in the book is quiet. It's the point where the two friends, still dripping, stop saying "This is your fault" and start saying what was good about the other's plan. That shift, from blame to giving credit, is something children can practise in real life, and it's a moment worth pausing on when you read together.

### A real-world truth inside the story

The two ideas in the book fit the real animals nicely. Real beavers build dams and lodges from logs, branches, mud and stones, and use their flat tails for balance and swimming. Real tree squirrels build nests, called dreys, by weaving together twigs and leaves. So Venny thinks like a beaver and Sammy thinks like a squirrel, and the bridge is what you get when both ways of building meet.

The book's theme line sums it up: "Together, we can do more."

## Teaching teamwork to preschoolers: ideas for home

These ideas work with real children on ordinary days. Pick one or two to start.

### Give children jobs that truly need two

The Max Planck rope study is a good model: the children had to pull at the same time, or nothing happened. At home, look for tasks where two people really are needed. Carrying a laundry basket between you, holding a sheet while someone folds it, making a blanket fort where one holds and one clips. When the task genuinely needs both, cooperation stops being a lecture and becomes the only way.

### Coach the disagreement instead of ending it

When two children argue over how to do something, our instinct is to decide for them or split them up. Sometimes that's necessary, especially if anyone is getting hurt. But when it's safe, try staying close and coaching instead:

- "You both have a plan. Sam, tell me yours. Now Lily, tell me yours."
- "What's good about Lily's idea?"
- "Is there a way to use a bit of both?"

You don't need to find the answer for them. Often just slowing the moment down is enough for one of them to suggest something.

### Name what's good in each idea

Children learn to give credit by hearing grown-ups do it. "Your tower is strong at the bottom. Your sister's has a clever window. I wonder what would happen if you put them together." Over time, children start doing this for each other, which is exactly the move Venny and Sammy make in the book.

### Model it with your own partner or friends

Children watch how adults solve disagreements. When you and another grown-up disagree about something small, like what to make for dinner or where to go on Saturday, let your child hear you work it out kindly: "I wanted pasta, you wanted soup. How about soup tonight and pasta tomorrow?" It's one of the most powerful teamwork lessons there is, and it costs nothing.

### Share the reward fairly

After children complete something together, let them share the result: the cookies they baked, the stickers they earned, the first go on the new swing. The *Nature* study suggests children already lean toward splitting fairly after working together. You can support that instinct by talking about it: "You both carried the basket, so you both get to choose a story."

### Keep it light when it goes wrong

Teamwork is messy at this age. Games fall apart and forts collapse. Treat those moments the way the book does: everyone is a bit cross, and then you try again.

## Share it with your child

Some things to say or do after reading *Friendship Bridge* together:

- **Ask about the argument.** "Why couldn't Venny and Sammy agree at first?" Let your child explain both sides.
- **Find the good in both.** "What was good about Venny's idea? What was good about Sammy's?" If your child only picks one, ask about the other.
- **Talk about the wobbly bridges.** "What went wrong when they each built alone?" This helps children see that neither idea was enough by itself.
- **Bring it home.** "When have you and a friend had different ideas? What did you do?" Listen without fixing; just being heard matters.
- **Build your own bridge.** Use blocks, sticks and string, or cardboard and tape to build a small table-top bridge together, one of you with "heavy" pieces and one with "light" ones. See which design holds a toy car. (Keep this to the table or the sandbox; children should never build or cross makeshift bridges over real water.)
- **Plan a team job.** "What could we build or do together this week?" Let your child choose, and give them a real part in the plan.
- **Use the theme line.** When a joint project works, say it together: "Together, we can do more."

## Questions parents ask

### At what age do children start to work as a team?

Earlier than many parents expect. Studies from the Max Planck Institute found that toddlers around two years old already encourage a partner to keep going in a joint game, and that three-year-olds tend to share rewards fairly after working together. Smooth teamwork takes much longer: turn-taking and solving disputes grow through the preschool years, with lots of grown-up help at first.

### My child always wants to be in charge. Is that normal?

Yes, very common. Young children have strong ideas and are still learning that other people's ideas can be good too. Gentle coaching helps: ask them to explain their plan, then ask what's good about someone else's. If you have real worries about your child's behaviour or how they get on with others, talk to your child's doctor or educator.

### Should I step in when children argue during play?

Step in right away if anyone is being hurt, or about to be. Otherwise, try staying close and coaching rather than solving it for them: help each child say their idea, ask what's good in the other's, and wonder aloud whether there's a way to use both. Children learn more from finding a solution with support than from having one handed to them.

### How is teamwork different from just sharing?

Sharing is about dividing something that already exists, like toys or snacks. Teamwork is about making something together: a plan, a game, a tower, a bridge. The two are linked, though. Research suggests that young children are more likely to share fairly after they've worked together to earn a reward.

### Is Friendship Bridge right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The story is simple and easy to follow, the two friends are easy to tell apart, and the falling-down bridges are a moment small children enjoy. Older children, around 5 to 7, enjoy talking about why each idea failed alone and what each friend learned.
