Teaching forgiveness to preschoolers often begins with a grown-up saying two words: "Say sorry." The child mumbles it, the other child is told "Now say it's okay," and everyone moves on. It looks like forgiveness, but it usually isn't, and children can tell. In this article we look at what forgiveness really means for a three- to seven-year-old, what researchers have found about how young children understand apologies and forgiving (they understand more than we might think), how our picture book *The Fox Mistake* shows both halves of making up, and practical ways to help your child say a real sorry and forgive in their own time.

## What forgiveness means to a three- to seven-year-old

For a young child, forgiveness shows up in small, everyday moments:

- a brother knocks over a block tower by accident, and after a while his sister lets him help build it again;
- a friend grabs a toy, gives it back and says sorry, and the two go back to playing;
- a child blames a classmate for something, finds out they were wrong, and goes to put it right;
- a grown-up snaps at bedtime, apologises in the morning, and the child climbs into their lap.

At the heart of all of these is one simple idea, and it's the one we put at the centre of *The Fox Mistake*. For a child, we say it like this: everyone makes mistakes; when we're wrong, we say sorry, and when someone says sorry, we can forgive them.

Notice that this idea has two sides. Forgiveness isn't only about the child who was hurt. It also depends on the child who did the hurting: noticing what they did, owning it and trying to make it right. When we teach forgiveness, we're really teaching a little dance of repair between two people.

### What forgiveness is not

It helps to be clear, for ourselves first, about what forgiveness isn't. Suzanne Freedman, a professor of human development at the University of Northern Iowa who studies forgiveness education, writes for the Greater Good Science Center at UC Berkeley that forgiveness is different from forgetting, excusing or pretending something didn't happen. It is also voluntary and takes time. A child who says "It's okay" through tears hasn't forgiven yet; they've just learned the words that end the conversation.

For young children, the most useful version of this is: *forgiving doesn't mean it didn't hurt.* You can be hurt, say so, and still choose to be friends again.

## What research tells us about young children and forgiveness

Parents sometimes assume forgiveness is too abstract for a preschooler. Studies from the University of Virginia suggest children start to understand it surprisingly early.

### By five, children value forgiving

In one study, published in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* by psychologists Janine Oostenbroek and Amrisha Vaish, four- and five-year-olds watched short videos. In each, a girl accidentally broke a toy she had been admiring and apologised. Sometimes the toy's owner forgave her; sometimes the owner accepted the apology but stayed upset.

The five-year-olds were consistent: they preferred the forgiving character and judged the unforgiving one as "not nice." Four-year-olds' answers were more mixed, but children of both ages gave more of their flowers (a small gift in the study) to the character who forgave. As the Greater Good Science Center summarised it, the researchers concluded that children begin to see the social value of forgiveness by around four, and that it is well established by five.

### Remorse matters to children

In a related study in *Child Development*, described by UVA News, two adults accidentally tore a child's drawing. One showed that they were sorry; the other just acknowledged the tear without remorse. Between ages four and five, children began to treat the two adults differently, and by five they clearly preferred the one who was sorry.

This is worth knowing as a parent. Young children don't only notice *whether* someone said sorry. They notice whether the sorry seemed real.

### Forced apologies don't fool children

That brings us to the "Say sorry" moment. A 2018 study from the University of Michigan, led by Craig Smith and published in *Merrill-Palmer Quarterly*, asked children aged four to nine about different kinds of apologies between peers. Children rated willing apologies about the same whether or not an adult had prompted them. But coerced apologies were seen as less effective, especially by the older children, who tended to think a forced apologiser was mostly worried about getting in trouble. Children of all ages thought the hurt child felt better after a willing apology than after a forced one.

The takeaway isn't "never prompt an apology." It's that a sorry squeezed out of an unwilling child may not help either child, so it's often worth waiting until your child is calm and ready.

## Why apologising comes before forgiving

Many of us were raised on a quick script: "Say sorry." "Say it's okay." Done. The research above, and plenty of everyday experience, points to a slower and kinder order:

1. **Calm down first.** Nobody, child or grown-up, makes a good apology while still angry.
2. **Understand what happened.** Help the child who caused the hurt think about how the other person feels.
3. **Make a real sorry.** Name what you did, say it was wrong, and offer to fix it if you can.
4. **Let the hurt child say how they feel.** This is the step we most often skip.
5. **Forgive when ready.** Sometimes that's in a minute; sometimes it's after lunch. Both are fine.

Freedman's article gives similar advice, including not forcing closure: a hurt child can honestly say, "I'm still sad. I need some time." If children learn they must say "it's okay" the instant someone apologises, they learn to hide hurt, not to forgive.

## How The Fox Mistake shows forgiveness

*The Fox Mistake* is the fourth book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and its value is forgiveness. But as we wrote it, we realised it's equally a book about apologising, and about not jumping to conclusions.

Felix and Finn are best-friend foxes who live in dens side by side in an autumn forest. They race through the falling leaves and share everything. One day Felix finds a beautiful shiny pebble by the river and keeps it, proudly, in his den. The next morning it's gone. When he sees a shiny pebble outside Finn's den, he jumps to a conclusion and accuses his friend. Finn says he found his own, but Felix won't believe him. The two foxes turn away from each other, and for days they're both miserable and lonely.

Then Felix discovers something that shows him he was wrong. What he does next, and how Finn responds, is the heart of the book, and we'll let your child find the details as you read. You can find the book here: [The Fox Mistake](/books/the-fox-mistake).

### Nobody is the villain

Nobody in the story stole anything. Felix isn't a bad fox; he made the very ordinary mistake of being sure he was right. That matters, because most hurt between friends comes from someone getting something wrong, not from someone being cruel.

### A real apology

When Felix realises his mistake, he doesn't hide or hope it'll blow over. He goes to his friend, explains what happened, and says sorry for not believing him. That's a model apology for young children: it names exactly what he did wrong. "Sorry for not believing you" teaches far more than a general "sorry."

### Hurt first, then forgiveness

The moment we most hope parents pause on is quiet. Before Finn forgives, he tells Felix how much it hurt to be called a liar. Only then does he forgive. We kept that order on purpose. Children need to see that it's okay to say "That hurt me," and that saying it is part of making up, not the opposite of it.

The book's closing lesson puts it simply: "Mistakes could happen, but forgiveness made their friendship even stronger."

## Teaching forgiveness to preschoolers: ideas for home

These ideas are meant for ordinary days with real children. You don't need all of them; pick one or two that fit your family.

### Wait for calm before the sorry

When something goes wrong between children, start with safety and calm, not with the apology. Separate if you need to, breathe together, offer a hug or a quiet minute. A sorry made once everyone has settled is far more likely to be meant, and far more likely to be accepted.

### Help your child see the other side

Once your child is calm, ask simple questions: "How do you think your sister felt when the tower fell?" "What did her face look like?" You're not trying to make your child feel bad. You're helping them connect what they did with how someone else felt, which is what makes a sorry real.

### Teach a three-part sorry

A real apology has three parts children can learn:

- **What I did:** "I'm sorry I said you took my pebble."
- **Why it was wrong or how it felt:** "That wasn't true, and it made you sad."
- **How I'll fix it:** "Can I help you look for yours?" or "Next time I'll ask first."

For a three-year-old, just the first part is a fine start. Six- and seven-year-olds can often manage all three. And when a sorry is hard to say out loud, a drawing or a small kind act can be the start of one.

### Make room for the hurt child

After the sorry, turn to the child who was hurt. "Do you want to tell him how it felt?" Let them say it. Then, rather than "Say it's okay," try "Are you ready to play again, or do you need a little time?" Both answers are allowed. If they need time, reassure the child who apologised: "She heard you. Sometimes it takes a while to feel better."

### Model it yourself

The most powerful lesson is watching a grown-up do it. When you snap, rush or get something wrong with your child, apologise the same way: "I'm sorry I shouted this morning. You didn't deserve that. I'm going to try to take a breath next time." And when your child says sorry to you, take it seriously and forgive warmly. Children learn the whole dance by seeing it done.

## Share it with your child

Some things to say or do after reading *The Fox Mistake* together:

- **Ask about the pebble.** "Why did Felix think Finn took his pebble?" This opens a gentle talk about how easy it is to be sure and still be wrong.
- **Imagine Finn's feelings.** "How do you think Finn felt when Felix didn't believe him?" Let your child find the words: sad, cross, lonely.
- **Talk about the sorry.** "What did Felix do when he found out he was wrong?" Point out that he went straight to his friend and said exactly what he was sorry for.
- **Talk about the forgiving.** "Why do you think Finn forgave him?" There's no single right answer.
- **Practise with toys.** Act out a small mix-up with two soft toys, then have one say a three-part sorry and the other say how it felt before deciding to make up. Let your child play both parts.
- **Make a sorry card.** Next time your child wants to make up with someone, offer paper and crayons. A picture can say sorry when words feel too big.
- **Use the theme line.** After a real make-up at home, say it together: "Mistakes could happen, but forgiveness made their friendship even stronger."

## Questions parents ask

### At what age can children understand forgiveness?

Earlier than many parents think. University of Virginia studies found that by around four, children are starting to see the value of forgiving, and by five they clearly prefer someone who forgives and someone who is truly sorry. Understanding grows slowly through childhood, though, so expect lots of grown-up help along the way.

### Should I make my child say sorry?

Gently prompting is fine, but forcing a sorry from an angry child often backfires. A University of Michigan study found that children see coerced apologies as less effective than willing ones. It usually works better to help your child calm down, think about how the other person feels, and apologise when they're ready, even if that's a little later.

### My child won't forgive a friend or sibling. What should I do?

Let them have some time. Forgiveness is a choice, and it can't be rushed. Help them say how they feel, acknowledge that it hurt, and let them know they can decide later. If a child is often very upset after conflicts with others, or you're worried about how they get on with friends, talk to your child's doctor or educator.

### Does forgiving mean my child has to let it happen again?

No. Forgiving a friend for a mistake doesn't mean accepting being hurt over and over. Children can forgive and still say "Please don't do that again," and grown-ups should step in when one child is regularly hurting another.

### Is The Fox Mistake right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The story is simple, the two foxes are easy to follow, and the lost pebble is something small children understand right away. Younger children enjoy the friendship and the making up; children of five to seven enjoy talking about why Felix got it wrong and what he could have done differently.
