Teaching belonging to preschoolers has two halves, and most of us only think about the first one. We want our child to be welcomed: invited to play, chosen for the team, saved a seat at lunch. The second half is quieter and just as important: a child who knows how to make room for someone else. In this article we look at what belonging means for a child of three to seven, why being left out stings so much at that age, how our picture book *The Snug* tells the story of a little porcupine who stops waiting to be let in, and practical ways to grow both halves of belonging at home and in the classroom, with a few sentences you can share with your child.

## The value at the heart of *The Snug*

*The Snug* is the thirteenth book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and its value is belonging. The theme line puts it in one sentence: "If there's no room for you, build a place with room for everyone." You can find the book here: [The Snug](/books/the-snug).

For a young child, we wanted belonging to be something they could picture and almost feel: a warm, crowded place on a cold day, with your friends squeezed in beside you. For grown-ups, the idea goes a little further. Belonging is not only something other people give you. It is also something you can build yourself, without waiting for permission. And the real test comes afterwards, when it is your turn to decide who gets to come in.

We were careful about one thing. Many stories about being left out end with the outsider proving their worth, and so the group lets them in. We didn't want children to learn that you belong once you're useful. In *The Snug*, nobody earns a place. The door is open because the one who built it decides it should be.

## What belonging means for a 3 to 7 year old

Ontario's early years framework, [How Does Learning Happen?](https://www.ontario.ca/page/how-does-learning-happen-ontarios-pedagogy-early-years), names belonging as one of four foundations for children's learning and development, alongside well-being, engagement and expression. The framework describes belonging as a sense of connectedness to others and as making contributions as part of a group, a community and the natural world. It also describes these foundations as conditions that children naturally seek for themselves.

That two-part definition is useful for parents. Belonging is being connected (people know me and are glad I'm here) and contributing (I have something to add). A child who feels both tends to walk into a room differently.

At three to seven, belonging is very concrete. It looks like:

- someone saving you a spot on the carpet;
- your name on the cubby;
- being asked "Do you want to play?";
- having a job, like watering the class plant.

The American Academy of Pediatrics explains on HealthyChildren.org that [around age three, children start playing with other children](https://healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Social-Development-in-Preschoolers.aspx) rather than simply alongside them, and begin to form particular friendships. It adds that through friendships, children learn that they have qualities other people like, which supports their self-esteem. That is exactly the age when "You can't play with us" starts to happen, and exactly why it hurts.

## Why being left out stings so much

When four-year-olds tell another child "you're not playing," it is usually not cruelty. Young children are still learning how groups work: they protect a game they're enjoying, or copy what an older child said. A new face, or someone who plays a bit differently, can be the easiest one to leave out.

For the child on the outside, though, it can feel enormous. They often can't yet tell the difference between "this game is full right now" and "nobody likes me." That is the moment the book is built around.

In the story, Quilla is new in Bramble Hollow. Her family has just moved into an empty burrow as the first cold wind arrives. The village animals keep warm by huddling together under the Big Oak, and when Quilla asks to join, they shuffle away because her quills look prickly. She ends up alone at the edge of the meadow. Nobody is a villain; the others are a little unsure, and they move. Many children recognise that feeling straight away, from one side or the other.

## Two kinds of courage: crossing the meadow and building your own

The middle of *The Snug* shows two different brave choices, and both are worth talking about with your child.

### The friend who walks over

Bodie the badger loves nothing more than a cosy squeeze under the oak. He notices Quilla sitting alone. He is nervous, because if he goes over, the others might leave him out too. He crosses the meadow anyway and sits beside her.

This is the kind of belonging children can practise most often: noticing who is on the edge and going over. Bodie doesn't fix anything or tell anyone off. He just sits down. For a child, "Do you want to play with me?" is the same act. (If your child likes stories about being brave when you're scared, our article on [teaching courage to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-courage-to-preschoolers-pip-the-brave-parrot) goes deeper.)

### The child who stops waiting

The turning point belongs to Quilla. After a long look at the oak, she decides she has waited long enough to be invited. She and Bodie will build a warm place of their own, right where they are. Bodie digs, Quilla climbs for vines, and her own loose quills become the pins that hold the walls together. The refrain carries the work along: "Scoop, loop, snug!"

This moment gives a left-out child something to do with the feeling besides wait. Not "pretend you don't care," not "make them let you in," but: you can start something. A child who is turned away from the block corner can build a tower of their own, and very often, other children wander over because it looks like fun. That is what happens in the book, too, when a squirrel, a mouse and a mole join in.

## The real test: who gets to come in

When the hut is finished, somebody asks who it is for. Quilla remembers how it felt at the edge of the meadow, and she says it is for everyone. On the first snowy night, the whole village comes, including the rabbit twins who had turned her away. We'll leave the rest for you to read together.

This is the second half of belonging, and the hardest, for children and grown-ups alike. Once we have a warm place, it is tempting to guard it: the clubhouse with a password, the game only best friends can play. Quilla's choice shows another way.

Two details in the book matter here, and they are worth pointing out to older children.

- **The door is open before anyone says sorry.** The rabbits do apologise, and that matters, but the apology is a repair between friends, not a ticket in. Quilla had already decided the Snug was for everyone.
- **No grown-up sorts it out.** No elder steps in to tell the village to be nice. The change comes from two young animals and the choices they make.

There is a real classroom version of this idea. Vivian Paley, a kindergarten teacher, wrote a book called *You Can't Say You Can't Play* (Harvard University Press, 1992) about her concern with children being shut out of play. As educators described in [an ASCD article](https://ascd.org/el/articles/everyone-here-can-play), she raised the rule with her class and asked the children whether it would be fair and whether it would work, and other teachers later adopted the same rule in their own classrooms. You don't need to make it a household law, but it is a good question to ask children: would it be fair if nobody could say "you can't play"?

## Teaching belonging to preschoolers at home

Here are practical things to try. Pick one or two and let them become habits.

### Make home a place of belonging first

Small, steady signs help: a place for their things, a family job that really matters, a ritual that is "ours" (a Friday pancake breakfast, a goodnight phrase). The Ontario framework's idea of contributing to a group fits family life as well as school.

### Listen before you fix

When your child says "nobody played with me," the urge is to solve it straight away. The Child Mind Institute suggests, in its article on [helping kids who are lonely](https://childmind.org/article/how-to-help-kids-who-are-lonely/), listening before problem-solving: asking open questions, noticing out loud without judgment, and reflecting back what you hear, such as "It sounds like you're having a hard time." The same article suggests not pushing too hard if a child doesn't want to talk, and trying again a day or two later. Sharing a time you felt lonely yourself can make it easier to start.

### Practise the words

Some children want to join in and simply don't know how. Role-play is a good fit for this age. First 5 California suggests [practising social skills through role-play](https://www.first5california.com/en-us/articles/what-to-do-if-your-child-hasnt-made-friends-at-preschool/), acting out everyday scenes like sharing, taking turns and listening to a playmate. You can play both sides: the child asking to join, and the child who is asked. Try phrases like:

- "Can I play too?"
- "What are you building? Can I help?"
- "Do you want to play with me?"
- "There's room here. Come sit with us."

### Make a plan A and a plan B

The Child Mind Institute also suggests helping a child think through what to do if reaching out doesn't work. This is Quilla's idea in small form. Plan A: ask to join. Plan B: start something of your own nearby, or look for someone else who is on their own.

### Talk to the educator

If your child is often on the edge at daycare or school, First 5 California suggests asking the teacher how your child interacts with classmates; teachers can help by including a child in group activities or pairing them with a peer who has similar interests. A playdate with one classmate is often easier than a big group.

### Celebrate making room

When your child lets a sibling join a game or invites the new child to their table, say so, specifically: "You saw that she was on her own and you asked her to play. That was kind and brave."

## Share it with your child

Short, plain sentences you can read aloud or say in your own words. Use the ones that fit.

- "Everybody wants to belong somewhere. That's a feeling we all have."
- "If you feel left out, you can tell me. It's not your fault."
- "You don't have to wait to be invited. You can start something, and others might join."
- "When you have a game going, look around. Is there someone who would like to play too?"
- "Being the first to walk over takes courage. Little steps count."
- "There's always room for one more."

If your child enjoys the book's refrain, it can become a family shorthand. When someone makes room on the sofa or in a game, "Scoop, loop, snug!" says "you did it" without a lecture.

## Talking about the story after reading

There are no right answers. Follow your child's lead, and for a three-year-old, one question is plenty.

1. How do you think Quilla felt sitting at the edge of the meadow?
2. Why was it brave for Bodie to walk over and sit with her?
3. What did Quilla decide to do instead of waiting?
4. Why do you think she said the Snug was for everyone?
5. Is there someone who might like you to sit with them?

For a six- or seven-year-old, the fourth question opens a talk about how it feels when someone who left you out wants to join later, and why opening the door anyway is a strong choice.

## When to ask for more help

Every child has days when nobody wants to play, and friendships take time to form. As First 5 California puts it, every child meets milestones at their own pace. But if your child is often alone and unhappy about it, seems very distressed about friendships, or starts not wanting to go to daycare or school, talk to your child's educator or doctor. They can see the whole picture in a way an article can't.

## Questions parents ask

### How do I teach my preschooler about belonging?

Start with home: give your child a real place and a real job in the family, so they feel both connected and useful. Then practise the outward half: noticing who is alone, inviting others in, and saying "there's room here." Picture books like *The Snug* help by giving children a story to point back to.

### What should I say when my child is left out of play?

Listen first. Try "That sounds like it really hurt" before any advice. Later, when your child is calm, you can role-play ways to ask to join, and talk about a plan B, such as starting a game of their own or finding someone else who is on their own.

### My child excludes other children. What can I do?

It's very common at this age and usually not meanness. Ask how it might feel to be the one on the outside, and remind your child of a time they were left out. Practise phrases like "You can play too" and praise them specifically when they make room. If it is happening often at school, talk with the educator so you can use the same words.

### At what age do children understand being included and excluded?

The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that around age three, children begin playing with peers rather than just beside them and start forming particular friendships. That is when being included or left out starts to matter a lot. Their understanding of other people's feelings keeps growing through the preschool years.
