## Story in short
High on a mountain, melting snow becomes a tiny spring named Nura. She gives water to Willo, a young willow, and he gives her shade and birdsong. When she learns her water flows down to the whole valley, she fears she'll spread thin and vanish. One stormy night rocks block her stream, and she decides to keep all of herself. She swells into a proud pond, but grows still, murky and heavy; the frogs leave, the valley dries, the otters and bees struggle, and Willo droops. Nura is full but lonely. Grandmother Cloud reminds her she was a gift to begin with, and gifts are meant to keep moving. Nura spills over the rocks and rushes downhill, and Willo and the valley come back to life. Instead of shrinking she becomes a river, reaches the sea, rises as a cloud and returns as snow to begin again.

## Characters
- **Nura** (a mountain spring, she): small and sparkling, afraid of disappearing if she gives herself away; learns that sharing keeps her alive. The hero.
- **Willo** (young willow tree, he): Nura's friend; gives shade and birdsong; suffers when she holds back, revives when she flows again.
- **Grandmother Cloud**: wise elder who explains where Nura came from and that gifts should keep moving.
- **Valley creatures**: frogs (leave the stagnant pond), otters and otter kits (look for a swimming hole, later splash in), bees (find only dry flowers, later sip), field mice (fill buckets for their gardens), birds (leave Willo, return).

## The real animals
- Willow (genus *Salix*, species not named (check)): willows love water and grow along streams, rivers and wet ground in the Northern Hemisphere; their roots help hold riverbanks in place. Willow catkins are an early food for bees in spring (check).
- Otter (species not named; North American river otter, *Lontra canadensis*, or Eurasian otter, *Lutra lutra* (check)): otters live in and beside rivers, streams and lakes, need clean water and plenty of fish, and their young are called pups or kits.
- Frogs: need ponds and wet places to lay eggs; many leave or decline where water becomes polluted or dries up (keep claims general unless checked).
- Bees: need flowers, and flowers need water; a drought means fewer flowers.
- Field mice (species not named (check)): small rodents of fields and meadows. Carrying buckets and keeping gardens is a playful bend.
- The water cycle (the real "main character"): snow melts, soaks into the ground and feeds springs and streams; streams join to form rivers that flow to the sea; the sun evaporates water, which forms clouds, which fall again as rain or snow.

## Setting and environment
A mountain in spring as the snow melts, a spring and a young willow near the top, a rockfall after a storm, and below: a valley with a meadow, flowers, an orchard and a mill, otters' swimming holes, other small streams joining a river, and finally the sea and the sky. The story spans a full water cycle, ending in winter snow and a new spring.

## The value
For a child: when we share what we have, it doesn't run out; it keeps life flowing, for others and for us.
For a grown-up: the book takes on the fear behind not sharing (I'll be used up, I'll disappear) and answers it with the water cycle: what is given comes back in a new form. Holding on brings stillness and loneliness; giving brings connection. It also shows that what we have was given to us first.
What the book avoids: shaming the child for wanting to keep things; sharing as giving everything away until nothing is left. Nura becomes more, not less.

## Where the value comes from
Shared across traditions. Related sayings:
- **Ancient Greece & Rome**: "Give what you have." Original: Ἔχων χαρίζου. Source: Delphic maxims, Sosiades' list in Stobaeus, Anthology (Wachsmuth–Hense vol. 3, pp. 125–128), no. 45. care_flag: none.
- **Rabbinic teaching (Mishnah)**: "What is mine is mine and what is yours is yours" is the average way; "what is mine is yours, and what is yours is yours" is the way of the kind-hearted (chasid). Original: שֶׁלִּי שֶׁלְּךָ וְשֶׁלְּךָ שֶׁלָּךְ, חָסִיד. Source: Pirkei Avot (Mishnah Avot) 5:10, as numbered on Sefaria. care_flag: none. 2. Nura learns her water flows down to the whole valley and fears she'll disappear.
3. A storm piles rocks across her stream; she chooses to keep all her water.
4. She becomes a big, proud pond, but grows still, murky and heavy; the frogs leave.

## Refrain and theme line
- Refrain: none.
- Theme line: "Water that is held grows still. Water that is shared stays alive."

## Questions to ask
1. Why did Nura want to keep all her water?
2. What happened to the valley and to Willo when Nura stopped flowing?
3. Why do you think Nura felt empty even though she was so full?
4. What happened to Nura after she shared her water? Did she get smaller?
5. What is something you have that you could share? How might it come back to you?
