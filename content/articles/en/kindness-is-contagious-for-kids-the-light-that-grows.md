"Kindness is contagious" is one of those sayings we put on classroom posters and lunchbox notes. But how do you help a four-year-old actually feel it, especially on the days when they're tired, shy or worried about having enough? In this article we look at what it means for a young child that kindness spreads, what researchers have found about kindness passing from person to person (including among preschoolers), why being kind sometimes takes courage, and how our picture book *The Light That Grows*, about the smallest firefly in the meadow, turns all of this into a story children can hold on to. You'll also find simple things to say and do at home, and answers to the questions parents ask most.

## What kindness means for a three- to seven-year-old

For a young child, kindness is very concrete. It's waiting for a friend who runs slower. It's patting the dog gently. It's noticing that a baby brother dropped his spoon and handing it back. It's a hug when Grandpa looks sad.

What changes between 3 and 7 is how much a child can *see* around them. A three-year-old notices the person right in front of them, usually someone they love. By six or seven, many children can notice the child alone at the edge of the playground, guess what a new classmate might be feeling, and plan a kind act for later.

So when we talk about kindness at this age, we're really helping children grow three things:

- **Their eyes:** noticing who needs something.
- **Their heart:** caring about that person, even if they're not a best friend.
- **Their nerve:** being willing to do something about it, even when it feels a little scary.

That last one gets forgotten. We'll come back to it, because it's at the very centre of Lumi's story.

## Kindness is contagious: what the research says

The idea that kindness spreads isn't only a nice poster. Researchers have watched it happen.

In a 2010 study, James Fowler of the University of California, San Diego, and Nicholas Christakis of Harvard set up a cooperation game in which each player was matched with strangers and never met the same person twice. People who had been treated generously were more likely to be generous to the next person they played with, even though that person couldn't pay them back. The effect rippled out: one person's giving influenced the people they met, and then the people *those* people met, up to three steps away. The researchers compared the group to a "matching grant," where one gift ends up multiplied by everyone else's.

One honest detail matters, too: in the same study, unkind, stingy behaviour spread as well. Children copy what they see in both directions. That's not a reason to worry; it's a reason to notice how much the mood of a family or classroom is shaped, one small act at a time.

What about young children? A 2013 study in a Japanese nursery school watched five- and six-year-olds during free play. After a child helped or shared with a classmate, the *other* children who saw it were more likely to be kind to that helper in the minutes that followed, and to come closer, chat and touch their arm. The study was small, and the authors note they couldn't rule out that children were simply copying the helping they'd just seen. Either way, the picture is the same: in a room full of small children, one kind act changes what happens next.

And kindness seems to change the kind child, too. In a Canadian study, 9- to 11-year-olds in Vancouver schools were asked by their teachers to do acts of kindness over four weeks, such as sharing their lunch. Compared with a group asked to keep track of pleasant places they'd visited, the kind children were later chosen by more classmates as someone they wanted to work with. Those children were older than our readers, but the message travels well: kindness tends to bring people closer to you, not leave you with less.

## The courage inside kindness

Here's something parents often notice but rarely name: kind children aren't always the ones who *act*. Sometimes a child sees another child crying and freezes. Sometimes they want to share but are afraid of losing their turn, their toy or their place. Sometimes they're shy and the thought of walking up to someone new feels enormous.

That isn't a lack of kindness. It's a lack of courage, or a big feeling getting in the way, and both are very normal at this age. Being kind often means stepping out a little: speaking up, going first, offering something when you're not sure how it will be received.

This is why we think it helps to talk about kindness and bravery together. When your child hesitates, you can say, "It's okay to feel nervous. Kind things can feel a bit scary sometimes." That gives them a way to understand their own freeze without feeling like a "bad" kid, and it shows them that brave is something you can be with a shaky voice.

## How The Light That Grows shows kindness

*The Light That Grows* is set in a summer meadow at night. Lumi is the smallest firefly there, with the warmest glow. Her best friend Moss is a moth who can't glow at all, but he loves her light.

### A very real fear

One day the wise old glowworm of the meadow warns that a very dark night is coming, a night with no moon, when every light will be needed. Around the same time, some of the fireflies' lights begin to fade. Lumi becomes afraid that her own light will run out too. So she does something many children will recognise: she hides. She tucks herself into a hollow log and covers her glow.

The book is gentle here. Nobody scolds Lumi. Her fear is treated as real, which is exactly how a child's fear feels from the inside.

### What happens when kindness hides

While Lumi's light is covered, the meadow changes. Little neighbours stumble on the paths and lose their way in the dark. And Lumi's own hidden light feels smaller and colder, not safer. Children notice this without needing it explained: keeping something good to yourself doesn't always keep you warm.

### Brave enough to shine

On the darkest night, Moss goes looking for his friend and gets caught in brambles. Lumi hears him. Trembling, she uncovers her light and flies out to help. Then she shares her glow with another firefly whose light has gone out. What she discovers surprises her, and we'll leave the rest for you to read together, except to say that the book's theme line sums it up: "Some gifts grow bigger every time you give them away."

### A gentle science note

Real fireflies make their own light through a chemical reaction in their bodies, and they can't pass it to another firefly. Lumi's shared glow is story magic standing in for something true about people: when we share kindness, it spreads and it doesn't run out. If your child asks, it's a lovely chance to say, "Real fireflies can't do that, but people can do it with kindness."

## What the story is careful not to say

We wrote this book with two things in mind that matter to us as parents.

**Kindness isn't giving until you're empty.** Lumi doesn't wear herself out for everyone else. She shares, and she grows. For children, especially very caring ones, it helps to hear that being kind includes being kind to yourself: resting, asking for help, and saying "not right now" when you need to. A child who gives away every toy to keep the peace isn't learning kindness; they may be learning that their own needs don't count.

**Being afraid doesn't make you unkind.** Lumi hides because she is scared, not because she doesn't care. That distinction is a gift to anxious or shy children. They can be the hero of a kindness story too.

If you've read our article on the first book in the series, [Teaching kindness to preschoolers: the quiet kind that lasts](/parents/articles/teaching-kindness-to-preschoolers-secret-hero), you'll notice a difference. *Secret Hero* is about quiet, unseen helping. *The Light That Grows* is about what happens when kindness is shared out loud and starts to travel.

## Helping kindness spread at home

### Widen the circle

Richard Weissbourd, a psychologist who co-directs Harvard's Making Caring Common project, talks about a child's "circle of concern": the people they feel care for. For small children it starts with family and close friends. You can gently widen it. Point out the child playing alone at the park. Wonder aloud about the new kid at daycare. In the book, Lumi's light helps the mice, the snail and even a little firefly she barely knows, not just her best friend.

### Name the ripple

When kindness spreads in your home, say so. "When you helped your sister find her shoe, she helped you carry the bag. Did you notice?" Children often don't see the chain unless we point to it.

### Let them see your own chain

Tell your child when someone was kind to you and what you did next. "A man held the door for me today, and it made me want to let someone go ahead of me in line." This is the Fowler and Christakis finding in kitchen-table form.

### Practise the brave part

Role-play small brave-kind moments with toys or stuffed animals: asking someone to play, offering a turn, saying "Are you okay?" Rehearsing makes the real moment less scary.

### Keep a "light jar"

Put a clear jar on the counter. Each time someone in the family notices a kind act, add a button, a pom-pom or a paper star (choose bigger items if you have a child under three in the house). Watch the jar fill up together. It's a simple, visible picture of kindness growing, with no prizes needed.

### Make room for "not right now"

When your child doesn't want to share a special thing, you can respect that and still help them be kind in another way: "This is your special bear, so you don't have to share it. What could you share with Leo instead?"

## Share it with your child

Things to say or ask after reading *The Light That Grows*:

- **"Why do you think Lumi hid her light?"** Listen first. You might add, "She was scared it would run out. Have you ever felt like that?"
- **"What happened to the meadow when the lights went out?"** This helps your child see that what we hold back can matter to others.
- **"What helped Lumi be brave?"** Talk about how caring about Moss was bigger than her fear.
- **"What happened when she shared her light?"** Let them enjoy the surprise.
- **"What's a light you have?"** A smile, a joke, a game, help with buttons, a drawing. Every child has one.
- **Kindness can be catchy.** Try saying, "When you're kind, it's like passing on a little light. Let's see where it goes."
- **Keep the night part in the story.** Lumi and Moss fly out in the dark, but real children stay close to a grown-up at night. If your child wants to see fireflies, make it a family outing.

## Questions parents ask

### Is kindness really contagious?

Research suggests it can be. In a well-known 2010 study, people who were treated generously by strangers went on to be generous to others, and the effect reached people up to three steps away. In a nursery-school study, children who saw a classmate help were more likely to be kind to that helper soon after. Unkindness can spread too, which is why small daily choices in a family matter.

### How do I teach my child that kindness spreads?

Point it out when it happens ("You helped Mia, and then she helped Sam"), share your own stories of kindness passed on, and use visible reminders like a jar that fills with a button for each kind act you notice. Picture books like *The Light That Grows* give children a simple image to remember: a light that grows when it's shared.

### My child is kind but too shy to act. What can I do?

That's very common. Tell your child that kind things can feel scary, and that's okay. Practise small brave moments at home with toys, and start with low-pressure kindness, such as drawing a card, before face-to-face ones. If shyness or worry is getting in the way of daily life, talk to your child's doctor or educator.

### Should my child share everything to be kind?

No. Kindness doesn't mean giving until you're empty. It's fine for a child to keep a special toy, take a break or say "not right now." You can help them find another way to be kind instead, so they learn that their needs and other people's needs both count.

### What age is The Light That Grows for?

It's written for children about 3 to 7. Younger children enjoy the glowing fireflies and the friendship between Lumi and Moss; older children can talk about fear, courage and why sharing kindness doesn't use it up.
