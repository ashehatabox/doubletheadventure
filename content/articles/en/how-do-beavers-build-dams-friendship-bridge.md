How do beavers build dams? It's a question that comes up almost every time a child sees a beaver in a book, and our picture book *Friendship Bridge* is no exception. In the story, Venny the beaver wants a bridge of heavy, solid logs, and Sammy the squirrel wants one of light, quick branches. Those two ideas aren't random: they come straight from the way the real animals build. In this article we look at how real beavers build dams and lodges, how real squirrels weave their nests, what each animal eats and how it lives, where the story playfully bends nature, and simple ways to share it all with a 3 to 7 year old on your next walk.

## Meet the real Venny: the North American beaver

The book doesn't name Venny's species, so we picked the one North American children are most likely to meet: the **North American beaver** (*Castor canadensis*). Beavers live throughout Canada, as far north as the mouths of the Mackenzie and Coppermine rivers on the Arctic Ocean. The only other living beaver species is the Eurasian beaver (*Castor fiber*) of Europe and Asia.

A beaver is big: Canada's largest rodent and the second-largest rodent in the world, after the South American capybara. An adult can measure up to 1.3 metres from nose to tail and weigh about 16 to 35 kilograms. That's roughly the weight of a big dog, or of a child in the early school years.

A beaver is built for life in and around water:

- **Teeth that never stop growing.** A beaver's front teeth (its incisors) keep growing all its life. The upper and lower teeth grind against each other, which keeps their edges sharp. The front of each tooth is covered in a hard, dark orange enamel, so a beaver's smile really is orange.
- **A tail with many jobs.** The flat, leathery tail can be up to 30 centimetres long. In the water it works like a rudder for steering. On land it acts as a prop, so the beaver can sit or stand up on its back legs, and it helps the beaver balance while it carries mud, stones or branches in its front paws.
- **A warning signal.** When a beaver senses danger, it slaps its tail hard on the water. It sounds like a pistol shot, and it warns other beavers that danger is near.

That last fact is a lovely one to point out in the book. Venny taps his thick tail when he talks, which is our playful nod to the real animal's tail slap.

## How do beavers build dams?

A beaver dam is a wall across a stream that holds the water back and makes a pond. Hinterland Who's Who, the Canadian wildlife series, describes how it starts: the beaver chooses a narrow spot in the stream where the current is fastest. It lays sticks and rocks on the stream bed and pushes some sticks in so that their thick ends point upstream. Then it packs stones, roots and mud into the gaps, layer upon layer, until the wall holds.

Most dams are somewhere between 10 and 100 metres long, and some have been found as high as 5.5 metres. The longest known beaver dam, in Wood Buffalo National Park in Alberta and the Northwest Territories, is about 850 metres long.

### Why do beavers build dams at all?

Beavers don't build dams to cross a river. They build them to make deep, calm water around their home. The pond gives them water deep enough for underwater doorways into their home, and lets them reach their stored food under the ice in winter.

### The lodge: a beaver's house

Behind the dam, many beaver families build a **lodge**: a dome of branches and sticks woven together and plastered with mud and grass. Most lodges are about 5 metres across and 2 metres high. Inside is a dry room on a platform just above the water line, and at least two tunnels lead out underwater, so the family can come and go without stepping onto land. As freezing weather begins, beavers plaster the lodge with fresh mud, leaving a small air hole near the top. When the mud freezes, it becomes a shell as hard as concrete.

### What does this tell us about Venny?

Real beavers are builders of heavy, solid things. They work with logs, stones and mud, and their buildings last. So when Venny insists on heavy logs, he is thinking like a true beaver. In the book, though, his logs drift away in the current. A real beaver would have an answer for that: it anchors its sticks into the stream bed and packs them with mud and stones so the water can't carry them off. He had the right material, but not the thing that holds it all together.

## What do beavers eat?

Beavers are plant eaters; they don't eat fish. Their favourite foods are the bark, twigs, buds and leaves of trees such as trembling aspen, poplar, willow and birch. Hinterland Who's Who notes that about half a hectare of aspen can feed one beaver for a year.

In spring and summer, beavers switch to softer food: grasses, leaves, water plants and fruits. In winter, when the pond is frozen over, they live on a food store they've been building since summer. They pile branches in deep water close to the lodge, and all winter long they swim out under the ice, grab a stick and bring it inside to gnaw the bark. They also pull up the thick roots and stems of water plants such as pond lilies and cattails.

So would a real beaver want those strawberries? Quite possibly: summer fruit is on the menu.

## Beaver families and babies

A beaver colony is a family: two parents, last year's young (yearlings) and the new babies, called **kits**. Beavers usually stay with the same mate for life, and family members spend time grooming one another.

Kits are born in late spring, in May or June, usually three or four at a time. A newborn kit weighs about 450 grams, about as much as a loaf of bread. Unlike many baby animals, beaver kits are born with fur, already have teeth, can see, and can swim. Young beavers live with their parents until they are about two years old, and beavers can live up to 12 years in the wild.

### A Canadian symbol with a comeback story

The beaver is one of Canada's official national symbols, and it has stood for Canada since at least the 1600s, long before the maple leaf. A beaver sitting on a log has been on the five-cent coin almost without a break since 1937, so a nickel is a perfect thing to show your child after reading.

During the fur trade, so many beavers were trapped for hats that they became scarce across North America. Conservation work helped them recover, and today their population is healthy. Scientists call beavers a **keystone species**, because their dams and ponds open up the woods and create homes for many other plants and animals.

## Meet the real Sammy: the eastern grey squirrel

Sammy's species isn't named in the book either. We like to think of him as an **eastern grey squirrel** (*Sciurus carolinensis*), the squirrel children see most often in parks and backyards across eastern North America, including southern Canada. (Our article on [the animals behind Secret Hero](/parents/articles/are-rabbits-nocturnal-secret-hero) looks at its smaller cousin, the red squirrel.)

An eastern grey squirrel measures about 38 to 52 centimetres from nose to tail tip, and that bushy tail makes up around 15 to 25 centimetres of it. Here's a fact children love: not every grey squirrel is grey. Black squirrels are the same species, just with a darker coat, and they are more common in the north. Researchers have found that black squirrels lose about 18 percent less heat than grey ones when it's colder than minus 10 degrees Celsius. A dark coat is a warm winter coat.

### What do squirrels eat?

Grey squirrels mostly eat nuts, seeds, buds and flowers from trees: acorns from oaks, hickory nuts, walnuts, beechnuts and maple seeds, along with fruits such as mulberries, wild cherries and hawthorn berries, and some fungi. In summer they also eat insects, and occasionally bird eggs.

In autumn, they get ready for winter by **scatter hoarding**: burying food in many small hiding places instead of one big pile. They find their stores again later using both memory and smell. The ones they don't dig up can sprout, which means squirrels help plant new trees.

## How do squirrels build their nests?

This is where Sammy's idea comes from. Tree squirrels build round nests called **dreys**, high up in the fork of a tree. Grey squirrels often live in a hollow in a tree trunk too, but tree holes can be hard to find, so a drey is the usual answer.

The Royal Botanical Gardens in Hamilton, Ontario, describes how they're made. The squirrel weaves twigs and branches together into a ball and covers it with leaves to keep the rain out. The door faces the tree trunk. Grey squirrels build the outside from bark and twigs, then line the inside with soft things: moss, dried grass, thistle seeds and even feathers. A grey squirrel's drey is about 30 to 60 centimetres across, roughly the size of a beach ball, and the inside of a well-built drey can be 20 to 30 degrees warmer than the air outside.

### What does this tell us about Sammy?

Real squirrels are weavers. They take light, bendy materials and twist them together. So when Sammy wants light branches for a quick bridge, he's thinking like a true squirrel. In the book, his branches wobble and snap on their own. That makes sense too: a drey works because it's woven into the strong fork of a tree, not stretched across open space.

Put the two animals side by side and you have the heart of the story. The beaver's way is heavy and solid. The squirrel's way is light and woven. Each one works best with something the other has. That's exactly what Venny and Sammy discover, and it's why the theme line of the book is "Together, we can do more." ## Where the story playfully bends nature

Picture books stretch the truth a little, and it helps children when we point out where:

- **Beavers and squirrels don't team up.** In the wild, they live side by side in the same forests but don't build together. The friendship is the story's invention.
- **Beavers build dams, not bridges.** A real beaver would block the river to make a pond, not cross it.
- **Strawberries don't grow on tall bushes.** Wild strawberries are low plants that hug the ground, usually no taller than about 15 centimetres, spreading by runners. The fruit is about a centimetre across and ripens in early summer. Lots of animals enjoy them, from birds and turtles to rabbits.
- **Watch out for the "strawberry bush".** There really is a shrub called strawberry bush (*Euonymus americanus*), native to the southeastern United States, with bumpy red seed pods in the fall. It is not a strawberry, and its fruit, leaves and stems are mildly poisonous if eaten. It's a good reason for the family rule: we never eat wild berries unless a grown-up who knows plants says they're safe.

## Share it with your child

- **Ask the "why" question.** After reading, ask: "Why do you think Venny liked logs and Sammy liked branches?" Then share the real answer: beavers build with heavy logs and mud, squirrels weave light twigs.
- **Look for dreys.** Late fall and winter are the best time, because the leaves have fallen and the nests are easy to spot as dark, leafy balls high in the trees.
- **Visit beaver country safely.** Some parks and conservation areas have beaver ponds with trails or boardwalks. Look for a lodge (a big dome of sticks in the water) or branches with the bark chewed off. Stay on the path and well back from the water's edge, and never stand on a dam or a lodge.
- **Try the two-material test.** At home, build two tiny table-top "bridges" between two books: one of a single heavy block, one of a few pencils or craft sticks. Then try tying them together with string. What changes? Keep it on the table, with a grown-up, and never try it over real water.

### A few safety rules for animal walks

Wild animals are best enjoyed from a distance. Don't feed squirrels or beavers, don't try to touch them, and keep dogs on a leash near ponds. Ice on ponds can be thin even when it looks solid, so keep children off pond ice unless it's a supervised, tested rink.

## Questions parents ask

### How do beavers build dams?

A beaver picks a narrow spot in a stream, lays sticks and rocks on the bottom with the thick ends of some sticks pointing upstream, then packs stones, roots and mud into the gaps, layer by layer. The dam holds back water to make a deep pond around the family's lodge.

### Do beavers eat fish?

No. Beavers eat only plants: bark, twigs, buds and leaves, plus grasses, water plants and fruit in summer. In winter they eat from a store of branches kept underwater near the lodge.

### Why are a beaver's teeth orange?

The front of a beaver's teeth is covered in hard, dark orange enamel. The teeth keep growing all their life, and grinding top against bottom keeps them sharp.

### What is a squirrel's nest called?

A drey: a ball of woven twigs and leaves high in a tree, lined with moss, grass and feathers. Squirrels also use hollows in tree trunks.

### Is the squirrel in Friendship Bridge a real kind of squirrel?

The book doesn't name his species. We think of him as an eastern grey squirrel, and his love of light, woven branches matches how real tree squirrels build. You can find the book and its characters on the [Friendship Bridge page](/books/friendship-bridge).
