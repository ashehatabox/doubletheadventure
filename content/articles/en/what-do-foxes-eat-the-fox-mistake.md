What do foxes eat? It's often the first question a child asks after meeting Felix and Finn, the two best-friend foxes in our picture book *The Fox Mistake*. The answer is more surprising than "chickens," and it leads to other good questions: where foxes sleep, whether they really keep treasures in their dens, and why a fox jumps straight up in the air before it lands on its dinner. This article gives you the real facts about the red fox, points out where the story playfully bends nature, and ends with simple things to share with a 3 to 7 year old, including what to do if your family meets a fox in the park.

## Which fox are Felix and Finn?

The book never names the foxes' species. They look like **red foxes** (*Vulpes vulpes*), and that's the fox most children in Canada, the United States, Europe and much of Asia are likely to see, so that's the one we'll follow here.

The red fox is a small, dog-like animal with a pointed face, tall pointed ears, a light, nimble body, long glossy fur and a big bushy tail. According to Hinterland Who's Who, the wildlife series from the Canadian Wildlife Federation and Environment and Climate Change Canada, an adult weighs about 3.6 to 6.8 kilograms (roughly the weight of a large house cat) and measures 90 to 112 centimetres from nose to tail tip. About a third of that length is tail.

Here's a fact that delights children: **not every red fox is red**. Most are some shade of orange-red, often with black "socks" on their legs, black backs to the ears and a white tip on the tail. But some are darker, with a black stripe down the back and another across the shoulders, which makes a cross; these are called **cross foxes**. Others are nearly black with silvery tips to their fur, and are called **silver foxes**. They are all the same species, and Hinterland Who's Who notes that two different colours can even turn up in the same litter.

Foxes belong to the dog family, and the University of Michigan's Animal Diversity Web says the red fox has the widest range of any member of that family: from the Arctic down to Central America, across Europe and Asia, and into northern Africa. It lives in forests, fields, mountains, prairies, deserts, farmland and cities. It likes "edges" best: the places where woods meet open ground, which is exactly the kind of place the story's foxes call home.

## What do foxes eat? Almost everything, but mostly mice

Now for the big question. Red foxes are **omnivores**, which means they eat both animals and plants. Animal Diversity Web lists their main foods as rodents, rabbits, insects and fruit, and says they will also eat dead animals they find. Hinterland Who's Who agrees that small mammals are probably their number one food: voles, mice, lemmings, squirrels, hares and rabbits.

What makes foxes so interesting is that their menu changes with the seasons:

- **Autumn and winter:** mostly small mammals, especially mice and voles.
- **Spring:** they add eggs and young birds, especially from ducks and geese nesting on the ground.
- **Summer:** lots of insects, such as grasshoppers and beetles, plus berries and other fruit.

They are also clever at using whatever comes along. Hinterland Who's Who describes red foxes catching fish by leaping from the shore into shallow water, and eating fruit of all kinds, reptiles and, near people, garbage and pet food.

### Do foxes really steal chickens?

They can, if a henhouse is easy to get into, and that's where their old "sly chicken thief" reputation comes from. But Hinterland Who's Who points out that on farms, foxes more than make up for it by eating huge numbers of the mice and insects that damage crops, and that many farmers now see them as helpers. That's a nice little lesson in itself: a reputation isn't always the whole story. Which, as it happens, is what *The Fox Mistake* is about.

### Why does a fox jump straight up?

This is the fact children remember. When a red fox hunts a mouse in tall grass or snow, it stands perfectly still and listens. Its hearing is so sharp it can hear a mouse moving where it can't see it. Then it leaps high and comes down front paws first, pinning the mouse. This is called **mousing**.

There's an even stranger idea about it. Scientists in the Czech Republic watched nearly 600 mousing jumps by 84 wild foxes and found that the foxes mostly jumped towards the north-east, and that those jumps worked far more often (about 73 percent of the time) than jumps in most other directions (18 percent). They suggested foxes might use the Earth's magnetic field to help aim, a little like a built-in compass. National Geographic's report on the study is careful to say this is still an unproven idea: nobody knows how a fox could sense magnetism. For a child, it's a perfect "scientists are still finding out" fact.

## Do foxes hide treasure in their dens?

This is where real foxes and the story meet in a lovely way. In the book, Felix finds a shiny pebble by the river and carries it home to keep safe in his den.

Real foxes don't collect pebbles. But they do something close to it: they **hide food to eat later**. When a fox catches more than it can eat, it digs a small hole, drops the food in, and covers it up. This is called **caching** (say "cashing"), and the hiding spot is a cache. Animal Diversity Web notes that red foxes are very good at finding their caches again. Hinterland Who's Who adds a detail that fits the story rather well: other animals often find a fox's hidden food and eat it first.

So in real life, when a fox's treasure goes missing, the cause is usually a hungry neighbour, the weather or just bad luck. In the story, as you'll see when you read it, the cause is even simpler, and nobody took anything at all. That makes it a good bridge to talk about how things sometimes disappear without anyone being to blame.

### Where do foxes sleep?

Red foxes mainly use dens for **raising their babies**. Hinterland Who's Who explains that from autumn until about March, foxes usually sleep outside in thickets and heavy bush, even in the coldest weather, curled up with that bushy tail wrapped over their nose and feet like a scarf.

When it's time for babies, a fox family often moves into an old woodchuck burrow or another animal's hole, or digs its own. Good spots are small hills in fields, stream banks, hedgerows and forest edges, often in dry, sandy soil, often facing south, with a clear view from the front door. Many dens have more than one entrance for a quick escape, and an undisturbed den can be used for years.

So Felix and Finn living in dens side by side in autumn is a story choice. In real life, two dens close together would more likely belong to one family than to two neighbours.

## Fox families and fox kits

Baby foxes are called **kits**, **pups** or **cubs**. They are born in spring, usually from March to May, in litters of about five (anywhere from one to ten or more). They're born blind, open their eyes in their second week, and start tumbling around the den entrance at about a month old.

Both parents help. At first the mother stays close to the den while the father brings food. Later, both hunt and bring back food for the kits, and the young foxes practise stalking mice in the long grass while the grown-ups watch.

Then comes autumn, the season of our story. That's when young foxes leave home to find their own territory. Hinterland Who's Who says young males have been tracked travelling as far as 250 kilometres from where they were born.

### Are foxes friendly with each other?

Mostly, adult red foxes live and hunt **alone**. They don't form packs like wolves. A home area usually belongs to one adult male and one or two females with their young, and foxes mark their area with scent. So two grown-up best-friend foxes racing through the woods together and sharing their fruit is the story's sweetest invention.

What's not invented is how much foxes "talk." Animal Diversity Web reports that scientists have described 28 different fox sounds, from barks and yelps to screams, and that each fox has its own recognisable voice. Felix and Finn would have had plenty of ways to tell each other how they felt, which is, after all, exactly what they have to learn to do.

## Story or real? How the book bends the fox

Here's a quick guide you can use when your child asks, "Is that what real foxes do?"

- **Real:** foxes live in forests with rivers and oak trees, are busy at dusk and at night, dig dens, and hide things to keep for later.
- **Real:** foxes eat fruit. Sharing fruit isn't something wild foxes do, but fruit really is on the menu in late summer and autumn.
- **Story bend:** wild adult foxes are mostly loners, so best-friend foxes are an invention.
- **Story bend:** foxes cache food, not pebbles.
- **Story bend:** real foxes don't argue, accuse each other or say sorry. The book borrows foxes to talk about something only people (and storybook animals) do: getting it wrong with a friend and making it right. You'll find more on that side of the book in our article on [teaching forgiveness to preschoolers](/parents/articles/teaching-forgiveness-to-preschoolers-the-fox-mistake).

Hinterland Who's Who makes a point worth sharing with older children: stories have long called foxes bold, cunning and sneaky, but real red foxes are **shy, secretive and nervous**, and very intelligent. Felix isn't sneaky either. He jumps to the wrong conclusion, which is a very different thing.

## Share it with your child

Here are some easy ways to bring these fox facts into your reading time.

- **Play "mousing."** Lay a cushion or a scarf on the floor as the "mouse." Your child crouches, stays perfectly still, cups a hand behind each ear to listen, then jumps up and lands with both hands on the cushion. Take turns being the fox and the quiet little mouse. Play it on a soft floor or grass with plenty of space.
- **Try fox ears.** With eyes closed, can your child point to where you crinkle a bit of paper? Try again with hands cupped behind their ears, like a fox's tall ears.
- **Make a pretend cache.** Hide a small toy in the garden or under a cushion, cover it, and see if your child can find it again tomorrow. Talk about how real foxes do this with food, and how sometimes another animal finds it first. (For children under 3, use a toy that's too big to swallow.)
- **Ask "story or real?"** After reading, ask: "Do real foxes have best friends? Do they keep pebbles? Do they eat fruit?" Children love catching the made-up parts, and it builds early thinking skills.
- **Talk about missing things.** "Felix's pebble went missing. When something of ours goes missing, what could have happened to it?" It's a gentle, everyday way to practise thinking of more than one explanation before blaming someone.

### If your family sees a fox

Foxes live in many towns and cities, so a sighting is quite possible. The City of Toronto notes that foxes are most active at dawn and dusk but can sometimes be seen sunning themselves in the daytime, and that young foxes are often out during the day. A simple rule for children: **watch from a distance, stay quiet, and never feed or try to touch a fox.** Feeding wild animals teaches them to come close to people, which isn't safe for them or for us. Keep pets on a leash or indoors, and don't leave pet food or garbage outside.

Hinterland Who's Who also gives one important safety warning: children should stay away from any fox that seems bold or "friendly," because healthy red foxes are shy. If a fox seems sick or acts strangely, keep away and let a grown-up call your local animal services. And if anyone is ever bitten or scratched by a wild animal, wash the wound and see a doctor right away.

If you're near a river, as Felix is when he spots his pebble, the same goes for water: children look from the bank, and only wade or reach into a river with a grown-up right beside them.

## Questions parents ask

### What do foxes eat in the wild?

Red foxes are omnivores. They mostly eat mice, voles, rabbits and other small animals, and add insects, eggs, birds, fish, berries and other fruit depending on the season. Near people they'll also eat garbage and pet food left outside.

### Do foxes live in dens all year?

No. Red foxes mainly use dens to raise their kits in spring and early summer. From autumn to early spring, they usually sleep outside in thick bushes, curled up with their bushy tail over their nose to stay warm.

### Are foxes nocturnal?

Mostly. Red foxes hunt mainly around sunset, at night and in the early morning. In cities they're often seen at dawn and dusk, and young foxes are frequently out playing in daylight.

### Is it safe for my child to get close to a fox?

No. Watch quietly from a distance and never feed or touch a fox. Healthy foxes are shy and will usually move away. A fox that seems bold, sick or strangely friendly should be avoided; a grown-up can call local animal services.

### Do foxes really hide food?

Yes. When a red fox catches more than it can eat, it buries the extra in a small hole to eat later. This is called caching. Foxes are good at finding their caches again, though other animals sometimes find them first.
