If you're wondering how to go about teaching kindness to preschoolers, here is some good news: you're not starting from zero. Children as young as one and a half already try to help, comfort and share. Your job is less about installing kindness and more about protecting it, giving it room and showing what it looks like. This article explains what kindness means at ages 3 to 7, why the quiet, no-applause kind matters so much, and how a small rabbit in our picture book *Secret Hero* can open the conversation at home or in class.

## What kindness means to a three- to seven-year-old

To a small child, kindness isn't an abstract virtue. It's a set of very concrete moments: handing a friend the red crayon, waiting while a younger cousin finishes a sentence, bringing a blanket to a parent who looks tired, letting someone else go first on the slide.

At this age children are learning three skills that make kindness possible:

- **Noticing.** Seeing that someone is sad, stuck or left out. This grows with language for feelings ("She looks worried").
- **Imagining.** Guessing what would help. Young children often offer what *they* would want, like their own teddy bear to a crying adult, and that's a lovely start.
- **Acting.** Actually doing something, even something small, without being told.

Kindness at 3 looks clumsy and generous. Kindness at 6 can be planned and a little more private. Both count.

## Children are born helpers (and rewards can get in the way)

Researchers have watched very young children help for years, and the findings are encouraging. In experiments by the psychologists Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello, toddlers readily helped an adult who couldn't reach something, without being asked. In one study, they tested what happens when you reward that helping. Toddlers who received a small toy each time they helped later helped *less* than toddlers who got only a word of praise or nothing at all. Psychologists call this the overjustification effect: when a reward becomes the reason, the inner reason can fade.

A second study, from the University of British Columbia, looked at how giving feels. Toddlers under two were happier when they gave a treat to a puppet than when they received one themselves, and happiest of all when they gave away a treat of their *own*. Giving, it seems, feels good from very early on.

Put those two findings together and you get a simple idea for parents: children already have a kind engine inside. Big prizes for being kind can swap that engine for a sticker chart. Warmth, attention and a quiet "That made her smile" tend to keep it running.

## The story: a rabbit who helps in secret

*Secret Hero* is the first book in our *Double the Adventure* series, and it is built entirely around this idea.

Bunny is a small, shy rabbit in a peaceful forest who loves to help but has no wish to be thanked. When a dry spell empties the watering hole, he slips out at night and mends the broken pipe that feeds it. When the wind knocks down a mother bird's nest and an egg goes missing, he searches in the dark and carries it home. When winter snow buries the paths to the animals' food, he clears them while everyone sleeps. Each morning the forest wakes up, finds the problem solved, and wonders who did it.

Eventually a squirrel spots Bunny and asks why he keeps it secret. Bunny's answer is the heart of the book: "It's not about being noticed. It's about making someone's day better."

What happens next is what we love most. Talk of a mysterious "Secret Hero" spreads, and other animals start helping too, each in their own way. Nobody gives Bunny a medal. The reward is a forest full of small, quiet kindnesses.

## Why "secret" kindness is worth talking about

We want to be careful here: there is nothing wrong with being thanked, and nothing wrong with a child who beams when you notice their good deed. Being seen is a real need. Bunny's quietness is his choice, not a rule for every child.

But the story gives you a gentle way to separate two questions children often mix up:

1. *Did I do something kind?*
2. *Did someone see me do it?*

When kindness depends on the second question, it gets fragile. Children start helping mainly when adults are watching, or they feel cheated when nobody says thank you. When kindness rests on the first question ("I made someone's day better"), it travels with them into the playground, the bus and, one day, the workplace.

The other quiet lesson in the book is about size. Bunny doesn't do anything heroic in the movie sense. He fixes, finds, clears. The monkey clears water paths; the birds simply sing. Small acts count, and they spread.

## How to practise kindness at home

Here are habits that work with real three- to seven-year-olds, drawn from family-tested routines and from Harvard's Making Caring Common project, which researches how to raise caring children.

### Make kindness a family priority, out loud

Children notice what we celebrate. If the first question after school is always "How did you do on…?", they learn that achievement comes first. Try sometimes asking, "Did you see anyone who needed help today?" or "Was anyone kind to you?"

### Describe the effect, not the child

Instead of "You're such a good girl," try "Look, Sam stopped crying when you gave him the bucket." Describing the effect keeps the focus on the other person, which is exactly where Bunny's attention is.

### Skip the prizes for kindness

Save stickers for things like brushing teeth if you use them at all. For kind acts, a warm look, a hug or a short sentence is enough. The research above suggests that's not just "nice"; it may protect the child's own motivation.

### Give them real jobs

Small children love responsibility. Let them feed the cat, water a plant, set out cups for guests, or carry the lightest grocery bag. Helping becomes part of who they are, not a special performance.

### Plan a "secret kindness" together

This is the most direct way to bring the book into your week. Choose a small, safe secret good deed with your child: a drawing slipped under a sibling's pillow, a "thank you" note for the mail carrier, a neatly lined-up row of a sibling's shoes. Do it together, in daylight, and don't tell. Later, ask how it felt.

### Model it, and admit your slips

Let your child see you hold a door, help a neighbour or check on a friend. And when you're short-tempered, say so and make it right. Owning mistakes is part of kindness too.

## Share it with your child

A few things you can say or do right after reading:

- **Wonder together.** "How do you think the animals felt when they woke up and the water was back?"
- **Ask about the secret.** "Why do you think Bunny didn't want anyone to know?" There is no wrong answer; listen.
- **Notice the ripple.** "What happened after the forest heard about the Secret Hero?" Help your child see that kindness spreads.
- **Make it personal.** "What small thing could you do tomorrow to make someone's day better?" Write their idea on a sticky note and put it by the door.
- **Keep it safe.** Bunny's night-time adventures are story magic. Real helpers go with a grown-up and stay inside or close by, and if you ever find a bird's egg or a fallen nest, the right thing to do is to tell a grown-up rather than touch it.

## In the classroom

Educators can turn the book into a week-long "secret helpers" project:

- **Read and pause.** Stop before each morning scene and ask the group to guess who solved the problem and how the animals feel.
- **A secret helpers' box.** Children draw or dictate a small kind act they did, without names, and drop it in a box. At the end of the week, read them aloud and let the class enjoy how many there are.
- **Kindness ripples.** Drop a pebble in a bowl of water and watch the rings spread. Talk about how one kind act inspired the monkey and the birds.

These activities link naturally to the social and emotional learning goals in most early-years programs, including the "belonging and contributing" area of Ontario's Kindergarten Program.

## Questions parents ask

### At what age do children start being kind?

Very early. Research shows that many children under two will spontaneously help an adult who is struggling, and toddlers in one study were happier giving treats than receiving them. Kindness grows with language and self-control, so it looks different at 3 and at 7, but the seed is there from the start.

### Should I reward my child for being kind?

Praise and warmth are wonderful. Material rewards for helping, such as toys or treats, can backfire: in a study of 20-month-olds, children rewarded with a toy for helping later helped less than those who weren't. Describing the effect ("You made him smile") is usually better than a prize.

### My child only helps when I'm watching. Is that normal?

Yes, it's very common, and it's part of learning. Children are still working out why kindness matters. Talking about how others feel, and trying a secret kindness together, helps move the reason from "a grown-up is watching" to "I like making someone's day better."

### Is Secret Hero right for a three-year-old?

Yes. The problems in the story are easy to follow, each one is solved by the next morning, and the message is simple. Older children (5 to 7) enjoy the mystery of the Secret Hero and the idea that kindness can spread.

### What if my child is shy, like Bunny?

Shy children are often deeply kind; they just prefer quiet ways to show it. Bunny is a good role model for them: you don't need to be loud or seen to make a difference.
