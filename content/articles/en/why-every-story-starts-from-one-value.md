Most picture books start with a character or a funny situation. Ours start somewhere else: with one value that people have carried in their stories for a very long time. Belonging. Getting up again. Looking after the place you love. Learning from the people who came before you.

This article explains why we work that way, what we borrow from the world's wisdom traditions, and what we are careful never to do.

## One book, one value

A child of three to seven can hold one big idea at a time. So each book carries exactly one, and every page serves it.

In *The Snug*, that idea is belonging. Quilla the porcupine is new in Bramble Hollow, and when she asks to squeeze in under the Big Oak, everyone scoots away. The book doesn't end with someone finally letting her in. Instead, with her friend Bodie the badger, she builds a warm little hut of her own, and when the first snow falls she decides who it is for: everyone.

That ending is the value. *If there's no room for you, build a place with room for everyone.* A child doesn't need the word "inclusion" to understand it. They feel it when the door swings open.

## Where the values come from

Since Book 14, each story starts from a value that one culture's traditional wisdom has put into words, and the note at the front of the book tells grown-ups where it comes from:

- **Ubuntu** from Southern Africa: *a person is a person through other people*. It shapes *The View From Down Low*, where a tall giraffe bends all the way down to see the world from his meerkat friend's height.
- **Nana korobi ya oki** from Japan: *fall seven times, get up eight*. It shapes *Eight Times Up*, where a little mountain goat keeps tumbling on her way to Cloud Rock.
- **Kaitiakitanga** from Aotearoa New Zealand: guardianship of the land and the sea, in *The Littlest Guardian*.
- **Sankofa** from Ghana: the bird that walks forward while looking back, in *The Old Path Song*.

We keep a library of more than six hundred of these values from two dozen traditions, each with its source. Before a value goes into a book, we check where the saying really comes from. We don't use quotes that only exist on posters.

You can see every value and where it comes from on our [values map](/values-map).

## Why the heroes are always animals

The stories are about little animals, never about people from a particular country. That's on purpose.

When a child meets a porcupine or a penguin, they don't ask whether the hero looks like them. They just step inside the story. A child in Toronto, Lyon or Accra can be Quilla for ten minutes. The culture lives in the value and in the note for grown-ups, not in a costume.

It also keeps us honest. We are not telling another community's sacred stories. We're borrowing one idea it has shared openly, and saying clearly where it came from.

## The child is the hero

In every book, the young character is the one who changes things. Not a wise grown-up who arrives at the end with the answer, and not luck.

This matters more than it seems. A three-year-old's day is full of moments where they can't do the thing yet: the zipper, the stairs, the friend who won't share. A story where someone small solves the problem with their own idea says, quietly, *you can too*.

## What we will never say

Some stories teach values in a way we think is harmful. The most common one is *you'll belong once you're useful*: the outsider is accepted only after saving the day. We never write that. In our books, a child belongs because they are there, not because they earned it.

We also avoid lessons that shame. When a character gets something wrong, the story is about putting it right, not about punishment.

## Readers from each culture check the book

Before a book built on a culture's value is published, readers from that culture read it. They tell us if a detail is wrong, if a saying is off, or if something should simply be left out. Some values are marked in our library as needing a cultural reader before they are ever used, and some we leave alone completely.

## Made for reading together

Every book has a short refrain that children can finish for you, like *Scoop, loop, snug!* or *Wobble, wobble... POP!* It's an invitation to join in. Reading researchers call this kind of back-and-forth *dialogic reading*: the grown-up asks, the child tells, and the story becomes theirs.

That's why our [parent corner](/parents) gives you a few questions for each book. After *The Snug*, try: *"Have you ever felt left out? What did you do?"* You may be surprised how much a four-year-old has to say.

## In short

- One book, one value, so a small child can hold it.
- Values from the world's wisdom traditions, with honest sources.
- Animal heroes any child can step into.
- The child solves the problem.
- No "belong once you're useful", no shaming.
- Cultural readers before publishing.

That's the whole idea behind *Double the Adventure*: a story to enjoy, and a value to carry with you.
